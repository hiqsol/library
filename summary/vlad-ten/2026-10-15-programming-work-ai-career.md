# When Programming Stops Feeling Like a Craft: Vlad Ten on AI, Work, and Agency

Vlad Ten and the hosts of *Мы обречены* ask what happens if software jobs survive while the part
programmers enjoy is delegated to AI. Their concern is craft and identity, not a demonstrated
forecast of mass unemployment [00:07:56–00:11:50].
Agentic coding can feel like standing in a queue: wait for output, inspect unfamiliar code, and
restart while retaining responsibility [00:55:34–00:58:09]. Faster generation can increase
cognitive load because code appears faster than a human can build a mental model of it.

The speakers argue that corporate adoption may matter more than perfect model quality. Generic
application development is most exposed; proprietary systems and scarce-domain work still reward
deep expertise [00:28:20–00:31:11; 01:07:43–01:08:34]. System design, product judgment, and
verification remain valuable.
Immediate answers also change learning. Stack Overflow searches once produced accidental
knowledge; an assistant can explain failures, but a user can skip understanding and request a fix
[01:04:50–01:07:43]. The claim that Stack Overflow “closed” is exaggeration, not fact.

DHH is treated as a symbolic convert because he championed enjoyable code. Lex Fridman's episode
501 transcript supports that he embraced AI agents in late 2025 and built Omarchy, though claims
that he abandoned all manual coding go beyond the evidence [01:08:34–01:11:41].

“Open source is dead” is also a provocation. Cheap AI-generated pull requests can increase review
cost and encourage trust-gated contribution, but reported burdens are anecdotal and uneven
[01:14:22–01:16:16]. They do not establish the death of open source.

The U.S. Copyright Office summary of *Bartz v. Anthropic* confirms that Anthropic bought and
scanned physical books and used pirated copies. The ruling was mixed: training and the
purchased-book digital library were fair use, while retaining the pirated library was not
[01:16:16–01:18:40].

Two commercial sections require disclosure. GigaCode and GitVerse sponsor 00:14:13–00:21:51;
claims of 100-fold productivity and a one-evening build are untested promotion. An Avito Tech
partner segment at 00:44:42–00:54:01 discusses agents as natural-language interfaces but provides
no adoption or unit-economics data.

The companion-app revenue claim of $450 million for the first half of 2026 is unverified
[01:24:45–01:25:03]. The reported $500,000–$600,000 salaries for data-center trades should be
read as exceptional anecdotes or exaggeration, not representative wages [01:20:31–01:20:44].

Warhammer miniature painting supplies the counterexample: outsourcing the painting defeats the
hobby's purpose [01:27:49–01:31:09]. The value lies in practicing, improving, and making the
object, not merely possessing an optimized result.

Vlad later describes serial reinvention as a way to remain “promising” without facing final
failure [01:40:16–01:43:25]. A debate about “NPCs” ends unresolved: private creation can express
agency, but treating output as human worth repeats the productivity pressure under criticism.

Phil's closing arrow story captures the episode. A hand-carved arrow mattered because difficulty
created skill and attachment. Once perfect blanks made good arrows effortless, he stopped playing
[01:57:46–01:58:54]. The unresolved question is which work should be optimized for output and
which should remain human because doing it is itself the value.
