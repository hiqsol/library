# Five Everyday Habits That Support a Child's Brain Development

## The five practices

1. **Respond back.** Kuznetsova calls this "serve and return": answer a baby's coo,
name the dog a child points to, or thank them and return a toy they hand you.
She says the exchange supports turn-taking, communication, and secure attachment.
The claim that each exchange creates a billion neural links is not substantiated.

2. **Share a picture book daily.** From birth to age five, name pictures, copy sounds,
let toddlers turn pages, and invite preschoolers to predict or retell events.
Kuznetsova links reading with attention, language, later reading, and symbolic thought.
She gives conflicting word-exposure figures—78,000 total and 78,000 per year.

3. **Keep a recognizable sleep rhythm.** Her example is bath, book, lullaby, then bed,
but she says families should use what works, even when evenings are chaotic.
She links sleep with memory consolidation, neural pruning, cortisol regulation,
attention, behavior, and emotional stability; no specific supporting study is cited.

4. **Pause before solving safe problems.** Let a child notice a rolled-away ball,
a dirty dropped banana, a torn dress, a broken car, or a shape-sorter mismatch.
Wait, celebrate success, and help when asked; do not withhold comfort or safety.
Kuznetsova links manageable frustration with regulation and executive functions.

5. **Offer real household work.** A two-year-old might pass laundry socks,
a three-year-old water plants, and a four-year-old sort containers by size.
She links chores with sequencing, motor skills, responsibility, and self-efficacy.
Expect slower work and spills; adapt the task, supervise, and avoid hazards or shame.

## Limits and age-sensitive use

These ages are examples, not milestones. Follow the child's signals and abilities;
use gestures or communication devices, brief books, prompts, and co-regulation as needed.
A routine is an opportunity, not a test, and illness or family demands may disrupt it.

The video also advises responsive feeding from complementary feeding in the first year:
respect hunger and fullness, avoid force or cartoons, and present food safely.
That aside promotes Kuznetsova's feeding guide; safety details need current guidance.

The source does not prove that these habits literally accelerate brain development.
It names no studies, methods, controls, effect sizes, or measured clinical outcomes.
Its opening million-links-per-second claim and later billion-per-exchange claim are unsourced.
