# AI Is Removing the Practice That Builds Professional Judgment
Dylan Davis argues that AI is automating the routine work through which junior knowledge workers
historically developed professional judgment.

Document searches, first drafts, numerical analysis, feedback, and repeated correction were not
only production tasks. They were an informal apprenticeship that taught workers what mattered,
what good work looked like, and which errors recurred.

The video's labor-market evidence is directionally relevant but presented too strongly:
- Revelio Labs found U.S. graduate entry-level postings more than 35% below January 2023, or over
  100,000 fewer new postings per month. This measures demand, not existing jobs, and AI does not
  explain the whole decline.
- Banks were reported to be cutting some analyst classes **by as much as** two-thirds. The video
  drops that qualifier and makes the change sound uniform across major banks.
- A Harvard-affiliated working paper covers 65 million workers at more than 280,000 firms. Junior
  employment declined relative to non-adopters, mainly because of slower hiring rather than
  layoffs. The paper is observational and uses job postings to identify AI-adopting firms.

Davis also says reviewing AI work requires different habits. Fluent, confident output can conceal
mistakes, so polish cannot substitute for domain verification.

A CHI 2025 survey of 319 knowledge workers and 936 AI-use examples found that greater confidence in
AI was associated with less reported critical thinking. It supports concern about cognitive
offloading, but does not prove that AI universally causes expertise to decay.

Davis proposes two replacement training models:

1. **Judgment-first practice:** let AI perform a task, then require juniors to find errors and
   explain the standards the output violates.
2. **Manual–AI comparison:** perform selected critical tasks manually, ask AI to do the same work,
   and compare failure modes on both sides.

The first model is attributed to Deloitte, but no matching Deloitte source was found. A similar,
documented shift at PwC emphasizes audit fundamentals, critical thinking, professional skepticism,
and supervising AI.

The second model is documented by Wall Street Prep and Financial Edge for 2026 analyst training.
The providers describe a sequence of manual work, AI work, and side-by-side comparison, though no
independent outcome study shows how widely it is used or how well it works.

The practical framework is to audit junior tasks, identify what each task teaches, maintain lists
of skills to retain or delegate, define explicit review rubrics, and preserve manual drills for
foundational or high-risk work.

Delegation should follow measured reliability, not merely an AI system's ability to produce a
plausible answer. High-stakes workflows may need independent evidence, deterministic tests,
adversarial cases, and accountable human review rather than one model checking another.

The unresolved question is whether judgment can be learned without enough underlying execution.
The video offers a useful management hypothesis, not proof that judgment-only training can replace
experience in every field or that displaced workers will necessarily move into strategic roles.
