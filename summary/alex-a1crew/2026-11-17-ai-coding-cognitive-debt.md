# AI Coding Speed Can Create Cognitive and Architectural Debt

[article](../../articles/alex-a1crew/2026-11-17-ai-coding-cognitive-debt.md)

AI can generate plausible code faster than developers can understand or review it. Passing tests
and polished syntax do not prove architectural fit, security, maintainability, or reviewer
comprehension. The video's engineering warning is useful, but its neuroscience is unsupported.

The generation effect is real: people often remember information better when they generate it than
when they only read it. This does not prove that developers must type every line. AI can replace
practice or support learning through prediction, explanation, feedback, and experimentation.

Myelin participates in some forms of learning, but a motor-learning study in mice does not establish
a coding mechanism. No evidence shows that reviewing AI code bypasses the hippocampus, causes
next-day forgetting, or produces "AI-induced neuroatrophy," brain shrinkage, or lost spatial memory.
"Turing fatigue" is also unsourced; automation bias and cognitive fatigue are grounded alternatives.

The alleged 2026 "seniority cliff" is a risk hypothesis. A Stanford working paper found employment
for ages 22–25 in AI-exposed work 19% below a comparison trajectory, mainly through lower hiring.
The authors called it early, non-causal evidence; it did not show impaired senior development.

The video attributes a shift toward intent, context, and validation to Criteo CTO Diarmuid Gill. His
identity is verified, but no matching editorial appeared on his official page. The attribution
remains unverified. The "Sarah" payment-system failure is an unsourced hypothetical or composite.

Architectural drift is a real risk. A 2026 study found that prompt rules alone did not reliably stop
agent boundary violations and supported deterministic architecture checks. Tests constrain only the
behavior they encode; teams also need security analysis, dependency review, architecture gates,
migration tests, and human design review.

Independently reconstructing a design before reading generated output may help learning and reduce
anchoring on high-risk work. Related learning research supports generation and productive failure,
but this exact protocol has not been tested for AI code or brain health.

The strongest recommendation is adversarial review: test odd inputs and state transitions, inspect
dependencies, and try to falsify the implementation. A study of 1,689 Copilot programs across 89
security scenarios found about 40% vulnerable; that rate is evaluation-specific, while fuzzing has
broader evidence for finding real defects.

A safer loop defines intent and acceptance criteria before generation, predicts dependencies and
failure modes, enforces scope, checks architecture and security, and investigates design deviations.
AI can remove toil without removing learning when teams preserve explanation, mentoring, feedback,
and accountability proportional to risk.
