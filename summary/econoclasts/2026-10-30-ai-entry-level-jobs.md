# AI May Close Career Entrances Before It Eliminates Whole Jobs

[article](../../articles/econoclasts/2026-10-30-ai-entry-level-jobs.md)

Wolfgang Münchau and Yanis Varoufakis argue that AI may first damage employment by reducing junior
recruitment rather than producing mass layoffs. Employers can retain incumbents while vacancies and
career ladders disappear. Young people then lose the work through which they acquire skills.

## What the evidence supports

A Stanford study of 3.5–5 million ADP employees per month found that employment among workers aged
22–25 fell about 11% in the two most AI-exposed occupation groups from November 2022 to June 2026.
It grew about 10% in the three less-exposed groups. Weaker hiring created most of the gap.

A Census study using near-universal private-sector records found a similar decline among workers
aged 22–24 in the most exposed state-industry cells. These are descriptive patterns, not causal
estimates. Education, interest rates, remote work, pandemic restructuring, and the technology-sector
correction remain possible contributors. Neither study finds economy-wide AI displacement.

UK information and communication vacancies fell from about 78,000 in spring 2022 to 37,000 in late
2026, but the wider vacancy market declined similarly. Graduate hiring among large employers fell
8% in 2024/25 and rose 4% in 2026. The data do not identify AI as the cause.

The United States still counted 392,880 paralegals and legal assistants in May 2025, contradicting
the claim that the occupation barely exists. The alleged average cost of closing a French or German
company equal to three years of operating expenses also lacks support.

## The speakers' positions

Münchau expects a gradual version of Citrini Research's fictional 2028 crisis. New AI-native firms
could displace labor-intensive incumbents while protected firms reduce recruitment. He expects a
large but delayed robotics payoff and sees durable value in subject depth and critical judgment.

Varoufakis agrees about blocked career entrances but rejects a distinct robot tax. He proposes
transferring company shares to a public social-equity fund so society receives dividends from the
knowledge and data it helped create.

He argues that Western AI may create useful output without earning enough revenue to cover its
investment. This is a theoretical forecast, not an accounting fact. Measured AI productivity effects
vary sharply by task, model, worker, and workflow.

Both contrast Western service-sector AI with Chinese manufacturing and robotics. China's large robot
market is real, but the episode does not establish that most Chinese AI goes into robotics or that
it is increasing youth employment. Varoufakis stresses ownership; Münchau expects hiring freezes.

The episode usefully redirects attention from redundancies to missing vacancies. Stable employment
can conceal weakening recruitment, apprenticeships, and career progression. Policymakers should
track hiring by age and occupation alongside layoffs.

The evidence supports concern about early-career hiring in highly exposed US work. It does not prove
an AI-caused lost generation or the broader forecasts about China, robotics, and political change.
