# Evolutionary Mismatch: Exercise, Sleep, Diet, and Modern Health

## Scope
This summarizes a 47:04 *Sasha i Sens* interview with Daniel Lieberman.
It is based on Ukrainian automatic captions translated into English.
Caption errors may affect names, dates, mechanisms, and causal wording.
All biomedical and numerical claims below are the speakers' claims, not verified facts.
This is educational reporting, not medical advice.
## Mismatch and movement
Lieberman calls modern illness partly an evolutionary mismatch: culture changed faster
than bodies adapted to abundant food, low activity, and chronic stress.
He says reluctance to exercise is natural energy conservation, but not necessarily healthy.
No exercise dose fits everyone; age, health, fitness, type, intensity, and duration matter.
He claims even about two vigorous minutes daily may help, with benefits later plateauing.
He favors varied cardio and strength work plus movement spread across the day.
Tanzanian hunter-gatherers and Americans may both sit about 10 hours, he says, but the
former often interrupt sitting after 10–15 minutes rather than remaining still for hours.
He links unused muscle to sarcopenia, falls, frailty, and loss of daily independence.
The 10,000-step goal began with a 1960s Japanese pedometer, not a biological threshold.
He says mortality benefits flatten near 7,000 steps, while cardiovascular gains may not.
## Running and sleep
Lieberman says running injuries are common but rejects the idea that running causes arthritis.
He says loading may support cartilage, while existing joint disease can make running worse.
Foot strike shifts load between knees, calves, and Achilles tendons; it does not erase risk.
He warns that any move from cushioned to minimalist shoes must be slow and technique-aware.
He describes about seven hours of sleep as typical for adults, not a universal target.
Sleep may aid memory and brain-waste clearance, while activity may help by lowering cortisol.
He warns that anxious sleep tracking can create "orthosomnia" and worsen sleep itself.
## Fat, food, stress, and prevention
Lieberman frames fat as an energy reserve for costly brains and lactation, with a downside.
His figures include 20% of energy for an adult brain, up to 50% for a child's brain,
and up to 600 calories daily for lactation; none was sourced within the interview.
He says chronic cortisol elevation can raise hunger, disturb sleep, and favor visceral fat.
He presents meat as nutrient-rich, including B12, but claims intake tracks with cancer risk.
He favors mostly plant foods and less sugar, refined starch, and ultra-processed food.
Cancer and inflammation are multifactorial; diet claims here are not personal prescriptions.
He revises an 80% preventable-disease claim to an uncertain estimate near 70%, above 50%.
His five themes are no smoking, better diet, activity, adequate sleep, and less chronic stress.
## Medicines, allergies, and cancer caution
Lieberman calls GLP-1 drugs a breakthrough for severe obesity but notes digestive effects,
muscle loss, cost, rebound after stopping, and unknown long-term outcomes.
He does not oppose use; he urges planning with clinicians for strength and discontinuation.
He favors the hygiene hypothesis for rising allergies but admits data and mechanisms are thin.
His peanut-exposure comment must not become unsupervised feeding advice; severe reactions occur.
He says antibiotics may disrupt immune ecology, but this is not a reason to avoid needed care.
He cites 150 activity minutes weekly and a 33–50% breast-cancer risk reduction, but those
claims, hormonal mechanisms, and his meat/TMAO discussion need primary-source verification.
Risk reduction is not prevention, and no exercise, food, sleep, or drug choice guarantees health.
