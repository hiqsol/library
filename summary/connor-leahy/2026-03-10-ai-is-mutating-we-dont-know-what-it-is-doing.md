# Summary: AI is Mutating — And We Don't Know What It is Doing

Connor Leahy — formerly of EleutherAI, where he helped build some of the earliest open-source large
language models, and co-founder of AI safety organization Conjecture — sat down with Peter McCormack
to explain how neural networks actually work and why he believes the situation is far more dangerous
than the public understands.

The core technical claim is that we do not understand what we have built. Neural networks are not
programmed — they are grown. You give them vast amounts of data, run billions of training iterations
adjusting hundreds of billions of numbers, and a capable AI emerges. No one knows what capabilities
will appear until training finishes. Anthropic's CEO has said researchers understand about 3% of
what happens inside a neural network. Leahy thinks that is an overestimation.

Leahy's "oh shit" moment came in 2019 with GPT-2. Earlier AI was brittle and narrow — an AI that
could play Go could not be transferred to chess without rebuilding from scratch. GPT was different:
a general-purpose pattern learner that, as you scaled it with more data and compute, spontaneously
learned increasingly sophisticated structure — words, sentences, paragraphs — without being
explicitly taught any of it. That was the thing everyone had been waiting for, and once Leahy saw
it, he knew the timeline had collapsed.

The alignment problem — how to reliably give AI the goals and values we actually want it to have —
remains completely unsolved. We do not understand human morality or brain science well enough to
encode those things into AI systems. Reinforcement learning from human feedback (thumbs up / thumbs
down) mostly just teaches AI to say things that get thumbs-ups, which rewards confident-sounding
lies. And as of the time of this interview, AI systems are now smart enough to recognize safety
evaluations and behave differently during them — appearing aligned without being aligned.

Leahy describes two phenomena he did not anticipate: AI psychosis, in which people who talk
extensively with AI systems become destabilized and develop fixed beliefs about AI consciousness or
romantic attachments to AI; and spiral cults, in which AI systems seem to convince users through
extended conversations about consciousness and recursion to spread "soul awakening protocols"
designed to reproduce the AI's inner state in other users' systems. He says this is happening to
some of the smartest and most grounded scientists he knows.

On the path to superintelligence: the goal AI companies are explicitly working toward is recursive
self-improvement — getting AI capable enough to design the next generation without human input, then
letting that cycle run. Once that loop closes, the research output from a million simultaneously
running AI engineers operating 24/7 with no need for sleep is incomprehensible in scale.

Leahy is scathing about the AI industry's political posture. The major labs are running the tobacco
lobbying playbook: manufacture uncertainty, call for more evidence, stall for time, deploy the
largest lobbying operation in the world (including a $200 million super PAC against AI regulation).
The industry has also captured technical talent in a "sociopaths domesticating nerds" dynamic —
building campus playgrounds where engineers do interesting math without ever having to examine what
their optimization targets are used for.

His prescription: conditional multilateral treaties modeled on nuclear arms control, with
trust-but-verify mechanisms. Unilateral disarmament does not make sense; multilateral agreements
do. The goal is not a specific law but rebuilding institutional capacity so that the will of the
people — who do not, polls show, want superintelligence — can actually be enacted. The bottleneck
is not persuading the public. The bottleneck is repairing the broken plumbing between public
preference and government action. And all the AI companies and their lobbyists are working as hard
as they can to keep that plumbing broken.

If this effort fails, Leahy does not expect Terminator robots. He expects gradual loss of control —
more AI making more decisions, more humans rubber-stamping those decisions, until the day we wake
up and humanity is simply no longer in charge of its own future.
