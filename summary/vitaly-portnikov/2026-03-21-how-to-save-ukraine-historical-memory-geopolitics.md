Public lecture and Q&A by Vitaliy Portnikov at an OpenCode.IF event, March 21, 2026 (the
anniversary of Shevchenko's death). Topics: historical memory in geopolitics, Ukrainian statehood,
Holodomor, Polish-Ukrainian relations, the ongoing war, occupied territories, and democracy.

- Historical memory is a genuine geopolitical force, not merely a screen for policy. Putin uses
  the ghosts of Peter the Great and Catherine I; Trump ran on "Make America Great Again."
- Russia's core calculation in February 2022: Ukrainians were "only pretending." Four years of
  war have proven the opposite — Ukrainian identity has intensified even in regions under heavy
  Russian cultural influence.
- People who switched to Ukrainian after Feb 2022 and later returned to Russian nevertheless now
  identify as Ukrainian. Portnikov calls that a social victory that should not be disclaimed.
- The comparison to Soviet Jews sent to Israel: people forced into a national identity eventually
  had to answer "what does it mean to be this?" The answer turned out to mean defending the
  country, not just holding a passport.
- Shevchenko (whose death anniversary was March 21) had to begin Ukrainian national narrative
  from the Haidamaka rebellions because Kyivan Rus was already claimed by Moscow via the Romanovs.
  Other nations (Poles, Lithuanians) could appeal to intact dynastic traditions. Ukrainians could
  not.
- Ukrainian state-building is continuous: Kyiv, Halych, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania,
  Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Hetmanate, Zaporizhian Sich — then a ~200-year gap before
  the Ukrainian People's Republic (1918). That 200-year gap should not erase a millennium of
  state-building.
- Ukraine is not a 35-year-old state as Putin claims. Ukrainian statehood is centuries old.
- Yushchenko was a mediocre politician but an outstanding statesman who understood memory
  politics when almost no one else did. Before him, the Holodomor was marginal in public discourse.
- Party of Regions voted almost unanimously against recognizing the Holodomor as genocide —
  fearing to offend Russia.
- The key evidentiary point on Holodomor: only Ukrainian ethnic settlement territories (Ukrainian
  SSR, Kuban, parts of Kazakhstan) were militarily sealed to prevent escape. That was a zone of
  execution, not a general famine.
- Osip Mandelstam — a Russian poet with no political agenda — wrote from Voronezh in the 1930s
  about "the terrible shadows of Ukraine and Kuban." He saw it; official Russia still claims not to.
- Personal account: Portnikov's great-grandparents in Kamianka (Cherkasy region) refused to
  evacuate before the German advance because the Holodomor had taught them that Soviet propaganda
  lied. They concluded Nazi atrocity reports were also propaganda. They were killed
  in the Holocaust.
- The Holodomor recognition problem: communities in eastern and southern Ukraine lived in houses
  taken from famine victims, or descended from those who enforced the famine. Reassessing the
  Holodomor meant reassessing family history.
- Why Russia insists on Kyivan Rus: "Russians" (Rossiyane) is itself an invented term applied to
  people historically called Suzdalians or Muscovites. The Rus identity arrived in Moscow with
  Ukrainian lands and Ukrainian intellectuals who offered it to Muscovite monarchs.
- Prince Andrei Bogolyubsky's declaration of the Vladimir principality as a separate Grand Duchy
  was, in modern terms, separatism from Kyiv. No nation wants to trace its history to separatism.
- Moscow's imperial formation: Ryazan, Tver, Novgorod — populations expelled or killed, replaced
  by Muscovites. Every Russian regional center was an independent state whose people were replaced.
  This was the imperial model from the start; Russia never built itself as a national state.
- Russia needs Kyiv because without the Kyivan Rus claim, its legitimacy rests on conquest
  alone. Kyiv provides the Rurikid genealogy that makes expansion appear as inheritance, not theft.
- Russia's war goal: liquidate Ukrainian statehood and the Ukrainian nation as an independent
  entity. This is not just Putin — it is supported by the majority of Russians.
- Russia has not achieved this goal. In February 2022 it planned to be in Kyiv; in March 2026
  it is still near Pokrovsk.
- If Ukraine preserves its state, nation, language, and European orientation, it has won —
  regardless of territorial lines at the ceasefire.
- Without Ukraine, there is no restored Russian Empire. Belarus, Tatarstan, Chechnya do not make
  Russia a hegemon. Only with Ukrainian territory does Russia become a European hegemon.
- Russian information strategy (via media, influencers, US right-wing channels) will claim
  Ukraine suffered total catastrophe. If Ukrainians believe it, they lose.
- The Finnish model: Finland ceded territory but zero citizens. Not one person remained in Vyborg
  or the Karelian territories — Finnish residents evacuated twice (after Winter War, after
  Continuation War).
- Children raised in Russian-occupied territories as subjects of the Russian Federation will become
  ordinary Russian citizens unless their parents bring them to Ukraine. Crimean Tatars are a partial
  exception because Crimea is their only homeland.
- On Polish-Ukrainian relations and Volhynia: the historical pattern is clear — when Poles and
  Ukrainians fight each other, Russians take everything. Both sides carry responsibility; dialogue
  must be mutual, not accusatory.
- Poland built a postwar ethnic state (Operation Vistula, expulsion of Jews in the 1960s) and
  has difficulty transitioning to a political nation model. Ukraine made that transition relatively
  fast — Maidans and the war accelerated the shift to a multi-ethnic political nation.
- The Polish episcopate found moral strength to say to German bishops "we forgive and ask
  forgiveness." The same formula should apply to Polish-Ukrainian dialogue over Volhynia.
- Ukrainian identity built on protest (language switch as resistance) is fragile. Identity built
  on love and self-awareness is durable. The task: build attachment to Ukrainian through love of
  the language and state, not opposition to Russia.
- Democracy is Ukraine's structural survival condition. There was no Ukraine under Stalin (left
  totalitarianism) and no Ukraine under Hitler (right totalitarianism). Ukraine exists only within
  and supported by the democratic world. Every time that world weakens, threats to Ukraine increase.
- State policy is the mechanism for converting cultural memory work into political reality —
  Ukrainian radio quotas, film support, theater investment. Without state policy, memory culture
  cannot scale.
