# Waterless Laundry with Liquid Carbon Dioxide

[summary](../../summary/theorix/2026-09-13-waterless-washing-machine-technology.md)

## Scope and evidentiary status

This article analyzes a 26:36 Theorix video published on September 13, 2026.
Its supplied headline is promotional and grammatically malformed; the neutral title above is used.
The video is a narrated secondary source, not a test report, peer-reviewed review, or LG release.
Its captions are automatic and contain likely recognition errors, so uncertain names are flagged.
Unless named evidence is specified below, technical and historical statements are Theorix's claims.

The video establishes only that a case is being narrated and that particular documents are alleged.
It shows no controlled wash test, prototype inspection, trial data, life-cycle assessment, price,
certification, customer installation, or statement from LG reproduced in the transcript.
A patent, a regulatory pathway, and older commercial systems can support plausibility.
They do not prove LG has a market-ready machine or that it can replace domestic water washing.

## The problem the video assigns to conventional laundry

Theorix says a conventional load uses roughly 100 liters of treated, drinkable water.
It first calls the actual dirt in a typical load about one teaspoon, then calls it one tablespoon.
That internal mismatch matters because neither estimate is sourced in the video.
The water must be captured, treated, distributed, heated in many cycles, and discharged.
The outgoing stream can contain detergent, surfactants, phosphates, optical brighteners,
fragrance chemicals, fabric softener, other chemicals, soil, and shed textile fibers.
The narrator calls laundry one of civilization's largest invisible water consumers and says
billions of loads are run every week, but provides no citation or global accounting.

For China, Theorix gives a much more specific figure: household laundry allegedly creates about
10 billion tons of wastewater each year. The video says treatment plants were not designed to
remove every laundry chemical, allowing some material to reach rivers and oceans.
Neither the quantity nor the treatment claim is demonstrated or linked to a named study.

The detergent explanation is simplified. The video says water's strong intermolecular attraction
and surface tension impede entry into fibers, while detergent lowers that tension.
It then says detergent is not what cleans, but what lets water do the cleaning.
That rhetorical formulation omits detergents' other functions and is not established by evidence.

## The proposed liquid-CO2 cycle

The process discussed for LG is liquid carbon dioxide, not ordinary gas, dry ice, or necessarily
supercritical CO2. The machine would refrigerate and compress CO2 until it becomes a clear liquid,
maintaining that phase under pressure. Theorix says liquid CO2 has very low surface tension and
acts as a strong solvent for oils, grease, and organic grime.

In the narrated cycle, liquid CO2 enters fabric spaces, dissolves oils that bind dirt, lifts soil,
and holds removed material in suspension. The source says this needs no detergent or softener.
At cycle end, depressurization changes the liquid back into gas, leaving clothing dry.
Dirt and oil remain in a collection chamber; the gas is captured, recompressed, and stored.
The claimed closed loop reuses the same CO2 over successive loads.

On that account, there is no rinse, water-extraction spin, separate drying step, wastewater,
or routine drain discharge. “Wash” and “dry” become one event because fabric was never wetted.
These are descriptions of the intended cycle, not results from a machine examined in the video.
The transcript gives no cycle time, CO2 inventory, recovery percentage, solvent make-up rate,
operating temperature, cleaning capacity, residue analysis, or measured emissions.

The narrator also discusses supercritical CO2, but that is a distinct pressure-temperature regime.
NASA's cited work, coffee decaffeination, and some industrial cleaning examples concern dense or
supercritical CO2; they do not by themselves validate LG's claimed liquid-CO2 laundry process.
Conventional dry cleaning is also distinct: its historic dominant solvent was said to be
perchloroethylene, or “perc,” rather than water or CO2.

## What LG is said to have added

The video does not support calling liquid-CO2 cleaning a new South Korean invention.
It portrays LG Electronics as reviving and engineering an old method for a new regulated market.
The claimed innovation is a practical sealed washer: housing, inner laundry drum, and a barrier
that seals the opening of a pressurized chamber, plus the systems needed for repeated operation.
No evidence in the transcript shows which details are novel over predecessor machines.

On December 30, 2021, South Korea's Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy allegedly gave LG
special approval under its Regulatory Sandbox Program to develop and test a CO2 washer.
Theorix says high-pressure rules otherwise made consumer commercialization effectively impossible.
The sandbox temporarily relaxed specified rules so safety could be assessed in real-world tests.
Its deliberation committee reportedly cited environmental features and foreign commercialization,
including Electrolux's earlier equipment.

LG allegedly committed to operate the machine for two years at its own research center.
If successful, the filed plan was to seek permission for machines in commercial laundries in
Korean shopping malls. The automatic captions render the venue words as “commercialies” and
“mallries”; “commercial laundries” is the likely meaning, but the transcript cannot confirm it.
The video provides no trial start or completion date, protocol, incident record, energy result,
or final authorization. A planned test and conditional next step are not a completed rollout.

Theorix also cites US patent 12,359,363, granted to LG Electronics in July 2025 and titled
“Washing Machine.” The first claim is paraphrased as CO2 washing in a sealed housing, with an
inner drum and a barrier sealing the pressure-chamber opening. The underlying application is said
to trace to Korean filings in late 2020, before public sandbox approval, and to name five inventors.
The captions say LG Electronics of “Soul,” almost certainly a recognition error for Seoul.
The patent is framed as proof of sustained engineering and interest beyond Korea.
It establishes, at most from this video's account, that rights were granted for claimed features.
It does not establish manufacture, performance, regulatory approval, commercial intent, or sales.

## Claimed environmental and garment benefits

### Water and chemicals

The headline benefit is zero process water and therefore no detergent-bearing greywater.
That would avoid the video's roughly 100-liter conventional-load figure at the machine.
It would also avoid detergent, softener, rinse water, and the energy of heating that water.
The video offers no system boundary covering equipment production, cleaning, maintenance,
or any water and chemicals used upstream to supply CO2 and electricity.

### Energy

Theorix argues that a fair comparison must include conventional water heating and an approximately
one-hour dryer cycle. A CO2 system would avoid both, but must refrigerate and compress gas.
The video says skeptical engineers have calculated liquefaction power, yet names none and gives
no values. It says the two-year trial should determine the balance across millions of loads.
No trial result or energy-per-kilogram comparison is presented, so an energy saving is not proven.

### Textile care, microbes, and microplastics

Low surface tension is claimed to let liquid CO2 enter fibers without water or surfactants.
The video implies gentler textile care because wet agitation and chemical stripping are avoided.
It provides no tensile-strength, colorfastness, shrinkage, wear, or repeated-cycle comparison.
Nor does it test mixed fabrics, trims, dyes, prints, elastomers, or sensitive finishes.

Theorix says dense CO2 has a natural bactericidal effect and therefore calls garments effectively
sanitized without bleach or hot steam. It mentions cleaning medical implants as an analogy.
No organism, exposure condition, log reduction, hygiene standard, or LG test is supplied.
The analogy cannot establish sanitization for a laundry load under LG's operating conditions.

For synthetic clothes, the video says tumbling, rubbing, and surfactants accelerate microfiber
release. It calls washers one of Earth's largest single microplastic sources and says many fibers
pass treatment plants into rivers, oceans, fish, and people. Those broad claims are not cited.
The CO2 process is said to prevent a wastewater “torrent” because it has neither surfactants nor
a drain. That may change the release route, but the video gives no fiber-shedding measurements.
Tumbling can still impose mechanical stress, and captured dirt or fibers still require disposal.
Thus reduced aquatic discharge is a mechanism claim, not proof of zero microplastic generation.

Samsung is said to offer an external filter capturing fibers down to 65 micrometers.
Theorix calls filters a patch and says several countries are drafting mandatory-filter laws.
No countries, bills, dates, test efficiency, or treatment of particles below 65 micrometers are
identified, so neither regulatory scope nor comparative effectiveness can be checked here.

### CO2 reuse and net emissions

The machine is described as capturing, recompressing, storing, and reusing CO2 in a closed loop.
The narrator consequently says it discharges no gas to the atmosphere during the normal cycle.
No recovery rate or leak test is given. “Closed” does not establish perfectly lossless operation.
The video does not identify whether the CO2 is newly produced or captured from another process.
It does not count electricity emissions, refrigerant impacts, replacement CO2, pressure-vessel
manufacture, transport, servicing, end-of-life treatment, or disposal of concentrated residues.
Reuse could reduce solvent demand, but the source does not prove zero or lower net emissions.
A comparative life-cycle assessment would be required for that conclusion.

## Safety and engineering constraints

Theorix says liquid CO2 exists only above roughly five atmospheres and practical cleaners operate
at much higher pressure. The chamber therefore resembles a thick-walled, heavy, sealed pressure
vessel rather than a thin household drum. Cost, reliable seals, doors, pumps, recompression,
refrigeration, fatigue life, sensors, and safe servicing are the central engineering problems.
Only the first four ideas are explicit in the narration; no detailed hazard analysis is supplied.

CO2 is described as nonflammable, colorless, odorless, non-toxic “in the quantities involved,”
and residue-free. The video invokes food decaffeination since the late 1970s and medical-device
cleaning. Those uses support familiarity, not the safety of this appliance, location, or cycle.
The transcript does not quantify “the quantities,” leakage, room ventilation, exposure detection,
emergency depressurization, vessel rupture, door interlocks, maintenance, or worker procedures.
Its confident statement that the unit will not “blow up” is not a demonstrated safety case.
Sandbox testing itself indicates that pressure safety and regulation still require proof.

## Cost, infrastructure, performance, and adoption

The narrator says that in the early 2000s a dry cleaner could buy three conventional machines for
the price of one CO2 system. At the time, cheap water and energy and little public discussion of
microplastics weakened its business case. The video later locates the reversal around 2005,
but supplies no price series, total cost of ownership, or evidence that economics have reversed.

LG's initial target is therefore said to be industrial and commercial service, not homes.
Large machines and shopping-mall laundries could spread capital cost over thousands of loads.
Hotels, hospitals, universities, laundromats, and dry cleaners are proposed as early users because
of water and sewer bills, hygiene needs, high utilization, drought rules, or pressure to replace
perc. The video does not address bulk CO2 supply, on-site storage, building permits, ventilation,
trained technicians, spare parts, pressure inspections, residue handling, or retrofit space.
Those requirements could be material infrastructure and operating constraints.

Cleaning ability is asserted mainly for oils, grease, and organic grime. No head-to-head protocol
covers particulate soil, salts, sugars, protein stains, odor, stain pretreatment, white retention,
or consumer laundry standards. The transcript supplies no throughput, rewash rate, load size,
fabric damage, cross-contamination, maintenance interval, or customer acceptance test.
Dry, fragrance-free output may also conflict with learned expectations that clean means wet,
warm, and scented. Theorix says habit helped defeat the early-2000s wave, but does not document it.

LG is said to be expanding professional heat-pump washers and dryers in North American
laundromats, hotels, hospitals, and universities. Current professional equipment allegedly washes
and dries a load in about an hour. That installed commercial strategy is presented as a beachhead,
not as evidence that CO2 models share its economics, serviceability, or customer demand.

## Predecessors, alternatives, and competitive pressure

### Earlier CO2 and dry-cleaning systems

The automatic transcript says an American inventor named “Maf” filed a 1977 patent for chilled
liquid-CO2 garment cleaning. Because “Maf” may be a caption error and no patent number is given,
the inventor's identity cannot safely be repaired or independently inferred from this source.

In 1979, NASA allegedly published a technical brief on supercritical-CO2 removal of oil and
chemical residues from delicate metal aerospace components. Theorix says this put core science
in public patent and government documents by the end of the 1970s. It is an industrial-cleaning
precedent, not a household-laundry demonstration.

In the 1990s, engineers reportedly built commercial liquid-CO2 dry-cleaning systems as safer
alternatives to perc. Specialized pump makers supplied hermetically sealed circulation systems;
companies formed around the method; and Sweden's Electrolux commercialized machines.
Theorix says the equipment was built, sold, and operated, yet lost commercially because of
pressure-vessel cost and economics. No models, sales, sites, test data, or failure evidence appear.

Perc is described as a chlorinated hazardous substance used for generations in US dry cleaning,
with worker-health and neighborhood-contamination concerns that prompted regulatory pressure.
The video predicts tightening bans, but names no law, regulator, jurisdiction, or effective date.
CO2 dry cleaning's existence shows feasibility in a professional context, not household parity.

### Other reduced-water approaches

In the United Kingdom, a University of Leeds textile-chemistry professor is said to have founded
Xeros. Its machines use millions of nylon polymer beads, each about the size of a grain of rice.
With a little humidity, the beads allegedly absorb stains while tumbling and knocking dirt loose.
The machine is said to recover 99.95% of the beads; a batch lasts hundreds of loads; and water use
can be one-tenth that of a conventional machine. The beads are not a zero-water or CO2 process.
During Cape Town's drought in South Africa, such machines were reportedly imported because water
for basic services was running out. No date, number of units, operator, or measured saving is given.

India's 80 Wash is described as claiming an 80-second cycle, a few milliliters of water, and zero
detergent. The claimed applications include clothes, PPE kits, and metal components.
The video does not name its cleaning mechanism, test conditions, capacity, validation, or rollout.

Researchers in China reportedly published a different approach in Communications Chemistry in
March 2026: two alternating polymer layers form an invisible hydration shield on each fiber.
Treated fabric allegedly sheds food stains, oily residues, and pathogens under plain tap water,
without detergent; the coating lasts more than 100 washes and works on cotton and synthetics.
This alters textiles rather than replacing the washer, and the source gives no paper title,
authors, comparative water quantity, scale-up evidence, coating impacts, or market status.

### Korean appliance rivalry

Theorix presents LG-Samsung competition as a commercialization motive, not technical proof.
Both are headquartered in the same city and allegedly recruit from the same universities, monitor
patents, hire rivals' engineers, and race products to market. LG reportedly beat Samsung by weeks
with Korea's first heat-pump washer-dryer combo, washing 25 kilograms and drying 13 kilograms in
one uninterrupted, one-button cycle. These unequal capacities are quoted, not explained.

Samsung's Bespoke AI Laundry Combo allegedly sold 1,000 units in three days and 10,000 within a
couple of months. Its newest version is said to beat its predecessors' wash-to-dry times and accept
conversational-AI voice commands. In 2026, LG reportedly created five AI-generated K-pop idols,
each representing a washer feature, to market an appliance. These details illustrate competition;
they neither concern CO2 performance nor show consumer demand for pressurized cleaning.

## Commercial outlook and conclusion

The video expects commercial laundry before retail homes and explicitly rejects a local-store CO2
washer “next year.” It points to water scarcity in Cape Town, the American Southwest, Southern
Europe, Northern India, and much of the Middle East as a demand driver. It speculates that drought
rules could eventually make a zero-water machine the only type allowed to operate.
No such CO2-specific mandate, purchase order, installed LG machine, launch date, or price is shown.

Theorix predicts that a household washer may still use some water ten years later, while eventually
casting conventional practice as 100 liters of drinking water plus a cup of chemicals applied to
a tablespoon of dirt, followed by an hour of drying. This repeats the video's teaspoon/tablespoon
inconsistency and converts an argument into a forecast, not a measured universal load.

The strongest defensible conclusion is narrower than the video's “question of when” ending.
Liquid-CO2 cleaning has narrated historical precedents, and LG is linked to a sandbox and patent.
The proposed closed cycle could avoid process water and drying, but every major comparative claim
still needs disclosed testing: cleaning, hygiene, fiber wear, leakage, energy, cost, reliability,
life-cycle emissions, infrastructure, and regulatory compliance. The video supports investigation
of a plausible commercial technology; it does not prove a safe, economical, market-ready,
net-low-emissions replacement for conventional laundry, much less a universal household one.
