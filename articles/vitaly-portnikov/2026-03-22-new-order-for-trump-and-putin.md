# New Order for Trump and Putin

A lecture-format interview with journalist and political analyst Vitaliy Portnikov,
recorded at King Danylo University. The discussion spans professional development,
Ukrainian state-building, the current war, and the geopolitical realignment taking
shape around Trump, Putin, and China.

---

## Finding a Professional Path in Turbulent Times

The conversation opens with a question aimed at students: how did Portnikov find his
professional path and what separates those who reach the top from those who remain
in the middle?

Portnikov's formative years coincided with the collapse of the Soviet Union — a
period of radical uncertainty comparable to what today's young Ukrainians face. He
recalls receiving a Lithuanian-published novel by Jewish writer Grigory Kanovich
while studying in Dnipro in 1985, a book about the impossibility of self-realization
for Jews under the Russian Empire. The resonance was immediate: Portnikov himself
could not enroll in Kyiv due to systemic discrimination and had no desire to do
Soviet-style journalism built on lies.

He had no ambition to become a famous journalist. His motive was entirely different:
he wanted Ukraine to be independent, Jews to be free to emigrate to Israel, and
communism to end. Journalism was the instrument to achieve those goals — not a career
ladder, but a tool for change.

His advice to students: don't orient around career goals or earnings. Orient around
what you want to change in the world and ask whether you can do it without resources.
Financial independence is necessary, but it's a means, not an end. People who set
high humanistic goals tend to outperform those who only chase positions and salaries.

## Ukraine's Role After 35 Years of Independence

When asked what he now wants to change through journalism, Portnikov traced the
evolution of his goals. Ukraine became independent sooner than he expected — at 24,
he had achieved the first major objective. But independence arrived before Ukrainian
society fully understood what it meant to be a state-forming people.

He describes the work of those decades: building international journalism in Ukrainian,
shifting Kyiv's media from Russian to Ukrainian language, creating a first-ever
Ukrainian-language international TV program. Now the task is to support society in the
war and in a realistic perception of the challenges ahead.

He notes wryly that he keeps setting a retirement date — 50, then 55, then 60 — and
each time history intervenes. In May 2022, the war was just beginning. Now, with 60
approaching in May 2027, he sees no moral right to step back.

## Ukraine as a Military and Technological Power

On whether Ukraine can build a global role on its wartime military expertise:

Portnikov draws a striking comparison to a German TV series about two parallel worlds —
one that developed high technology, another that poured all resources into medicine
after a pandemic. Ukraine, under existential pressure, has produced the world's most
sophisticated practitioners of drone warfare. "Spider Web" (Pavutyna) — Ukraine's
deep-strike drone operation — represents tomorrow's warfare, not yesterday's.

He argues there are currently only two genuinely modern armies in the world: the Armed
Forces of Ukraine and the Russian Armed Forces. The difference is which coalition they
serve. Russia's side includes China, Iran, and North Korea — powers seeking to
destabilize the civilized world. The democratic world has American missiles, F-16s,
and money — but these are yesterday's tools.

Ukraine is more inventive precisely because it fights for survival. Russia can rely on
overwhelming force; Ukraine must rely on ingenuity. This makes Ukraine's military
knowledge about contemporary warfare — including how Iran-style adversaries think when
fighting the US — uniquely valuable to Western partners.

## Russia's Stability and the Question of Collapse

A question from students challenges Portnikov: given that Russia's economy is on a
thread and its army stalls, should Ukraine reject peace deals and push for Russia's
breakup?

His answer is measured. When he says Russia faces no external threat to its
statehood, he means it literally: Russian forces were near Kyiv; Ukrainian forces are
nowhere near Moscow. Russia remains the world's largest nuclear power alongside the
United States. Territorial integrity of Russia will always be of interest not only to
Russia itself.

On the collapse scenario: the non-Russian peoples of the Russian Federation are
already a shrinking minority, and Moscow has proven willing to bomb its own population
to maintain control, as in Chechnya. More importantly, 25 years of intensified
Russification — more aggressive than anything in the Soviet era — has hollowed out
national identities. Many young Dagestanis, Tuvans, and Buryats now identify as
"russkie" (ethnic Russians) rather than even "rossiyane" (Russian citizens). The
researchers predicting independence movements are drawing on Soviet-era data, not
Russian-era reality.

There is also a logical trap: if we argue that ethnic sub-groups within Russia can
form separate states, Russia's own propaganda can apply the same logic to Ukraine —
claiming Galicia, Donbas, and central Ukraine are sufficiently distinct to be separate
entities. Portnikov insists on consistency: either international law governs
(territorial integrity for both) or it doesn't (in which case Ukraine itself could
disappear from the map).

## 35 Years of Ukrainian Statehood: What Went Wrong

On the failures of Ukrainian state-building over three and a half decades:

The core problem was unwillingness to recognize Ukrainians as a state-forming people.
In fact, Ukrainians have deep roots in statehood — from Kyivan Rus, through the Grand
Duchy of Lithuania, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, and the Hetmanate — but the
loss of the Hetmanate's autonomy marked the end of Ukrainian participation in
governance until the Ukrainian People's Republic. That gap — a few centuries — bred
disrespect for institutions and a belief that institutions can be exploited for private
gain rather than built for the common good.

Ukraine remained essentially a renamed Soviet republic until 2014. The understanding
that the state exists for people — but that people must build the state — was absent.

A second failure: treating regional diversity as a weakness rather than an asset.
Portnikov draws comparisons to Italy (Naples, Lombardy, Sicily, Sardinia) and Germany
(Bavaria, Saxony, Baden-Württemberg) — countries that are stronger because of their
internal variety. Galicia, Bukovyna, Donbas, and Dnipro region are all different, and
that is a good thing that needs to be embraced rather than erased.

Third: the persistent fantasy of multi-vectorism — existing between worlds. Ukraine
is now irreversibly in the binary: Europe or Moscow.

## Institutions and the Rule of Law

On the state of Ukrainian institutions now, Portnikov points to a structural problem:
since 2019, power has been concentrated in a single pair of hands — not the head of
the president's office, but the president himself. This is a departure from the
parliamentary-presidential model that provided checks and balances from 2014 to 2019.

He compares this unfavorably to the US, where even a charismatic figure like Trump
is constrained by the Supreme Court. In Ukraine, the only real checks are NGOs
carrying protest signs and anti-corruption agencies — inadequate substitutes for
independent judiciary and a powerful parliament.

His prescription: independent courts, a genuinely functioning parliament, meaningful
presidential accountability, and a president whose constitutional role is limited to
security guarantor and foreign policy anchor.

## The New World Security Architecture

On whether 2022 marked the start of a new world order:

The shape of the new architecture will be determined by the outcome of current wars —
not just the Russia-Ukraine war, but others already underway or on the horizon. The
sad reality is that this architecture is being forged in war, not in peace.

Portnikov hopes the idea that only force produces results will eventually yield to
diplomacy and compromise. But he is clear: for Ukraine, there is only one place —
with the democratic world. Historical memory confirms this: the Bolsheviks destroyed
Ukrainian statehood; the Nazis denied Ukrainians the right to a state; the liberal
democratic world's victory in the Cold War in 1991 was the reason Ukraine has existed
as a state for 35 years. The answer to "with whom?" is written in Ukrainian history.

## China's Role and the Taiwan Angle

On China: Portnikov rejects the idea of China as a passive bystander. China openly
threatens Japan, nearly openly threatens the Philippines, and is fundamentally
interested in a bipolar world. Whether that world emerges depends on how current wars
end.

If Russia loses, the world returns to unipolarity and China will be cautious. If
Russia wins, multipolarity grows. China may be comfortable with the status quo; Ukraine
cannot be, because the status quo means the erasure of Ukrainian statehood.

On Iran: a US-led campaign against Iran is in part an attempt to cut into China's
sphere of influence. If the Iranian regime survives, it will be a triumph for Beijing,
not a blow.

On Taiwan: no one in China doubts that Taiwan is part of China, and most Taiwanese
also believe this — the dispute is about which China (democratic vs. authoritarian).
Outright independence for Taiwan is not a majority position on the island because
people understand it means direct war.

## EU Membership: Too Late for the EU We Wanted?

A student asks whether Ukraine has arrived too late for the EU that existed in 2014.

Portnikov's answer is foundational: the EU's real purpose is not prosperity — it is
the guarantee that European nations do not go to war with each other. In 1945, every
city on the continent lay in ruins. The EU is the mechanism that prevents a repeat.

Countries that are not EU members — Iceland, Norway, Switzerland — still live by EU
rules, participate in Schengen, and align with European trade regulations. The
alternatives for Ukraine are stark: either the EU or the Eurasian Union, which is
Russia's project to erase Ukraine as a people and a state. There is no third option
for a continental power without an island's luxury of detachment.

## Trump, Putin, and the "New Order"

The most politically charged section of the interview addresses why Trump treats Putin
differently from other leaders.

Portnikov's analysis is concise and withering: in Trump's worldview, there are three
serious men who run serious states and set the rules for everyone else — Trump,
Xi Jinping, and Putin. Everyone else, including Zelensky, Macron, and Merz, occupies
a subordinate tier. Macron has limited nuclear weapons; Merz has none. Zelensky can
be voted out; Putin is permanent.

Trump believed he could call Putin as an equal and offer him a rational deal: keep
the occupied territories, lift sanctions, normalize cooperation. Putin would say yes.
Instead, Putin — like the fairy-tale fisherman's wife who wanted to be Queen of the
Sea — wants everything. Trump cannot understand this, because it falls outside his
transactional worldview.

The result: Trump blames Ukraine and Europe for blocking his deal with Putin, even
though it is Putin who refuses rational terms. Europeans tell Trump he cannot make a
deal without them. Ukrainians tell him they will not accept conditions that undermine
their sovereignty. Trump fumes.

As for conspiracy theories — that Epstein and Maxwell were Soviet agents, that Trump
was filmed in Moscow hotels, that crypto flows to Trump — Portnikov dismisses them
not as false, but as irrelevant. Even if any were true, it would not change the
structural analysis above.

## Is Trumpism a Trend or a Person?

Portnikov is clear: MAGA is a social trend, not just a political figure. A portion of
American society genuinely believes the country went off track and that a golden past
is recoverable. It is not — because that past had different demographics and a
different economic structure. No amount of tech billions can buy a time machine.

This faction will exhaust itself eventually, but before it does, it will deploy maximum
effort to reverse time. The attempt is structurally doomed; the damage in the meantime
is real.

## Zelensky, Donbas, and the False Dilemma

A question: if Zelensky agrees to give up Donbas, will there be peace?

Portnikov calls this a self-hypnotic fiction. Putin has never actually said: "Give us
Donbas and we will stop." Putin's goal is the liquidation of Ukrainian statehood.
Territorial demands are instruments of destabilization, not terms of peace.

There is no genuine dilemma of sacrificing land for peace — that option does not exist
in reality. Every time Ukraine, Witkoff, Kushner, or Trump falls for this framing, it
destabilizes Ukrainian society without producing peace, because Putin does not want
rational peace.

## "Good Russians" and Collective Responsibility

A student asks why the West loves "good Russians," and whether good Russians are just
a democratic face for imperial preservation.

Portnikov rejects collective responsibility categorically: assigning collective guilt
always ends with killing children of hated peoples to prevent them from growing up.
That path leads to atrocity.

What he believes in instead is fellow-thinkers. He does not believe in "good Russians"
as a category, because many of those so labeled are liberal imperialists. But there
are individuals who genuinely support Ukraine, fight on Ukraine's side, and defend
Ukrainian positions — Gary Kasparov being his example.

Those who say Kasparov is not really Russian (Jewish and Armenian heritage) miss the
point: political nationhood versus ethnic nationhood. Kasparov defended Ukrainian
interests before many Ukrainians did.

Portnikov divides the world not into good and bad Russians, or good and bad Ukrainians,
but into those who recognize Ukraine's right to statehood and those who do not.

On Russian culture: not the culture itself, but its weaponizers. Gergiev and Netrebko
are not problems because they are Russian cultural figures; they are problems because
they are propagandists of war. At the same time, conflating dead Tchaikovsky with
living Netrebko, or conflating Netrebko with Irina Arkhipova (who defended Ukraine
and lost her Russian stage presence) is a failure of discrimination that serves no one.

## Message to the Young Generation

Portnikov closes with an anecdote from Berlin: a student from Enerhodar, almost
finished with school, asked whether there was any realistic path to restoring
Ukrainian control over the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant. Portnikov said no — and the
boy began to cry.

He went up to the boy afterward and told him: don't cry. What matters is that we
preserve Ukrainian statehood. If we do that, we preserve your home.

His message to every young Ukrainian: think of it this way — if we preserve Ukraine,
we preserve the future of every one of you. That is what must be fought for. Not
some abstract state, but the concrete reality that without Ukrainian statehood,
no Ukrainian is counted for anything — a lesson he draws from the history of his
own people, who had no state before 1948, and who discovered in the most brutal
circumstances what it means to exist without one.

The lecture ends with the host urging students to preserve and build Ukraine's future —
the practical task that connects individual professional development to civilizational
survival.
