## Can the Iranian Regime Be Toppled Without a Ground Operation?

The central question of the episode. Portnikov's answer: *almost certainly not.*

Air campaigns have achieved much: the Supreme Leader Khamenei eliminated, National Security
Council Secretary Larijani eliminated, ministers and IRGC commanders killed, missile sites and
possibly nuclear sites destroyed. But *regimes don't fall from rocket strikes alone.*

*Four conditions for regime collapse:*
- Popular revolution — requires a population stronger than the security forces
- Ground invasion — requires an invading army stronger than the defending one
- Military coup — requires the army being willing to defy the IRGC
- Elite conspiracy — requires elites ready to control security structures

None of these conditions is currently met. Of the four, *ground invasion is the most viable* —
but comes with enormous costs.

*The American political problem:*
- *65%* of Americans think Trump will order a ground operation
- Only *7%* support the idea
- Trump's approval rating has fallen to a historic low, driven by inflation fears linked to
  the Iran war
- Politico: "The most unpopular war in modern US history" — in a country that hasn't even
  suffered casualties yet

*Historical warnings:*
Vietnam: the US won every battle, but lost the war. Portnikov supported the war's goals but
notes that during Vietnam, the US effectively *lost the Cold War* — only saved by the Soviet
Union's planned economy and the Sino-Soviet split. If China had had a market economy then,
the US would have been a loser state.

Afghanistan: another "supported the goals, watched the incompetence." The defeat led directly
to Putin believing the US was weak — which led directly to the 2022 invasion of Ukraine.

*The key question:* How many more humiliating defeats can the US absorb and remain a strong
state? At some point, winning becomes mandatory.

## The Arms Queue Problem for Ukraine

The US is rushing emergency arms sales to Gulf states — bypassing Congress due to Iran threat:
- UAE: *$8.4 billion* package (the largest)
- Kuwait: equipment and munitions
- Saudi Arabia, Bahrain, Oman, Azerbaijan: all in line

Ukraine now competes with Qatar, Kuwait, Oman, Bahrain, UAE, Saudi Arabia, and Azerbaijan for
*the same* air defense missiles. All of these countries are simultaneously scrambling for
the same systems. The US military-industrial complex was already not running at full capacity
*before* the Iran war — it produces what generates adequate profit, not what's needed for a
global confrontation. The queue will be long, and Ukraine will not be first in it.

## NATO Fractures

The war exposed NATO's internal incoherence. Timeline:

*March 16:* France, Germany, UK refused to send warships to Ormuz. France: "We are not a
party to the conflict." Germany's Merz: "Action only possible after hostilities end."

*March 19:* Under Trump pressure, UK, France, Germany, Italy, Netherlands, Japan, Canada
signed a joint statement expressing readiness to contribute to Ormuz security — *but with no
plan, no timeline, no details.* Portnikov: "The statement will remain on paper."

*Trump's fundamental failure:* He launched this operation without consulting allies,
convinced he and Israel could handle it alone. When reality proved otherwise, he started
begging allies to join. But for allies to join properly, the US needs to *transfer NATO
command of the operation* and become a participant — not the unilateral commander demanding
help. Trump has no articulated goals: regime change? Oil control? Eliminate missiles? Seize
nuclear materials? Netanyahu's goal is clear. Trump's is not.

*Trump and NATO's future:*
Portnikov is direct: Trump would pull the US out of NATO if he had the congressional votes.
He doesn't — yet. *American democracy saves America from Trump.* But he can significantly
weaken US participation in the alliance. For Poland and Europe, this means one conclusion:
build European defense capability inside the EU, independently.

Poland's *SAFE program* (€44B defense industry credit from the European Commission) was vetoed
by President Nowrocki — a pro-Trump figure — to protect US arms sales. Polish FM Sikorski's
explanation: "We cannot buy everything from the United States." The veto is a direct example
of US pressure on European defense autonomy.

*Poland: 65.1%* of Poles oppose providing military support to the US in Ormuz.

## Orbán's Blockade: The Real Mechanics

Orbán is blocking Ukraine's €90 billion EU credit and EU membership path — demanding restoration
of Russian oil transit through the Druzhba pipeline.

Portnikov's reading: *Orbán does not want the pipeline fixed.* He needs it broken until the
April 12 Hungarian elections as a campaign tool — "Ukrainian threat" narrative. If anyone
repairs it, Russia will strike it again before the elections. It is a Russian political
technology operation, not a genuine energy dispute. Portnikov says he could personally identify
the Russian political technologist who designed this combination: "A very primitive combination."

*After April 12:* Orbán's calculus changes entirely. If he wins, he'll need EU structural
funds — which requires not burning all bridges. The post-election Orbán "will look nothing like
the current Orbán." Von der Leyen and European Council President Costa have both confirmed the
€90B credit *will be delivered* regardless: "A deal is a deal. Nobody can blackmail the
European Council."

*The harder scenario:* If the Middle East crisis continues and Iran's oil stays offline,
Russia may become the main energy supplier not just for Hungary and Slovakia, but for a broader
set of European states desperate for alternatives. All current assessments may be obsolete by
April.

## The Century at Risk

Portnikov closes with the large frame. The 21st century faces a fork:

*If democracies prevail:* The global confrontation continues but the West holds. Liberal
capitalism remains the dominant model. Technological progress benefits free societies.

*If democracies fail to consolidate:* A century of dictatorships — authoritarianism and chaos,
with the leaders of scientific and technological progress dedicating their achievements to
Xi Jinping and Putin or their successors. This is the actual stakes of what's happening now.

The US, despite everything, remains the essential counterweight. The question is not whether
it's imperfect — it is — but whether the alternative is better. It isn't.
