# How to Save Ukraine: Historical Memory and Geopolitics

This is a transcript-based synthesis of a public lecture and Q&A given by journalist and political
analyst Vitaliy Portnikov at an OpenCode.IF (@opencodeif) event on March 21, 2026 — the anniversary
of Taras Shevchenko's death. The talk covered historical memory, Ukrainian statehood, geopolitics,
the Holodomor, Polish-Ukrainian relations, the ongoing war, and the future of democracy.

---

## Historical Memory as a Geopolitical Tool

The moderator opened by noting that political leaders routinely invoke historical memory as a
political instrument. Putin surrounds himself with the ghosts of Catherine I and Peter the Great.
Trump won on "Make America Great Again." Is historical memory a genuine geopolitical force, or a
screen for policy?

Portnikov's answer: it is both, but one must distinguish shallow instrumentalization from deep
civilizational stakes. The enemy's primary calculation in February 2022 was that Ukrainians were
"only pretending to be Ukrainian" — that if Russian troops arrived in parade dress, people would
say, "We were waiting for you. Now we'll have cheap oil and cheap gas."

Four years of full-scale war have done the opposite. The sense of Ukrainian identity has intensified
not only in regions where Ukrainian culture was always strong, but also in regions that were at the
border of or inside the "Russian world." People who switched to Ukrainian in the first months of the
war and later returned to Russian still feel themselves to be Ukrainians. Portnikov calls that a
social victory.

The analogy he used: Soviet Jews sent to Israel who hadn't wanted to go were eventually forced by
war and terrorism to answer the question "What does it mean to be Jewish?" Being Jewish turned out
to mean not just holding a passport, but defending the country and preserving something larger than
economic interest. Ukrainians are now going through the same process.

## Ukraine as a State-Building Nation — Reclaiming a Stolen Narrative

Portnikov argued that the attempt to erase Ukrainian historical memory has always been central to
Russian aggression — not just since 2022, but as a constant. He noted that March 21 is the
anniversary of Shevchenko's death, and that Shevchenko addressed Ukrainians as a nation and a
self-standing European people at a time when there was no "Spring of Nations" inside the Russian
Empire.

Other Central European nations had state traditions to draw on:
- Poles divided between Russia, Germany, and Austria appealed to the Crown tradition.
- Lithuanians appealed to the Grand Duchy of Lithuania (hence Gediminas Avenue in Vilnius).
- Ukrainians could have said: "We are the nation of St. Volodymyr and Yaroslav the Wise." But
  Moscow had already claimed those as Russian forebears through the Romanov dynasty.

Shevchenko was left to build Ukrainian national identity starting from the Haidamaka uprisings —
constructing the image of Ukrainians as a people of rebels rather than state-builders. Portnikov
rejects this framing:

- Ukrainian state-building continuity runs from Kyivan Rus through Halych, the Grand Duchy of
  Lithuania, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Hetmanate, and the Zaporizhian Sich.
- The break lasted roughly 200 years — from the destruction of the Zaporizhian Sich to the
  proclamation of the Ukrainian People's Republic and the Western Ukrainian People's Republic.
- Ukraine has a thousand-year history of state-building; 200 years of interruption should not be
  a verdict on a nation that built institutions for millennia.

He also pointed out that Ukrainian pioneering in Canada is a peripheral note in Ukrainian national
memory, while Poles and French celebrate contributions to the founding of the United States as
central to their national narratives. Ukrainians fail to claim their own participation in building
the modern world.

The key message: Ukraine is not an accidental state that appeared 35 years ago, as Putin claims.
Ukrainian statehood is centuries old. That distinction must be internalized.

## The Holodomor: Why International Recognition Fell Short

The moderator asked why Yushchenko's efforts to raise the Holodomor to the level of national and
international memory were not fully successful — why does the debate continue?

Portnikov's assessment of Yushchenko: he was a brilliant economic manager and central banker, a
mediocre politician, but an outstanding statesman. He understood the politics of memory at a time
when almost no one else did. Before Yushchenko, the Holodomor was a marginal topic. The Party of
Regions voted against recognizing it as genocide — all but two deputies from Galicia — because they
feared offending Russia.

Why the Holodomor recognition remains contested:

- Russia insists the famine had no ethnic dimension — just a general Soviet famine.
- The counter-argument: why were only territories of Ukrainian ethnic settlement (Ukrainian SSR,
  Kuban, and parts of Kazakhstan with Ukrainian diaspora) guarded by the military to prevent
  escape? Those territories were execution zones.
- The Russian poet Osip Mandelstam, no politician, wrote from Voronezh in the 1930s about
  "the terrible shadows of Ukraine and Kuban" — he knew, even if official Russia claims not to.

The deeper problem: communities in eastern Ukraine occupied the homes of those killed by the
famine, or were children and grandchildren of those who enforced it. Reassessing that history meant
reassessing family identity. In Crimea, the same psychology operated: people who knew they lived in
houses taken from deported Crimean Tatars welcomed the Russians in 2014 in part to secure a
guarantee that those houses remained theirs.

Portnikov shared a personal account: his great-grandparents stayed in Kamianka on the Cherkasy
region during WWII despite warnings, because the Holodomor had taught them that Soviet propaganda
lied. They concluded that reports of Nazi atrocities against Jews must also be Soviet propaganda.
They were killed in the Holocaust. The Holodomor reached them in a different form, through
distorted information trust.

## The Kyivan Rus Question: Why Russia Needs Kyiv

A student in the audience (international relations, first year) asked: why do Russians insist on
claiming Kyivan Rus? Why don't they call themselves Muscovites or simply Russians?

Portnikov's answer:

- "Russians" is itself an invented term. In chronicles, these people were called Suzdalians or
  Muscovites — and they called themselves that too.
- The idea of "Rus" arrived in Moscow together with Ukrainian lands and with intellectuals from
  those lands who offered Muscovite monarchs the concept.
- Moscow's actual national history begins when Prince Andrei Bogolyubsky declared himself Grand
  Prince of Vladimir — a separate title from the Kyivan grand prince. That is, in today's terms,
  separatism from Kyiv.
- No one wants to trace their national history to an act of separatism. If you start from Kyiv,
  you start from Rurik, you encompass Novgorod, and you can explain why you destroyed it (because
  it was a foreign state that Muscovy genuinely subjected to genocide-scale depopulation and
  replacement).
- Ivan the Terrible saw himself as a Rurikid heir — hence the curse of the Pereyaslav agreement
  for both nations.

The Muscovite imperial model:
- Moscow built its principality by annexing rivals with the support of the Golden Horde.
- Ryazan: population expelled or killed, replaced by Muscovites. Tver: burned, population
  expelled. All current Russian regional centers were once independent states whose populations
  were replaced.
- Novgorod was ethnically cleansed to such a degree that its current residents walk among palaces
  of Yaroslav and a Sophia Cathedral but have no living connection to their history.

The conclusion: Russia cannot stop being an empire because it has never once built itself as a
national state. Its entire identity requires imperial legitimacy, which requires Kyiv.

## Victory, Defeat, and the War's Outcome

The moderator asked: what will the memory of this war look like?

Portnikov's framework:

- Russia's goal is the liquidation of Ukrainian statehood and the Ukrainian nation as an
  independent entity. This is the Kremlin's goal, the FSB's goal, and it is supported by the
  majority of Russians.
- Russia cannot achieve this goal: in February 2022, it planned to be in Kyiv; in March 2026, it
  is still near Pokrovsk.
- If Russia fails to liquidate Ukrainian statehood, it has suffered the greatest defeat in its
  imperial history — because without Ukraine, there is no restored Russian Empire. Belarus,
  Tatarstan, Chechnya do not make Russia a hegemon. Only with Ukrainian territory does Russia
  become a political hegemon of Europe.

On ceasefire and territorial loss:

- Ukraine may face a temporary stop on certain lines that do not allow full control of
  internationally recognized territory. That is not a territorial defeat in the permanent sense.
- Ukraine cannot lose its territories permanently — it can only lose the state.
- The task: preserve the state, preserve the Ukrainian people on Ukrainian land. Even a smaller
  population than before the war can rebuild the state, just as Israel, with 6-7 million Jews
  inside the country (and 6-7 million more living better in the US), maintains its state in its
  territory.

The enemy's information strategy: Russian media, influencers, and certain American right-wing
channels will work to convince Ukrainians they have suffered territorial, demographic, and economic
catastrophe. If Ukrainians believe it, they lose. Victory is defined as: the Ukrainian state
survives, the Ukrainian nation survives, the Ukrainian language survives, the Ukrainian path to
Europe survives.

## Polish-Ukrainian Relations and the Volhynia Tragedy

The moderator raised the issue of Polish-Ukrainian historical memory, particularly the Volhynia
tragedy and its recurring political use in Polish domestic politics.

Portnikov's assessment:

- The underlying dynamic: whenever Ukrainians and Poles quarrel, Russians come and take everything.
  This historical pattern is observable and its consequences well-documented.
- He recalled buying a beautifully illustrated book about Galicia in Warsaw — meticulously
  researched, covering the Kingdom of Galicia-Lodomeria — and finding Ukrainians and Jews mentioned
  in a three-page section near the end. Yet Ukrainians were a majority of the population in most
  of Eastern Galicia, and Jews were a major cultural and national substrate.
- Poland went the ethnic route in building its postwar identity: Operation Vistula (deportation of
  Ukrainians from southeastern Poland), the final expulsion of Jews in the 1960s. These were acts
  of communist government, but the resulting ethnic Poland made it harder to transition to a
  political nation model.
- Ukraine, by contrast, transitioned relatively quickly from an ethnic to a political nation
  project. The Maidans and the war accelerated this: people of multiple ethnic backgrounds defend
  Ukraine. The political nation model is now the survival model.

On dialogue:

- The Polish episcopate found the moral strength to say to German bishops: "We forgive and ask
  forgiveness" — at a time when Poles had every reason not to apologize to Germany.
- Why can't that formula apply to Ukrainian-Polish relations? Both sides carry responsibility for
  Volhynia, and both need to move through acknowledgment toward reconciliation.
- The danger: political actors in Poland who use Ukrainian-phobia as a domestic tool. The example
  he cited — Grzegorz Braun, an antisemitic and Ukrainophobic Polish politician who went to pay
  condolences on Khamenei's death — illustrates the absurdity: Poland's security depends on its
  alliance with the US and unified Europe. Undermining that alliance to exploit national grievance
  is sawing off the branch the nation sits on.

The formulation he endorsed (quoting Adam Mickiewicz): "For your freedom and ours" — that should
be the operating principle of Polish-Ukrainian relations.

## The Question of the Russian Claim to Kyivan Rus (Deeper Cut)

In response to the historian Andriy's question about whether the Kingdom of Rus (the Galician-
Volhynian principality under Danylo Halytskyi) could serve as the new spine of Ukrainian historical
memory:

Portnikov argued against selecting any single episode:

- Ukrainian statehood should be understood as a continuous flow, not a collection of highlighted
  episodes.
- Yaroslav the Wise was voted the greatest Ukrainian in the television poll of notable Ukrainians
  (edging out Bandera, to the relief of then-Minister Tabachnyk). That result matters: Yaroslav
  built Kyiv, established Sophia Cathedral, created its library. Renouncing him as a "Russian"
  figure because his era is called "Rus" is historically incoherent.
- Danylo Romanovich (Danylo Halytskyi) is an outstanding figure in 13th-century European history,
  but he is one moment in the flow, not the whole story.
- The Galician-Volhynian Rus is best understood as proof of Ukraine's European orientation.

## Crimea, Occupied Territories, and Generational Memory

Two audience questions addressed Crimea and the southern territories occupied since 2022.

Portnikov's response on Crimea:

- Yes, a generation has grown up in Crimea that does not know Ukrainian Crimea. But that is the
  first generation that also does not know that "Ukraine is Russian." The second fact matters
  more.
- Ukrainians who grew up after 2014 — especially after 2022 — have no connection to Russia
  whatsoever. Young people from Melitopol who once photographed themselves on Moscow's Red Square
  for Instagram will never do so again. That is a civilizational shift.

On the occupied southern territories:

- The Baltic states were occupied for 50 years; Latvians, Lithuanians, and Estonians were not
  "lost." Soviet nationalities policy did allow national schools and culture there — different from
  the current Russian approach.
- The Finnish precedent: Finland ceded territory but not a single person. Every Finnish resident
  of Vyborg (now Vyborg in Leningrad Oblast) left — twice, after the Winter War and the
  Continuation War. Zero people remained on what became Soviet/Russian Karelia.
- If people remain in territories formally incorporated as subjects of the Russian Federation,
  their children will grow up as ordinary Russian Federation citizens. That is not a moral
  judgment, it is a demographic reality. Parents who do not want that outcome must bring their
  children to Ukraine — and those families will not return to Melitopol or Berdyansk.
- Crimean Tatars are a partial exception because Crimea is their only homeland; they have a
  national substrate that has no other place.

## Democracy, Authoritarianism, and Ukraine's Survival

An audience member named Oles asked: what future is there for democracy if conflicts keep
expanding?

Portnikov's argument:

- Democracy is not just an ideal; it is Ukraine's survival mechanism. When you remove checks and
  balances, you make errors — as the current US approach to the Iran conflict illustrates.
- The 20th century provides two data points:
  - The left-radical Bolshevik regime liquidated Ukrainian statehood and replaced it with a
    surrogate.
  - The right-radical Nazi regime denied Ukrainians any statehood even when Ukrainian politicians
    were willing to collaborate.
  - The democratic world won the Cold War, and the Soviet model collapsed. Ukraine has existed
    for 35 years within the democratic world.
- Every time the democratic world weakens or stops fighting for democracy, threats to Ukrainian
  statehood emerge immediately. The moment the US began drifting toward authoritarianism, support
  for Ukraine changed.
- His conclusion: whether you like democracy or not — to use Putin's phrase — you will have to
  accept it, because for Ukraine, democracy is not optional. There was no Ukraine under Stalin,
  no Ukraine under Hitler. Ukraine exists only when democracies prevail.

## Ukrainian Identity: Love vs. Protest

A final question addressed people who switched to Ukrainian after February 2022 and later switched
back to Russian, even though they now identify as Ukrainian.

Portnikov's distinction:

- He does not believe in identity built on protest. People switched as an act of resistance to
  Russian aggression. Over years, the resistance state of mind fades even as the war continues.
  People return to the language that is simply more comfortable.
- Identity built on love and self-awareness is durable; identity built on protest is not.
- His own case: he began speaking Ukrainian around age 6-7, despite grandparents who had been
  Russified by Soviet Kyiv, and despite first words heard in Yiddish and early family conversations
  in Russian. He speaks and writes Ukrainian not as protest but because he loves the language and
  finds it liberating.
- He argued that Ukrainian is exceptionally suited to poetry and literary journalism — the
  language creates conditions for poetic expression that Russian and Polish do not match.
- The task for Ukraine: build Ukrainian identity through love for the Ukrainian state and the
  Ukrainian language, not through opposition to something else.

## Memory Policy and the Role of the State

A question from the audience asked how to convert cultural and educational memory work into a
political instrument — referencing thinkers like Vyacheslav Lypynsky, who argued that cultural
work is not the same as politics.

Portnikov's two-word answer: state policy.

- When Ukraine introduced radio broadcast quotas for Ukrainian-language content, Ukrainian radio
  stopped sounding like a provincial station from Bryansk. Before that, it was Russian-language
  radio with bad Russian songs.
- Ukrainian cinema emerged as a genuine cultural force — films filling theaters even during the
  war — because of state support.
- Ukrainian theater found its audience through a combination of state policy and social development.
  Ten years ago, before the 2013-14 Maidan, Ukrainian pop music and Ukrainian film were considered
  marginal. Even Kvartal 95, a beloved comedy troupe, was making content for the Russian market.
- Without state policy, cultural memory work cannot scale. That means building a state that
  understands the importance of such policy, and voting for politicians who share that
  understanding.

## Closing: What Does It Mean to Save Ukraine

Portnikov closed by returning to the central question of the event: how to save Ukraine.

His answer was not a military strategy or a diplomatic formula. It was a statement of what
survival means:

- If Ukrainians remain on Ukrainian land.
- If the Ukrainian state persists.
- If the Ukrainian language is spoken.
- If Ukrainians remember Shevchenko and the defenders of Ukraine rather than Putin or Pushkin.
- If figures from the national liberation movement — whatever their political views — are
  remembered as people who wanted Ukraine to be a state.

Then Ukraine has won the war. However it ends, whenever it ends, on whatever territorial lines.

He quoted the line attributed to Polish poet Wieniawski, which he had once asked Ivan Malkovych to
translate: a poet asks a young woman what Poland is. She places her hand where a necklace would
rest on her left side and says, "There is something beating there." That, said Portnikov, is what
Ukraine is too. Not romanticism — but the feeling that allows people to nurture Ukraine and defend
it. If a person feels the country as a heartbeat, they understand why it must exist.
