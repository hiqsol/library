# Strategic Oil Reserves, Sanctions, and the Economics of Russia's War

## Source and scope

This article reconstructs the argument made by Vitaly Portnikov in a 16:22 Espreso
interview hosted by Andriy Saichuk. The video was published on September 19, 2026,
at 19:00 in the system timezone. It is based only on the supplied Ukrainian
automatic-caption transcript and video description.

The captions contain evident recognition and transcription errors. Ambiguous wording,
names, and figures therefore should not be treated as a verified verbatim record.
Political, economic, causal, predictive, military, and numerical claims below are
attributed to Portnikov or, where indicated, to the interviewer.

## The central distinction: a sanction weapon is not its use

The interviewer presents a newly signed US law as the once-unthinkable enactment of
the so-called "hellish sanctions" associated with the late Republican senator Lindsey
Graham and Democratic senator Richard Blumenthal. He says active buyers of Russian
oil could face a 100% tariff and that banks, defense, energy, the shadow fleet, and what
he calls the Kremlin's principal cashbox are under pressure.

Portnikov's central answer is that the law changes nothing in practice unless President
Donald Trump chooses to use it. In his account, Congress gives the president authority,
not an obligation, to impose tariffs of up to 100% on products from the five largest
countries buying Russian energy by volume. Trump may impose them or may not.

Portnikov contrasts that discretion with an earlier Graham-Blumenthal proposal. Under
that proposal, the US president would demand that the Russian president halt fire on
the Russian-Ukrainian front and establish peace within either 15 or 30 days; Portnikov
explicitly says he no longer remembers which version contained which deadline. A
refusal would have obliged the US president to impose 500% tariffs on Russian products
and on products from countries buying Russian oil. The automatic caption says
"Russian NATO" at this point, an evident error; the surrounding passage concerns oil.

According to Portnikov, Graham spent a year and a half coordinating the bill with Trump
and died when it was close to, but had not completed, that process. Portnikov says the
result replaced a mandatory 500% instrument with an optional tariff of up to 100%.
He later compares the change to replacing a real weapon with a plastic model so as not
to quarrel with Trump. The interviewer had earlier called Graham deceased and added,
of Blumenthal, "God grant him health."

## Why congressional authorization matters

Portnikov says a US president cannot impose tariffs on other countries' products
without congressional consent. He says the US Supreme Court therefore barred Trump's
worldwide tariffs and that Trump lost his tariff war in part because of his own Supreme
Court's ruling. This law, in Portnikov's reading, supplies authority only against the
top five buyers of Russian energy.

He says many Democrats withheld support because they feared Trump could manipulate
that authority to continue his trade wars. The narrow grant nevertheless matters:
Portnikov says no Supreme Court could now cancel tariffs imposed within it.

Trump's manner of signing contributes to Portnikov's skepticism. Portnikov contrasts
the triumphant, televised White House Rose Garden signing of Trump's broad tariff law,
complete with a display board, with the dry White House notice announcing this law.
He says Trump held no ceremony, made no statement, and did not stress the law's
importance at a press encounter immediately before signing. Portnikov notes that Trump
had nevertheless insisted on adding sanctions against Iran.

## China and the limits of tariff coercion

Portnikov identifies the People's Republic of China and India as the significant buyers
among the five. He says volumes taken by the other members of the five are small and
that some, such as Turkey, are allies of the United States and Ukraine.

For China, Portnikov doubts that Trump will risk a 100% tariff. He says Trump already
tried tariffs, discovered that the US economy and its strategic sectors depend heavily
on China, and learned that any tariff on Chinese goods is a shot in America's own foot.
In Portnikov's assessment, Trump lost that tariff war and will not start it again.

The interviewer notes that Xi Jinping was expected to visit America for three days the
following week to meet Trump. He suggests the law might provide leverage, adding that
their sanctions war was postponed until, as he recalls with uncertainty, November 10.
Portnikov agrees only to the narrower point: Xi would know Trump could impose tariffs
on Chinese products at any time under the law, without the Supreme Court undoing them.
Portnikov calls that unpleasant news for Xi, not proof that Trump will act.

## Why India may keep buying Russian oil

Portnikov says India is more sensitive to tariffs than China, but Indian officials had
already said they would continue buying Russian oil even if tariffs were imposed. He
describes two successive reasons for India's purchases.

First, Russian crude became a premium commercial opportunity after Russia's full-scale
war against Ukraine began. Portnikov says India bought the oil in rupees, with it still
unclear how those rupees were transferred to the Russians, refined it into petroleum
products, and sold those products on the world market for dollars. Before the full-scale
war, he says, India did not buy Russian oil at all because it would have been expensive
and would not have produced a large profit.

Second, Portnikov says the situation has changed fundamentally because oil routes
through the Strait of Hormuz are blockaded and a similar blockade is possible in the
Bab el-Mandeb Strait. In this setting, he argues, Russian oil is no longer merely a
profitable feedstock. India needs it to fill gaps in its refining complex as crude grows
scarce and strategic reserves diminish.

He calls India a country with a population in the billions and says it is trying to
prevent domestic disruption. Even if the United States imposed tariffs of up to 100%,
Portnikov says it is unclear whether India would gain more by ending Russian purchases
or by accepting the tariffs, reducing trade with the United States, and retaining oil
supply. Without oil, he warns, unpredictable pressures could arise in India around
social standards and fuel prices. He adds that people there may react differently than
people in the United States.

The interviewer characterizes diversification of risky investments as a rule of
business. That observation supports Portnikov's later argument that refiners may cut
Russian exposure without waiting for formal penalties.

## Reserves, prices, refineries, and export constraints

Portnikov separates the immediate disruption from the larger danger. He says the true
crisis or catastrophe will begin not merely while the Strait of Hormuz remains blocked,
but when countries exhaust strategic oil reserves that currently offset shortages.
The world has accumulated a great deal of oil, he says, so the breaking point is not
instantaneous. Yet he argues that continued failure to resolve Hormuz will eventually
bring the world to it.

He also says a Saudi Arabian overland pipeline is already being blocked, bringing that
point of no return closer. In that scenario he predicts an energy crisis of unheard-of
scale. He gives no reserve totals, depletion rates, price path, or date for exhaustion.
His timing is conditional: it depends on how long the route constraints persist and
whether the Hormuz problem is resolved.

The price mechanism appears through fuel as well as crude. Portnikov says Trump blames
Ukrainian strikes on Russia for increasing the price of diesel. Portnikov asks how,
under that reasoning, Washington can reproach India or China for protecting supply.
The exchange does not quantify any diesel-price increase or demonstrate the effect.

Portnikov's India example also shows how refinery and export economics intersect.
Russian crude can keep Indian refineries supplied; refined products can then be sold
on world markets for dollars. Tariffs on Indian exports to the United States could make
that trade costlier, but Portnikov says the alternative may be worse if India cannot
source enough crude. The video supplies no refinery capacity, export-volume, discount,
revenue, or margin data.

## Russian revenue and the war

The interviewer links oil buying, the shadow fleet, and the Kremlin's principal source
of funds, implying that restricting sales would constrain Russian revenue. Portnikov
does not provide a calculation of Russian oil income, fiscal dependence, or the amount
by which tariffs would reduce either. His argument is instead that China and India have
strong reasons to keep buying and that Trump has strong reasons not to punish them.

Portnikov therefore rejects expectations that the law itself will change the war. He
calls it a decorative pre-election story and says it will have no effect on the
Russian-Ukrainian war. Calling it "hellish sanctions" does not alter that assessment.
He also says Russia has weathered the existing sanctions, so making those sanctions
harder to lift may not itself determine how the war ends.

His policy comparison is with direct support for Ukraine. Portnikov says a separate
Ukraine Support Act, already passed by the House of Representatives, would provide
financial infusions into Ukraine's budget, multibillion-dollar military assistance,
and new orders for US defense companies, continuing what existed under President Joe
Biden. He says it is sitting in the Senate without even being scheduled for a vote.

Portnikov believes it would have a Senate majority, but says Senator John Thune, the
leader of the Republican majority, will not put it to a vote and cannot be bypassed as
he could be in the House. Portnikov is also confident Trump would never sign that bill.
By contrast, he says the tariff law creates no problems for Trump. For Portnikov, praise
for an optional tariff tool can obscure the absence of aid more likely to affect the
war directly.

## Two forms of real, but delayed, effect

Portnikov identifies a first positive result unrelated to immediate oil coercion. He
says no US president may now repeal, without Congress's consent, sanctions imposed on
Russia by the administrations of Barack Obama, Donald Trump, and Joe Biden. Even if
Trump wanted to ease them in exchange for concessions from Vladimir Putin in the war,
Congress would have to decide that the concessions were sufficient to regard the war
as over.

In Portnikov's formulation, the law has removed the Trump administration's freedom to
maneuver over sanctions. He predicts those sanctions will probably remain indefinitely
and that the law has put a barrier in Russian-American economic relations for decades.

He compares this endurance with the Jackson-Vanik amendment. Portnikov says it was
introduced against the Soviet Union in the 1970s in protest at persecution of religious
minorities. He notes that discussion usually centers on Jews, but says Protestants in
the Soviet Union were also affected. The amendment denied the Soviet Union access to
technology products and, in his interpretation, fortunately became a major cause of
its decline and collapse.

The Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, Portnikov says, but the amendment was not then
repealed. It was lifted only decades later for former Soviet republics that had become
independent states. Until then, he says, those countries, including Ukraine, lacked
access to certain categories of high-technology US products.

Portnikov predicts a similar lag: the Russian-Ukrainian war will end, while limits on
Russian economic activity with the United States will last another 20 years. He says
that may not shape the war's conclusion, but will degrade Russian-American economic
relations regardless of what happens in Russia after Putin.

The second possible benefit is what Portnikov calls delayed punishment. Executives at
a large Indian or Chinese refinery may conclude, even after receiving a serious
40-page analysis predicting that Trump will not impose 100% tariffs, that they cannot
ignore the possibility that he might. Portnikov dramatizes the downside as figures so
bad that a chief engineer would spare a director with a weak heart from hearing them.
A firm facing possible bankruptcy could reduce Russian crude volumes as a precaution.

Thus, Portnikov says the law may have effects without any sanction or tariff actually
being imposed. Businesspeople manage risk differently from commentators, and Trump's
unpredictability can itself alter procurement. This is a scenario, not evidence in the
video that Indian or Chinese refiners have already reduced Russian purchases.

## What the title and interview do not establish

The Ukrainian title says the "real catastrophe" will begin when the world exhausts its
strategic oil reserve. Portnikov does make that conditional warning, but the title is
more sensational and more definite than the supporting discussion. He does not show
that a single global reserve exists, that world reserves are about to be exhausted, or
when any national reserve would run out. He supplies no inventory series, demand model,
shipping-flow evidence, price forecast, or independent source for the claimed Hormuz,
Bab el-Mandeb, or Saudi-pipeline constraints.

The video also does not establish that the optional tariffs will be imposed; that China
or India will stop buying Russian oil; that Russian revenue has already fallen because
of this law; that refiners have reduced Russian inputs; or that the law has changed the
military course of the war. It does not quantify Russia's oil revenue, India's refinery
shortfall, reserve depletion, global crude supply, export disruption, or diesel prices.

Portnikov nevertheless sees limited value in passage. If asked whether voting for the
law was better than not voting for it, he says voting for it was better. His final
caveat is that it should not generate excessive expectations: immediate coercion is
uncertain, while institutional lock-in and business risk may matter over a much longer
horizon.
