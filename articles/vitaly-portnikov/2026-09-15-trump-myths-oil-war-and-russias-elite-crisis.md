# Trump's Political Myth, Russia's Oil Crisis, and Elite Impunity

## About the interview

This article explains a 36-minute Russian-language interview with journalist and political
commentator Vitaly Portnikov on *I Gryanul Grem*. It was published on September 15, 2026,
and speaks from that political context. The source is an automatic caption transcript, so
some names and phrases are uncertain or garbled. Claims below are attributed to the host or
Portnikov rather than independently verified.

## An energy ceasefire as an electoral narrative

The host opened with Donald Trump's statement that Russia and Ukraine had agreed not to
strike energy infrastructure, while noting that no confirmation was available. Portnikov
read the statement primarily as campaign messaging. In his account, Trump's recent approach
to both the Russia-Ukraine war and the war with Iran was being shaped by the US midterm
elections.

Portnikov argued that Trump needed to redirect blame for higher fuel prices. He cited talk of
gasoline at $6 per gallon and US articles asking how stations would display a possible $10
price when some signs lacked room for that many digits. He expected pump prices to influence
the congressional vote and described the period through November 3 as politically dangerous
for Trump.

The central dispute was causation. Portnikov said Trump and officials identified in the
captions as Treasury Secretary Bessent and Energy Secretary Wright blamed Ukrainian attacks
on Russian energy facilities for removing Russian diesel from world markets. Portnikov did
not deny that the attacks mattered: Russia had exported diesel, sometimes through channels
that he described as evading US and European sanctions, and was now buying diesel instead.

He nevertheless called the explanation misleading. In his account, the lost diesel supply
from Persian Gulf states was at least twice the Russian-market loss. He connected the larger
shortfall to Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz and raised the possibility of disruption
at Bab el-Mandeb. Even restored Russian exports, he argued, would not close the resulting gap.

On this reading, blaming the Russia-Ukraine war lets Trump tell voters that he is pressing
Volodymyr Zelenskyy to stop strikes that allegedly hurt US interests. Ukraine could hardly
accept a one-sided halt while Russia continued attacking it, Portnikov said. A genuine deal
would therefore have to be reciprocal. He forecast no near-term ceasefire because Vladimir
Putin might worry about refinery damage but valued continuing the war more.

The host asked whether an unfulfilled declaration would hurt Trump. Portnikov answered that
Trump could seek another culprit, including accusing Ukraine of ignoring requests or breaking
an agreement. He described Trump as a politically "wounded animal" facing adverse polling and
possible Democratic investigations after the midterms, while conceding that no one knew how
the contest would end.

Pressure on Ukraine also did not fit the older Republican model associated with Ronald
Reagan's confrontation with the Soviet Union. Portnikov said Trump's core electorate was MAGA,
not the traditional Republican coalition. It included both Russia hawks and people who saw no
special problem in relations with Moscow. In his interpretation, Trump could still present
himself as pressuring Russia while insisting that Ukraine account for American economic needs.

## The wedding-funding allegation and what Portnikov inferred

The next segment concerned a recently published investigation. The host said it reported that
Umar Kremlev, described as a Kremlin-linked Russian oligarch, helped pay for Donald Trump Jr.'s
wedding. The captions call Kremlev a pseudonym and may render some proper names incorrectly.
Neither the investigation nor the alleged payments were independently assessed in the
interview transcript.

Portnikov characterized Kremlev as a controversial betting-industry businessman with a
criminal reputation and close ties to Putin. He speculated that Kremlev may manage money
belonging to the people who enabled his rise, possibly including Putin. Those are Portnikov's
interpretations, not established findings in the transcript.

The discussion said a group of Kremlin-linked Russian businesspeople attended the private
event. The hosts also said they spoke Russian there. Portnikov dismissed the language itself
as unremarkable. What mattered to him was the reported acceptance of money and access while
the United States and Russia were in economic and political conflict.

According to the discussion, the Trump family did not primarily deny the event but objected
that journalists were intruding into private life. Portnikov interpreted that response as
showing that the family may not view such personal and financial links as politically or
morally improper. He suggested that power can create an expectation of impunity: people near
it stop noticing how their conduct appears and assume that outsiders will not investigate.

The host emphasized the apparently modest price of access. The reported contribution covered
fireworks and something else, totaling several hundred thousand dollars. That would be small
relative to the Trump family's wealth, yet the host found the access striking. Portnikov said
an expensive gift can function as an "entry ticket," but left open who wanted cooperation
more: the giver or the recipient.

Portnikov also noted that Donald Trump did not attend. He speculated that Trump may have
understood the potential fallout and stayed away, but offered no evidence for that inference.
He further asked whether Donald Trump Jr.'s anti-Ukraine statements represented conviction or
financial interest. He did not claim the reported wedding payment settled that question.

The episode returned the conversation to the midterms. Portnikov recalled House Speaker Mike
Johnson warning colleagues that a Democratic victory could turn Congress into a chamber of
investigations. Journalists had exposed only relatively small reported sums, he said; Congress
might seek other payments or cooperation schemes if it gained access to more information.
That was a possibility he raised, not evidence that larger schemes existed.

## Portnikov's model of Putin's business politics

The host asked whether Putin's KGB background helped him manipulate Trump. Portnikov allowed
that intelligence experience might matter, but rejected the image of Putin as an unusually
subtle master spy. He instead described Putin, polemically, as a product of corrupt
St. Petersburg networks whose recurring method was to draw Western businesspeople into deals.

Portnikov illustrated the pattern with a Soviet television report from August 23 or 24, 1991.
It showed St. Petersburg mayor Anatoly Sobchak meeting Western investors, with Putin seated
beside him. Portnikov said viewers had seen the footage then without recognizing Putin. About
35 years later, he argued, the role remained the same, except intermediaries now did the work.

He compared Kremlev's contact with Trump-linked figures to Kirill Dmitriev's outreach to Steve
Witkoff and Jared Kushner. In Portnikov's account, the offer is simple: enter opaque or gray
business arrangements, make money, and stop focusing on Ukraine. He speculated that gambling,
crypto, offshore structures, or other hidden ventures could promise large gains without the
need formally to lift sanctions.

Portnikov suggested that Russian interlocutors might tell the administration to wait because
Ukraine would capitulate in two or three months. He attributed interest in such deals to
possible secrecy, supposedly reliable income, and ordinary greed. The transcript supplies no
documentary proof of these broader schemes; this segment is Portnikov's theory of how the
network operates.

## Ukraine's prosecutor-general and the role of NABU

The interview then shifted to reports involving Ukraine's prosecutor-general, named in the
captions as Kravchenko. Portnikov treated the episode as a result of seven years of
personalized and deprofessionalized appointments. He argued that Parliament had merely
rubber-stamped choices made by one person rather than independently vetting candidates.

Portnikov claimed that every prosecutor-general appointed by Zelenskyy was then abroad. He
said two served as ambassadors and two did not, while the only recent prosecutor-general still
in Ukraine was Yuriy Lutsenko, the final holder of the office under Petro Poroshenko. These
status claims are presented as his statements and were not checked within the interview.

He further said Kravchenko had earlier sought leadership of the National Anti-Corruption
Bureau of Ukraine, or NABU, but failed an integrity review. Portnikov criticized the later
decision to make him prosecutor-general. He also alleged that the prosecutor-general charged
the head of NABU and left the country one day before Parliament was due to vote on his
dismissal, while promising to return.

For Portnikov, NABU remained a crucial check precisely because it sat outside the presidential
law-enforcement chain. He said it was created under Poroshenko at the insistence of Western
partners. In 2025, he recalled, the government tried to place it under the prosecutor-general,
and public protests plus a firm line from Group of Seven ambassadors helped defend the
anti-corruption bodies' independence.

Portnikov said he would prefer an ordinary justice system in which the prosecutor-general's
office could investigate senior officials reliably. The reported events made him doubt that
ideal was currently realistic. Under personalized rule, he concluded, externally supported
anti-corruption institutions may be the only effective barrier to entrenched corruption.
This is his institutional judgment, not a legal finding about any named official.

## Trains, Western visitors, and the risk beyond Ukraine

The host next referred to a Russian strike on a train said to be carrying Boris Johnson and
European national-security advisers. He asked whether deliberately targeting Western
politicians marked a new threshold. Portnikov was not sure Russian forces knew which train
they were attacking. His more basic point was that Russia was already striking trains carrying
civilians without regard to who was aboard.

Portnikov speculated that visits by foreign officials may have become less secret because
Western governments had come to expect that Russia would avoid such attacks. The strike could
force a return to concealed travel arrangements. He also paraphrased Kremlin spokesman Dmitry
Peskov as saying Russia struck wherever it could and that those affected were to blame.

The broader purpose of Russian conduct, Portnikov argued, was to persuade the West that Moscow
was willing to fight it too. The host asked whether crossing that psychological line could
backfire once the West judged war unavoidable. Portnikov warned against assuming that Russia
would gain nothing from escalation. Warfare changes technologically, and Moscow may believe it
has adapted in ways Western states have not.

The discussion cited Poland's order to shoot down objects entering its territory and a Shahed
drone reportedly downed over Lithuania "yesterday." Portnikov said such drones had crossed EU
airspace for roughly two to two and a half years before this first Lithuanian interception.
The automatic captions make the exact elapsed-time wording uncertain.

He warned that attacks on Ukrainian trains today could become attacks beyond Ukraine tomorrow,
including longer-range or jet-powered Shahed-type drones. In his view, Western assumptions
had already shifted from "Russia will not attack" to questions of interception and readiness.
NATO-area spillover was therefore no longer an abstract issue, though the form and timing of
any future escalation remained unknowable.

## Artificial intelligence and the next arms race

The closing exchange asked whether a predicted era of superintelligence could change the war.
Portnikov answered that any major change in the defense-industrial base would affect warfare,
and AI was no exception. What could not yet be known was the nature of that effect or which
side would incorporate AI into military decision-making first.

The host compared US-China competition in AI to the Cold War space race and summarized
Trump's argument that the United States could not pause because China would advance instead.
Portnikov agreed with the strategic concern: if authoritarian states gained a technological
edge over democracies, democratic societies would need extraordinary effort merely to
preserve themselves.

They ended on information integrity as well as weapons. The host worried that AI-generated
presenters could deliver Chinese Communist Party narratives, as he said already happened in
political TikTok content. Portnikov said experts he had consulted believed strong AI
imitations were already close to indistinguishable from people on a screen. Their final joke
about recognizing one another rather than a disguised "Comrade Major" underscored a serious
uncertainty: future conflict will involve not only physical strikes, but trust in who is
speaking and whose narrative an apparently human voice serves.
