# Nuclear Facilities Under Attack

## The Strike on Dimona and the Exchange of Blows on Nuclear Sites

Just before this broadcast began, news broke of an Iranian missile strike on the city
of Dimona in Israel, home to Israel's famous nuclear reactor. Iranian forces claim they
targeted residential neighborhoods to neutralize workers at this critical defense
facility. Israel has never officially acknowledged possessing nuclear weapons, but the
symbolic significance of the strike cannot be overlooked.

Iran frames the Dimona attack as a response to Israel's second strike on the nuclear
facility at Natanz, where uranium stockpiles essential to a potential Iranian nuclear
weapon may be stored. The exchange of strikes on nuclear facilities creates new risks
for the region — and potentially for the entire world. If such attacks continue, a
missile could eventually strike an active nuclear reactor, causing catastrophic
radioactive consequences.

During the same broadcast, a second strike was confirmed: an Iranian missile hit the
island of Diego Garcia in the Indian Ocean. Israel's military command acknowledged this
incident. The missile traveled approximately 4,000 kilometers. This range means that
all of Europe now lies within the potential reach of the Islamic Republic's arsenal.
Previously it was thought that Iranian missiles could reach Ukraine and Poland. Now it
is absolutely clear that Iran can strike anywhere in Europe.

## Iran's Revolutionary Ambitions and Strategic Logic

Iran is a revolutionary state. Since 1979, its clerical rulers have sought to spread
Islamic theocratic governance across the broader Middle East, beginning with the
destruction of Israel as a non-Muslim state to generate momentum for that wider
revolution. Iranian law — passed by the parliament — explicitly calls for the
destruction of Israel.

Iran built what it called the "Axis of Resistance": Palestinian armed groups in Gaza,
Hezbollah in Lebanon, militias in Iraq and Yemen, all coordinated through the Islamic
Revolutionary Guard Corps and its elite Quds Force. The goal was to use these forces
in concert to deal Israel a fatal blow, at which point the US would be unable to
intervene — hence Iran's drive to acquire nuclear weapons as a deterrent.

## Lessons from Russia: Nuclear Deterrence in Action

Iran's nuclear acceleration in recent years was directly accelerated by what Tehran
observed during the Russia-Ukraine war. Russia invaded a sovereign state, annexed its
territory, carried out mass atrocities against civilians — and the Western response was
limited to hand-wringing about what can be done against a nuclear power. The lesson
for Iran was stark: a nuclear weapon guarantees impunity. Trump is negotiating with
North Korea's Kim Jong-un; a nuclear Iran could expect similar treatment.

Iran understood that once it obtained a nuclear bomb, launching its operation to
destroy Israel would become far easier. Israel in turn understood that once Iran had
the bomb, its campaign to eliminate Israel would begin. The Hamas attack of October 7,
2023, served as a false start — it gave Israel justification for a broad regional
operation to dismantle the Axis of Resistance.

## The US-Israel Campaign and Its Limits

The US and Israel have achieved significant results: Iran's navy and air force have
been largely destroyed, and most of Iran's nuclear facilities could potentially be
eliminated. However, certain objectives remain out of reach through air power alone:

- Iran's full missile arsenal and launch infrastructure cannot be destroyed from the air
- The Strait of Hormuz cannot be unblocked without a large-scale ground operation
- Regime change is impossible without ground troops

A ground war in Iran would cost thousands — possibly tens of thousands — of American
lives. Trump cannot pursue a ground campaign in a country where the vast majority of
Americans oppose the war. Daily footage of military funerals would destroy his
political standing, trigger genuine impeachment proceedings with Republican senators,
and demolish the MAGA coalition that elected him on a promise of no new wars.

## Trump Under Pressure

Trump is negotiating from a position of growing weakness. His visit to China — planned
as the foreign policy centerpiece of his presidency — has been cancelled. Xi Jinping
is content to wait for Trump's economic exhaustion before dictating terms. Trump's
recent social media posts reveal a man in psychological turmoil: labeling Democrats as
fascists, threatening airport security measures, and publicly celebrating the death of
former FBI Director and special counsel Robert Mueller, who had investigated Trump's
ties to Russia.

Trump wanted not merely a reduction in Iran's military capacity but control over the
regime and control over oil — to arrive at Xi Jinping's table holding Iran and
Venezuela as trophies. Instead, he holds neither.

## Negotiations: Who Can Speak for Iran?

A fundamental obstacle to any ceasefire is uncertainty about Iran's leadership.
Supreme Leader Ayatollah Khamenei has not appeared in public since the US-Israeli
strikes on the Islamic Republic. His Nowruz (Persian New Year) greetings — one of the
most important occasions in the Iranian calendar — were posted on Telegram without a
video, delivered only by a reader. It is unknown whether he is alive, fatally wounded
from the strike on the Rahbar residence, or simply in hiding for security reasons.

President Masoud Pezeshkian issues statements and tweets, but no one takes him
seriously: he apologizes for Iranian strikes on Arab countries, then those strikes
intensify. He holds no constitutional authority over the armed forces; under the
Iranian constitution, command belongs to the Supreme Leader.

US demands for negotiations center on: complete nuclear disarmament, abandonment of
Iran's missile program, and guarantees that Iran will not threaten Israel or the Arab
states. Iran's demands are the opposite: a full nuclear cycle, and reparations for the
attacks. There is no common ground for a quick conclusion.

## Regional Diplomacy: Turkey's Proposal and Saudi Arabia's Warning

Iran's neighbors are scrambling to find a way out. Turkish Foreign Minister Hakan
Fidan met with Arab and Gulf state counterparts and proposed a compromise: if Iran
ends hostilities, Turkey and Saudi Arabia will offer Iran security guarantees and
economic cooperation. In return, the Gulf states want Iran to address the presence of
American military bases on their territory — the bases that manifestly failed to
protect them from Iranian attacks.

Simultaneously, Saudi Arabia ordered Iranian embassy staff to leave the kingdom within
24 hours, the first concrete countermeasure after Iran's continued strikes on Arab
civilian infrastructure. Saudi Arabia also revealed it may withdraw the enormous
financial payments it made to Pakistan under a mutual defense agreement, after Pakistan
failed to intervene on Riyadh's behalf when the war began.

## Pakistan, Nuclear Commitments, and Article 5

The Pakistan-Saudi non-intervention illustrates a broader principle: alliance
commitments hold only until they actually need to be honored. This has implications
for NATO's Article 5 as well. Russia is reportedly feeding Western media stories about
possible hybrid operations against the Narva region of Estonia. If such a scenario
arose — with Russian-directed "separatists" seizing local institutions without Russian
regular troops crossing the border — Article 5 almost certainly would not be invoked.
Western governments would instead call for consultations and demand Russia stop
supporting separatists.

Putin is unlikely to open a second front against the Baltic states before completing
the active phase of the Ukraine war (which, in his conception, means annexing Ukrainian
territory). His resources are stretched: the situations around Nagorno-Karabakh, Syria,
and now Iran all demonstrate Russia has no spare capacity.

## Iran's Remaining Leverage

Even if the US and Israel destroy Iran's nuclear facilities, Iran can still:

- Retain significant portions of its missile arsenal and launch systems
- Keep the Strait of Hormuz closed, sustaining an energy and economic crisis
- Accelerate its nuclear weapons program clandestinely to achieve a deterrent bomb and
  announce it to Washington and Tel Aviv
- Continue threatening Gulf states and European countries with its 4,000 km missiles

The question of rocket supply routes is also relevant: Russia maintains a military
division along the Tajik-Afghan border. The Tajik-Iranian border is often overlooked,
and weapons can be moved through Tajikistan or Afghanistan. The missiles used against
Diego Garcia may be Russian or Chinese in origin, not Iranian — a possibility
Portnikov considers highly plausible.

## Trump's Partial Sanctions Relief and Russian Windfall

The Trump administration has partially suspended sanctions on Russia for the duration
of the Iran war. Western analysts calculate that if those sanctions remain suspended
for several more months, Russia stands to earn an additional $250 billion from oil
sales — funds that will directly finance continued war against Ukraine.

A Ukrainian delegation is in Washington meeting with Witkoff and Kushner, trying to
ensure these sanctions are not extended. Meanwhile Russia has little incentive to
negotiate; peace talks — such as they are — consist of American envoys meeting
Ukrainians while Moscow ignores the process entirely.

## The Middle East War's Consequences for Ukraine

Portnikov is unambiguous: the outcome of the US-Iran war directly determines whether
Ukraine survives as a state, not IMF conditionality or domestic reform timelines.
If the world slides toward nuclear exchange, no one will be providing Ukraine with
assistance. Ukraine's specialists in drone and missile defense are actively consulting
both the United States and Gulf states — while Iranian Shahed drones continue to fly
over Ukrainian cities and an Iranian rocket strike on Ukraine remains a live threat.
(The Islamic Republic has explicitly threatened Ukraine with missile strikes; Portnikov
asks pointedly why the Iranian embassy in Kyiv is still open, four years into the
full-scale invasion that Iran helped enable with its Shahed deliveries.)

## European Defense and Ukraine's Future Role

On the question of a future European Defense Union, Portnikov is skeptical. European
countries will do everything possible to preserve the American nuclear umbrella.
Pro-Trump forces in Europe are working to prevent independent European defense
structures from emerging — illustrated by Polish President Karol Nawrocki's recent
veto on Poland's participation in the European rearmament program.

If a war-ending peace deal for Ukraine requires a neutrality clause prohibiting
membership in military alliances and banning foreign troops on Ukrainian soil, then
any Ukrainian participation in a future European defense structure becomes a
post-2030s question at best. The immediate priority is ensuring Ukraine does not end
the war as a collection of Russian Federation oblasts rather than a sovereign state.

## Conclusion

The war has entered its 22nd day with two alarming new developments: an exchange of
strikes on nuclear facilities that risks catastrophic escalation, and confirmation
that Iran possesses missiles with 4,000 km range — enough to reach all of Europe.
Trump is signaling openness to negotiations with Iran, but the Iranian leadership
structure is opaque and its conditions are incompatible with Washington's. The war
may last months or years. Meanwhile, every day it continues, Russia earns more oil
revenue, fires more drones at Ukrainian cities, and moves further from any genuine
peace process.
