# Inside Myanmar's Forced-Scam Economy
> **Content note:** This article discusses trafficking, confinement, forced labour,
> torture, sexual violence, financial loss, and death.
*Source note: The documentary's Russian transcript came from automatic captions.*
*Names, figures, and phrasing in that transcript may therefore contain errors.*
A job offer arrives with a real company website, paid travel, and plausible pay.
The contract and work visa, the recruiter says, will be completed after arrival.
A hotel stay in Thailand sustains the appearance of an ordinary relocation.
Then the traveller is driven to the border, moved across the Moei River,
and delivered to a guarded compound in Myanmar.
This is not merely a story about online fraud.
It is a forced-criminality system with two groups of people being harmed.
One group is trafficked, confined, and compelled to carry out fraud.
The other is approached online, emotionally manipulated, and robbed.
The system transfers suffering from people held behind fences to people at home.
Both groups deserve recognition, remedy, and accurate language.
UN investigators and crime agencies have documented the regional structure.
Deceptive recruitment feeds people into guarded compounds.
Passports and phones may be taken, invented debts imposed, and movement denied.
Long shifts, revenue quotas, food deprivation, beatings, and electric shocks
are used to make people work and punish resistance.[1][2][3]
People may be resold to another compound or held while families face ransom demands.
Fraud floors build false relationships and direct targets to fake investments.
The displayed profits are fictitious; deposits become difficult or impossible to recover.
Criminal networks increasingly add AI-generated images, voices, and live identities.[2][3]
The 2026 documentary by Anton Lyadov and The Люди enters this system through
interviews with two men identified as Saken and Artyom.
Their testimony is important, but it is not the same as an official finding.
The strongest account keeps three evidence layers apart:
regional findings from authoritative sources, the men's own testimony,
and serious allegations that remain unresolved.
## The map: Mae Sot, Myawaddy, KK Park, and Shwe Kokko
Mae Sot is a city in western Thailand.
Across the Moei River lies Myanmar's Myawaddy area in Karen State.
Thailand can be a destination named in a job offer and also a transit country.
People may land in Bangkok, travel through Mae Sot, and then enter Myanmar.[1][2]
Myawaddy is not the name of one compound.
It is a border township and a wider area containing multiple fraud hubs.
KK Park and Shwe Kokko are distinct places within that landscape.
Treating the names as interchangeable hides differences in location and control.
KK Park lies south of Myawaddy, close to the river and visible from Thailand.
It is one of Myanmar's largest and best-known scam compounds.
The claim that it is definitively the largest in Asia is not established.
BBC reporting has described many compounds along the border, not one unique site.[6]
Shwe Kokko, also called Yatai New City in sanctions records, is north of Myawaddy.
It developed around casino, hotel, and new-city projects.
US Treasury findings link parts of its economy to fraud, trafficking, and abuse.[5]
Those findings are government determinations, not criminal convictions.
Control along this border has changed during Myanmar's armed conflict.
A compound's developer, land lessor, security provider, utility supplier,
and the syndicate operating a fraud floor may be different actors.
Calling all of them “rebels” or “the Chinese” is inaccurate and stigmatizing.
In May 2025, the US Treasury designated the Karen National Army, or KNA,
and sanctioned its leader Saw Chit Thu and two sons.[4]
Treasury alleged that the KNA leased land, supplied security and utilities,
facilitated trafficking, and profited from compounds including KK Park.[4]
In September 2025, Treasury sanctioned She Zhijiang, Myanmar Yatai,
and related people and companies tied to the Shwe Kokko network.[5]
It alleged confinement, abuse, ransom, and forced commercial sex.[5]
These records show how armed protection and commercial infrastructure can enable fraud.
They do not prove that every actor has the same role in every compound.
## Recruitment that looks like employment
Authoritative reports describe vacancies designed to withstand a quick check.
Recruiters borrow the identity of a real business or create a polished front.
They conduct online interviews, test language skills, and send workplace images.
They may pay for flights, airport pickup, hotels, food, and onward transport.[1][2]
The candidates are not limited to one social class or education level.
Some are IT workers seeking advancement.
Others are multilingual graduates, models, assistants, or people facing debt.
A person's skill, scepticism, or travel experience does not make deception impossible.
The transition from recruitment to control can be gradual.
A missing contract may be explained as routine.
A promised work visa may be deferred until arrival.
A border journey may be presented as an administrative trip.
An armed escort can make the loss of choice clear without a spoken threat.
Once across the border, irregular immigration status can become another weapon.
A trafficked person may fear arrest, deportation, or punishment for the fraud.
Isolation makes the recruiter and captor appear to be the only source of food,
transport, documents, information, and contact with home.
OHCHR estimated in 2023 that at least 120,000 people in Myanmar
might be held in situations of forced online scamming.[1]
It stressed that clandestine activity makes precise measurement difficult.[1]
A later UN synthesis described a much larger regional workforce,
not a verified count of trafficking victims at any one site.
Numbers should therefore be tied to geography, date, and methodology.
“Thousands in the border compounds” is more defensible than a precise local census.
The documentary's estimate of 10,000 to 15,000 people in one compound
is Saken's recollection, not a verified population count, and should not be repeated.
## Saken's account: a plausible journey, attributed precisely
Saken, a technology worker from Kazakhstan, says he sought better-paid work online.
He recalls a recruiter using a real company's website and moving the chat to Telegram.
The offer was about $3,500 to $4,000, plausible to him for overseas IT work.
He asked for a contract and offer but was told they would arrive later.
According to Saken, recruiters bought a ticket three days after his interview.
He spent several days in a normal Thai hotel and remained in contact with them.
He says a car then took him toward Mae Sot.
During the journey, an armed escort entered and displayed a handgun.
Saken says he crossed the river by boat with three other people.
Armed men met them on the Myanmar side and vehicles took them onward.
He identifies the destination as KK Park and says he remained for nine months.
The documentary does not provide records that independently prove this timeline.
He says he was first subjected to exhausting physical work and little rest.
After about a month, he accepted an office role carrying out online fraud.
He describes that change not as a free career choice but as capitulation under coercion.
That distinction is central to understanding forced criminality.
Saken reports shifts that grew to roughly 16 hours, with no days off.
He describes surveillance, restricted food, little sleep, and punishment.
He says loud music, intense light, and strobe effects were used against him.
Regional reports corroborate comparable abuse, but not his exact episode.[1][3]
He also says his family assembled a $50,000 ransom in USDT.
The claimed demand, payment, and wallet destination have not been independently verified.
Ransom demands are, however, a documented pattern across scam compounds.[2][3]
Payment is not a safe or guaranteed route to release.
## Artyom's account: prior suspicion is not consent to abuse
Artyom, identified as Russian, says he was broke in Thailand when friends found work.
The offer reportedly included salary, commission, housing, and food.
He says he understood that the proposed role involved drawing people
into a cryptocurrency project aimed at the US market.
That initial awareness matters to his story, but it does not settle his legal status.
Knowing that work is dubious, unethical, or even fraudulent is not consent
to confinement, document theft, assault, forced labour, ransom, or resale.
Consent is irrelevant where trafficking uses deception, coercion,
or abuse of vulnerability under the applicable framework.[2]
Artyom says he spent three months in a guarded compound.
He recalls seeing a payment to the driver on arrival and interpreting it as a purchase.
He describes barred boundaries, armed guards, withheld travel documents,
and threatened transfer to another compound.
He says he witnessed a worker struck repeatedly with a bamboo stick.
He also reports electric-shock devices and other punishment.
His account resembles abuses documented across the region.[1][3]
That resemblance supports plausibility; it does not independently prove the incident.
Artyom says he and two friends refused a proposed transfer.
He recalls demands for payment and the removal of meal coupons.
He says they were eventually released, but his passport was not returned.
He presents that result as exceptional rather than a method others should attempt.
No reader should treat his account as advice to confront captors.
Resistance, signalling, evidence collection, or escape can increase immediate danger.
Any response must be case-specific and led by trained authorities or responders.
## What forced labour looks like
The ILO defines forced labour as work exacted under threat of a penalty
and not offered voluntarily.[8]
Indicators include restriction of movement, intimidation, violence,
retention of identity documents, debt, abusive conditions, and excessive overtime.[8]
A salary or small allowance does not cancel those indicators.
Nor do restaurants, casinos, karaoke rooms, or shops inside a fenced development.
A self-contained compound can imitate ordinary life while keeping departure impossible.
Comfort can be distributed as a reward and deprivation used as punishment.
Some people in a compound may participate willingly or supervise others.
That fact does not justify presuming that everyone is free.
Authorities need individualized, trauma-informed screening to distinguish
trafficked people, coerced participants, witnesses, and willing perpetrators.[1][2]
The non-punishment principle is crucial.
Trafficked people should not be punished for unlawful acts they were compelled
to commit as a direct consequence of their trafficking.[1][3]
That is not blanket immunity for every person found at a fraud site.
It is a demand for evidence, context, and individual assessment before prosecution.
Released people may need medical care, safe accommodation, interpretation,
legal assistance, psychosocial support, and secure repatriation.
Treating them first as immigration offenders can deepen harm and silence evidence.
At the same time, people defrauded online need financial investigation,
support, and an honest chance to understand how their trust was exploited.
## The fraud at prevention level
The dominant pattern is often called relationship-investment fraud.
A fabricated identity approaches a target and slowly builds trust.
The contact may appear romantic, friendly, professionally useful, or affluent.
Eventually the relationship is connected to an investment opportunity.[2][5]
A controlled website or app then displays invented gains.
The interface looks like a trading platform, but its numbers are not proof of assets.
Deposits may be routed through bank accounts, payment intermediaries,
or virtual assets and then laundered across a wider network.[2][5]
When a target asks to withdraw, the operation may block access,
demand new fees, or invent taxes and compliance problems.
The point is not a brilliant investment that later failed.
The investment display was part of the deception from the start.
AI can make a stolen identity more persuasive.
INTERPOL and UNODC describe synthetic profiles, generated content,
and deepfakes being incorporated into social engineering.[2][3]
A live video call is therefore not conclusive proof of identity.
Verification should happen through an independently obtained, trusted channel.
The documentary contains detailed claims about accounts, quotas, and scripts.
Those specifics should not become a how-to guide.
Saken's claimed $300,000 monthly quota was testimony, not a universal standard.
His claim of about 200 accounts likewise cannot be generalized.
The film also makes a factor-of-ten currency error.
It divides $300,000 by 100 to get $3,000 per target, which is correct.
It then describes $3,000 as about 23,000 to 30,000 rubles.
At the rates used in the audit, the range is about 231,000 to 300,000 rubles.
The correction does not validate the quota.
It only prevents a demonstrable arithmetic error from entering the article.
The useful warning is simpler: online intimacy can be organized at industrial scale,
and a convincing balance on an unfamiliar platform may be entirely fictitious.
## Violence: established pattern, specific testimony, unresolved claims
The established regional record is already severe.
OHCHR has documented arbitrary detention, beatings, electric shocks,
food and sleep deprivation, sexual violence, and public punishment.[1][3]
INTERPOL also identifies torture, rape, and sexual exploitation.[3]
These findings support reporting that violence enforces work and revenue demands.
They also make Saken's and Artyom's accounts credible subjects for investigation.
But a regional pattern cannot verify every event attributed to a named compound.
The article should never turn “consistent with” into “therefore proven.”
Several sensational claims in the documentary do not meet that test.
Saken alleges repeated executions at KK Park.
The film also alleges that bodies were placed in the Moei River.
Neither specific claim was independently corroborated in the reviewed record.
They are omitted here as established facts.
The claim that people were sold for organ removal is also unverified.
So is the account of a Belarusian woman's death, cremation,
and a demand that her family pay for her ashes.
The film itself acknowledges uncertainty around the woman's fate.
The alleged presence of “American psychologists” directing chats is unverified.
Nationality does not establish a professional role, and no credentials are supplied.
Claims of a universal $300,000 quota or 200 accounts per worker are unverified too.
One person's description of one floor cannot define an entire industry.
The claim that KK Park was surrounded by 500 kilometres of jungle is not credible.
The compound is near the river, roads, settlements, and the Thai border.[6][7]
Escape may still be lethal because of guards, conflict, terrain, mines,
irregular status, and the danger of being captured or resold.
Exaggerated geography is unnecessary to explain that danger.
## Raids disrupt compounds; they do not end the system
Myanmar's military raided parts of KK Park in October 2025.
It later demolished many structures.
BBC reporting and analysis by Myanmar Witness confirm major disruption,
but not the complete destruction claimed in the documentary.[6][7]
Myanmar Witness geolocated military activity and seized satellite equipment.
Its later imagery analysis found extensive but incomplete demolition.
Some structures were only partly destroyed and could potentially be rebuilt.[7]
The evidence supports “major disruption,” not “eradication.”
Raids can release large numbers of people and still create new risks.
Survivors may be stranded, detained, or left waiting for screening and repatriation.
Operators can move people, equipment, and capital to another site.
Enforcement without protection can displace the business rather than end it.[3][9]
The documentary also compresses the chronology of China's Ming-family case.
A Zhejiang court sentenced 11 Ming family members and associates in September 2025.
China executed them on 29 January 2026 after review.[10]
Their network was based around Laukkaing in northern Myanmar, not KK Park.[10]
That case corroborates lethal violence in Myanmar's wider scam economy.
It does not corroborate the alleged executions at KK Park.
Nor does it establish the film's claim that the group owned about 40 centres.
Separate cases should not be fused merely because both involve Myanmar.
## Before travel, and when someone is missing
Verify an employer through contact details obtained independently.
Do not rely on a recruiter-provided website, reference, review, or phone number.
Confirm that the named recruiter works for the organization.
Search corporate registration and consult the destination country's labour channels.
Obtain a written contract and the correct work visa before travel.
Check the worksite address, duties, salary, deductions, housing, and return terms.
A demand to depart immediately while paperwork waits until arrival is a warning.
Paid travel and a polished interview do not make an offer safe.
Leave passport and contract copies with a trusted person and keep secure copies.
Do not surrender your passport or phone to an employer or transport intermediary.
Share the itinerary, employer details, accommodation, and regular check-in schedule.
Agree in advance on an innocuous code phrase that signals danger.
If someone may have been trafficked, do not advise confrontation or an improvised escape.
Do not alert suspected captors through public posts or reckless direct contact.
Contact national police and ask that the case reach the proper international channels.
Also contact the relevant embassy or consulate without delay.[3]
INTERPOL works through national law-enforcement bodies, not as a substitute
for a family's local emergency number.[3]
Preserve messages, phone numbers, job ads, travel records, and payment details
only when doing so does not increase danger to the missing person.
Follow case-specific instructions from trained anti-trafficking responders.
For suspected investment fraud, stop sending money.
Do not pay new “taxes,” “verification fees,” or recovery agents on the contact's advice.
Contact the bank, exchange, or payment service promptly and report the fraud to police.
Use independently found contact details, not links supplied inside the relationship.
## The story that accuracy reveals
This system does not need embellished horrors to be alarming.
Its documented machinery is enough: deceptive recruitment, cross-border movement,
confinement, forced labour, quotas, violence, ransom, resale, and armed protection.
It turns trafficked people into instruments for harming distant fraud victims.
Saken's and Artyom's accounts give that machinery human scale.
Their testimony should be heard with care and checked without hostility.
Attribution protects them and the reader: “he says” is not disbelief.
It is an honest description of what the available evidence can establish.
The same discipline protects people defrauded online.
They were not foolish for trusting a patient, convincing relationship.
The person typing may also have been under threat,
but coercion on one side does not erase loss and trauma on the other.
A credible response holds multiple truths at once.
Organizers, willing perpetrators, enablers, and money launderers need investigation.
Trafficked people need identification, protection, and individualized screening.
Fraud victims need support and financial remedies.
Governments and platforms need to disrupt the infrastructure that joins them.
The essential warning is not that every overseas job or online relationship is false.
It is that appearances can be manufactured across an entire chain.
Independent verification, lawful paperwork, protected documents,
and early contact with police and consular officials can narrow that chain's power.
