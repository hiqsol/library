# How Russia May Test NATO Below the Threshold of War

## Scope and evidentiary caution

This article reconstructs journalist Ivan Yakovina's argument in a Russian-language interview.
It is analysis, not independent confirmation of Russian plans, NATO weaknesses, or future US
conduct. Events cited in the discussion are separated below from Yakovina's inferences and
forecasts. Automatic captions may also contain errors in names and details.

## Denial as preparation and political cover

Yakovina interprets Vladimir Putin's public denials as a governing method rather than reliable
signals of restraint. He recalls Putin denying or minimizing actions that others regarded as
obvious and describes the habit as a legacy of security-service tradecraft: deny involvement,
preserve room to escape responsibility, and force opponents to prove attribution.

In Yakovina's reading, apparently casual remarks can function as short, controlled addresses.
Friendly questioners let Putin choose the subject while preserving the appearance of an
interview. When Putin raises a sensitive issue and simultaneously denies an intention,
Yakovina treats that combination as evidence that the Kremlin is thinking about the issue.
That is an interpretive rule, not proof that a particular operation has been approved.

The strategic value of denial would be greatest in ambiguous incidents. An unattributed drone,
a short border crossing, sabotage, or an attack routed through a deniable channel could create
fear while delaying agreement over who acted and what response is justified. The method seeks
to keep coercion below the political threshold at which NATO members feel compelled to treat
an incident as war.

## Why pressure might fall on distant or less committed EU states

Yakovina argues that Putin increasingly sees Europe as an extension of Ukraine because European
support helps Kyiv continue fighting. In his account, Moscow may regard Europe as weaker and
less cohesive than the United States, yet indispensable to Ukraine's endurance.

This makes apparently peripheral states relevant. Yakovina names countries such as Spain,
Portugal, France, and Italy when discussing hypothetical pressure. He reasons that publics far
from the eastern flank may see the war as remote and may be less willing to bear costs for
Ukraine. Limited intimidation could therefore amplify domestic arguments for disengagement.

His proposed political mechanism has two stages. Pressure may help Russia-friendly forces gain
power in an EU member. Such a government could then reduce its own assistance, obstruct common
EU measures, or constrain states that want to do more. In this thesis, the goal is not
necessarily territorial conquest. It may be to fracture European decision-making and weaken
the supply network sustaining Ukraine.

No evidence in the interview establishes that Moscow has selected these states as targets or
that coercion would produce the political result Yakovina expects. His account is a theory of
incentives and vulnerabilities.

## Drone incursions and hybrid probes

The interviewer referred to drones entering Romania and to a Russian Mi-8 helicopter briefly
crossing into Poland. The discussion gave the latter incident as roughly 42 seconds and
300 meters. These were examples raised in the interview, not independently documented there.

Yakovina interprets such episodes as probes. In his escalation ladder, drones cross a border,
then helicopters do so, and later a drone might fall near or strike a military site rather than
an empty field. Each step could test detection, attribution, political unity, and the response
authorities are prepared to order.

He also imagines more serious possibilities: drone overflights of power stations, attacks on
NATO bases, or small groups of Russian troops entering Baltic territory. He speculates that
platforms at sea could launch drones toward targets farther west. These are scenarios he says
fit Putin's motives and capabilities. The interview provides no direct evidence that such plans
exist, and it does not show that Russia will attack NATO.

The essence of the thesis is calibration. A useful probe would be serious enough to expose
hesitation but limited enough to invite debate over accident, attribution, proportionality, or
whether collective defense applies. Repetition could normalize violations and reveal where
Europe's practical red lines lie.

## NATO readiness and lessons from Ukraine's drone war

Yakovina's strongest readiness claim concerns tactical adaptation. He says Ukrainian drone
operators participating in exercises with NATO forces were able to paralyze much larger
formations. He uses this reported experience to argue that combat-tested drone teams can
produce effects once associated with much larger conventional units. The interview offers no
supporting exercise records, so the claim should be treated as his account.

He contends that Russia, like Ukraine, has accumulated operational experience with drones,
electronic warfare, reconnaissance, and rapid adaptation. He distinguishes elite, well-equipped
Russian formations from expendable assault units that dominate some public images of the war.
In his view, focusing only on the latter leads Europe to underestimate the Russian military.

Yakovina is especially critical of force displays centered on tanks and infantry without clear
integration of drones and electronic warfare. His policy implication is that European forces
need personnel with current battlefield experience, not merely additional legacy platforms.
He goes so far as to suggest that Europeans could gain experience by serving alongside Ukraine.
That is his recommendation, not evidence that NATO armies as a whole are incapable of fighting.

The Ukraine lesson is narrower than the rhetoric sometimes used in the interview. Drones can
make exposed formations highly vulnerable and can compress the advantage of mass. They do not,
by themselves, establish that Russia could defeat NATO. Air power, logistics, air defense,
command systems, geography, alliance reinforcement, and nuclear deterrence remain outside the
interview's analysis.

## Kaliningrad in the strategic ledger

The interviewer presented Kaliningrad as a Russian vulnerability that NATO could rapidly attack
in a direct conflict. Yakovina replies that Putin might accept losing the enclave if Russia
could seize more valuable territory or politically break NATO and the EU.

This makes Kaliningrad a possible bargaining asset in Yakovina's strategic ledger: expendable
territory whose loss might be traded against gains elsewhere. He suggests Moscow could expect
European rule over the population to be less destructive than Russian conduct in occupied
areas, reducing the Kremlin's perceived cost of sacrifice.

That assessment is speculative. It does not establish Putin's willingness to surrender
Kaliningrad, NATO's ability or intent to take it, or the outcome of a wider conflict. It also
understates the military, nuclear, domestic political, and alliance risks attached to the
region.

## Trump and uncertainty over US intervention

Yakovina says Moscow's central benefit from Donald Trump is reduced US support for Ukraine and
Trump's tendency to describe European security as Europe's responsibility. From that, Yakovina
predicts Trump might decline to intervene in a limited Russia-NATO confrontation in Europe.

He also explicitly acknowledges that Trump's actions cannot be predicted with certainty. That
qualification matters. The interview supplies no decision, legal finding, or operational order
showing that the United States would withhold assistance. NATO obligations, Congress, US forces
in Europe, the scale of an incident, casualties, and domestic pressure could all shape a choice.

In the coercion model, uncertainty itself benefits Moscow. If European governments doubt that
Washington would act, they may respond more cautiously even without any formal US withdrawal.
But a gamble based on assumed US passivity could also fail and turn a limited probe into a
broader alliance response.

## Infrastructure and the proposed city-versus-city campaign

The interview discusses Russian strikes on communications, internet infrastructure, providers,
power-related systems, and data centers. Yakovina says Ukraine's internet is unusually
distributed among many providers. He offers his own uninterrupted service as an anecdote and
expects a continuing cycle of attack and repair.

Distribution creates resilience, not immunity. Damage to fiber routes, mobile networks, energy
supply, cloud services, or data centers could disrupt civilian life and military coordination.
Power and communications are also interdependent: outages can disable network equipment, while
lost connectivity can slow grid monitoring, repairs, emergency services, and public warning.
Concentrated data centers may be high-impact targets even when the wider network is dispersed.

By a city-versus-city campaign, the speakers mean reciprocal long-range pressure on urban and
industrial systems, not a formally declared doctrine. Yakovina argues that Ukraine has a wider
set of strategically valuable targets inside Russia, including oil refining and defense
industry sites. By contrast, much of the production and fuel supply supporting Ukraine is
located in other European countries and is beyond Russia's current war zone unless Moscow
chooses a much more dangerous escalation.

The interview mentioned a reported strike on the Ilsky refinery and alleged Russian strikes on
a kindergarten in Kyiv's Obolon district, a school in Ivankiv, and small data centers. Those
claims were cited during the conversation without independent verification. Yakovina uses them
to contrast attacks on Russian war-supporting industry with Russian attacks he views as less
militarily valuable.

In his framing, Ukraine's strategic objectives are to preserve a resilient society, keep
external supply lines open, and degrade Russia's ability to fund and sustain the war by
attacking oil and military-industrial capacity. This describes his assessment; it is not a
complete or official statement of Ukrainian strategy.

## What could turn limited coercion into escalation

A below-threshold operation is not controllable simply because its author intends it to be
limited. Several pathways could convert a probe into open conflict:

- A drone or missile could kill civilians or allied troops instead of landing harmlessly.
- A helicopter or ground unit could be shot down, captured, or forced to land.
- Sabotage could cascade through electricity, communications, hospitals, or transport.
- Attribution could become clear enough that restraint looks politically impossible.
- Repeated incidents could persuade allies that inaction is more dangerous than retaliation.
- Russia could misread a cautious first response as permission for a larger second move.
- NATO could misread an accident as an intentional opening attack.
- US behavior could differ sharply from Moscow's assumptions about Trump.
- Strikes involving Kaliningrad could engage regional forces or nuclear signaling.

The danger is reciprocal uncertainty. Russia might seek an incident small enough to split the
alliance, while NATO might respond forcefully to restore deterrence. Each side could then see
the other's next step as confirmation of hostile intent.

## The UN: useful forum, failed security instrument

Yakovina gives the United Nations limited continuing value. He sees it as a global stage for
public diplomacy, a place where leaders can meet, and a venue for technical coordination and
standards in areas such as aviation or communications.

He judges it a complete failure at its central purpose of preventing or ending wars. That is his
assessment, not a demonstrated institutional conclusion. The interview does not examine UN
aid agencies, monitoring work, treaty functions, voting coalitions, or the Security Council
veto structure in enough depth to support a comprehensive evaluation.

## AI, robots, and an accelerating battlefield arms race

The discussion begins from President Volodymyr Zelenskyy's warning at the UN that artificial
intelligence could soon make battlefield decisions. Yakovina says AI is already being used
extensively in the war. He is certain about Russian use but less certain about the Ukrainian
side, although he believes both employ it.

He describes a competitive trap. If AI-enabled targeting, navigation, reconnaissance, or
robotic systems confer an advantage, neither side can stop using them unilaterally without
risking defeat. Wartime pressure therefore accelerates development and gives autonomous systems
large amounts of operational experience.

Yakovina's most dramatic forecast is that robots may become much better fighters than humans
and could threaten humanity if control is lost. The interview provides no technical evidence
that current systems possess independent strategic agency or could seize control of the planet.
The credible near-term risks are more bounded: mistaken identification, brittle automation,
human overreliance, opaque targeting, faster escalation, wider proliferation, and reduced time
for human judgment.

His warning is best read as an argument for urgency around human control, testing,
accountability, fail-safe design, and international rules. It is not evidence that an
uncontrollable machine war is inevitable.

## What Yakovina's thesis establishes—and what it does not

The interview offers a coherent hypothesis: Russia may use deniable, graduated pressure to test
European cohesion, exploit uncertainty over US backing, and weaken support for Ukraine without
immediately choosing full-scale war with NATO. Ukraine's battlefield shows why drones,
electronic warfare, distributed infrastructure, and rapid adaptation deserve more weight in
European planning.

The thesis does not establish that Putin has ordered any scenario discussed, that Russia will
attack NATO, that Trump will refuse assistance, or that NATO would fail. It does not compare
Russian and allied capabilities systematically, test alternative explanations for border
incidents, or account fully for deterrence and escalation control.

Its practical value is therefore diagnostic rather than predictive. Governments can harden
power and communications, disperse critical services, improve counter-drone and electronic
warfare capacity, clarify response procedures, and strengthen political coordination. Those
steps reduce the value of a probe whether or not Yakovina's forecast proves correct.
