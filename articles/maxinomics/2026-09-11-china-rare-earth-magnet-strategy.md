# China's Rare-Earth Magnet Strategy Beyond Oil

## Source, scope, and evidentiary limits

This article reconstructs Maxinomics' argument in "China Found Something Better Than Oil."
The video is dated September 11, 2026, and runs 27:26.
It relies here only on its timestamped English automatic captions and description.
Those captions may mishear names, technical terms, dates, and numbers.
Where the transcript conflicts with or is clarified by the description, that is noted.

The categorical headline implies that China found a resource superior to oil.
The source does not demonstrate that rare earths replace oil as an energy source,
export earner, or universal instrument of power.
Its strongest supported thesis is narrower: Maxinomics argues that China deliberately
built dominant capacity in magnet rare-earth processing and manufacturing, then used
export controls to turn industrial concentration into strategic leverage.

Oil appears chiefly in Deng Xiaoping's quoted comparison:
"Saudi Arabia has oil. China has rare earths."
Maxinomics also mocks an unrelated prediction that oil wells would run dry in perhaps
one, two, or three years and society would become "full Mad Max."
The source supplies no oil-demand, production, reserve, price, or substitution data.

It names solar, cars, aluminum, steel, and ships as industries where Maxinomics says
China used, or may have used, a similar state-backed pattern.
It does not analyze solar deployment, wind power, batteries, or their supply chains.
Nor does it quantify clean-energy output, emissions, or displacement of fossil fuels.
Electric vehicles appear through their motors and magnets, not through battery chemistry.
Any broader clean-energy interpretation must therefore remain an inference.

## The indispensable component: compact permanent magnets

Maxinomics begins with a copper-wire coil, one battery, and one magnet.
The simple motor spins quietly and smoothly until the battery runs out, with little heat.
A current gives plain copper wire a temporary magnetic field.
That field pushes against a permanent magnet; constrained movement becomes rotation.
More current strengthens the temporary magnet and increases the shove.
A second permanent magnet can turn spinning into pointing in a chosen direction.
Maxinomics describes the modern electric world as this interaction, shrunk and repeated
"a thousand times": electricity creates a field, it fights a real magnet, and something
useful moves.

According to Maxinomics, adding a magnet rare-earth metal to molten metal and cooling it
creates a permanent magnet with unmatched force for its weight.
The video says an AirPods set has six magnets, an iPhone has 40, and a laptop at least 12.
It says an electric-car motor contains 12 lb of magnet material.
It also says every missile used by the US military has at least 12 rare-earth magnets,
which steer it; later, the narrower demonstrated example is the Tomahawk.

Early magnets made from iron or aluminum were heavy, weak, and poor at high temperatures.
Maxinomics says they nevertheless underpinned radar that let Allied aircraft see a
captioned "German hubot" during World War II; the automatic wording is uncertain.
Twenty-five years after the war, two Air Force engineers combined cobalt and samarium.
The resulting first rare-earth magnet was lighter, stronger, and heat resistant.

Its price was already high and rose when the Congo entered a major civil war.
The video says the Congo supplied 95% of cobalt then and still supplies a majority.
Cobalt prices rose 400% in less than one year.
Five years later, General Motors and the company that became Hitachi Metals unveiled the
neodymium-iron-boron magnet at a conference.
The video later dates that invention to 1983 and says both companies developed versions.

Maxinomics calls the new magnet smaller, more powerful, and "three times cheaper."
GM immediately used it to halve the size of the starter motor in all its cars.
The technology spread to power windows, power locks, windshield wipers, cordless tools,
hard drives with serious laptop storage, phones, speakers, refrigerators, and EV motors.
Avoiding rare-earth magnets, Maxinomics says, imposes costs in size, weight, efficiency,
or price; these magnets made it possible to shrink the electric world.

The video says 96 cents of every dollar in the rare-earth industry ends up in magnets,
with almost all of that value flowing into China.
China made exactly zero magnets when the neodymium magnet was invented in 1983.
Maxinomics says China now controls 94% of all "magnet rare earths."
Those figures are asserted in the video; the supplied sources do not document them.

## Deposits are widespread; separation and know-how are scarce

Maxinomics stresses that rare earths are not geologically rare.
The source says China did not win because it alone possessed deposits.
It won by doing the difficult, dirty separation work that others rejected.

The video distinguishes abundant light rare earths, captioned as neodymium and
"posemium," from heavier and scarcer ones, captioned as "deproium" and samarium.
The description clarifies the intended topics as dysprosium and samarium and separately
names neodymium; the uncertain "posemium" is likely a caption error but is not repaired
from outside evidence.
The heavier materials are described as especially important to militaries.

Mountain Pass lies in Southern California, about two hours from Las Vegas.
The mine was discovered because the US military wanted uranium.
It had uranium, but its rare earths were much more abundant.
Maxinomics calls it one of the best rare-earth mines ever and says that, over the past
50 years, it has repeatedly entered and left the news.
No other rare-earth mine, the video claims, yields as much rare earth per bucket.

In one scoop, 90% is ordinary rock and 10% is rare earths.
The caption lists cerium, lanthanum, neodymium, and "posemium."
It says almost nothing in the scoop is useful except neodymium and "posemium" because
the other rare earths are common by-products, useful but nearly given away.

A neodymium magnet about 1.5 inches across is said to have about 12 lb of pull.
The transcript then shows an amount of pure neodymium without a textual measurement.
About 200 lb of Mountain Pass earth could make only nine such magnets.
The same scoop could make about 250 sets of AirPods.
Most rare-earth mines would recover less than half Mountain Pass's yield.

Rare-earth neighbors on the periodic table have nearly the same size, electron behavior,
bonding, and chemical responses.
Maxinomics compares separating them with sorting identical twins in matching clothes,
rather than distinguishing a toddler from a professional basketball player in the dark.
They require dozens of tests repeated hundreds of times, each nudging elements apart.
The family takes more steps, chemicals, and time to separate than other mined metals.
Their similarity helps them cluster into deposits in molten rock, but frustrates refining.

At Mountain Pass, ore was mined from an open pit, trucked, crushed into fine sand, then
passed through bath after bath of hot chemicals to make rare-earth concentrate.
Some residue could be recycled and some chemicals were consumed.
Wastewater went through an eight-inch pipeline for 14 miles to evaporate in the desert.
County and state authorities had approved this after settlement-tank purification.
It was expected to be merely salty, on hot desert ground miles from anyone.

Over 12 years, however, the pipeline ruptured more than 60 times.
Maxinomics says radioactive thorium left in the water caked the pipe.
Almost one million gallons of toxic water spilled at points along the 14 miles.
Lawsuits, fines, and bad publicity followed.
The video calls this effectively the end of US rare-earth processing at that time.

A large mine on the edge of the Mongolian desert then accelerated production.
The description identifies Baotou, while the captions call it a legendary Mongolia mine.
Its separated waste goes into an open pool beside a town, one mile wide by one mile long.
Maxinomics says it has the same thorium and toxicity problems as Mountain Pass.
He argues that Chinese rules differ because rare earths are a government priority.
Companies in the process cannot effectively be sued; such cases would not be heard,
and even simple protest would carry risk.

Material moves from mine to town for processing, then by train to the electronics center
around the Pearl River Delta.
Maxinomics argues that political protection and cheaper waste handling make foreign
competition exceptionally difficult because clean treatment raises competitors' prices.
This is simultaneously a manufacturing advantage and a major domestic environmental,
legal, and social cost that the video says China suppresses rather than solves.

## Acquiring technology and assembling the domestic chain

Mountain Pass supplied the GM division that co-invented a neodymium magnet in 1983.
It still supplied that division when GM spun it out and sold it in 1995.
The resulting company is identified as Magnequench in the description.
Automatic captions vary among "Magnaquench" and "MagnaQuen."

For two years, a buyer captioned variously as "Ston," "Saxon," and "Sexton" tried to
persuade GM to sell the patents, knowledge, tooling, processes, and factories.
GM declined until financial problems led it to dispose of non-core businesses.
The buyer then acquired Magnequench.

Maxinomics says the transaction underwent federal review, as foreign purchases do.
He compares the sensitivity with the impossibility of a foreign country simply buying
Boeing, even for an outrageous sum.
Documents are scarce, the review appears to have been rushed during the rise of free
trade, and the source says the central question remains unresolved.
Magnequench supplied magnets to dozens of companies and the US military.

The video asks whether reviewers knew two Chinese companies owned 62% of the buyer.
It says the owners of those two companies were both sons-in-law of Deng Xiaoping.
Deng is described as China's ruler for 12 years, arriving two years after Mao.
During a tour of China, he made the Saudi-oil and Chinese-rare-earth comparison and,
Maxinomics says, laid out a plan for rare-earth dominance.

China already had substantial deposits, including the large northern mine and two key
mines in South China that supplied less common military-critical rare earths.
What it lacked was process technology, patents, know-how, and other downstream capacity.
Maxinomics presents the acquisition as filling the magnet-making gap.
China then had domestic deposits and production knowledge, while its regulatory system
permitted the polluting separation that was closing Mountain Pass.

## The four-step industrial-policy mechanism

Maxinomics generalizes a four-step playbook beyond rare earths to aluminum, solar, steel,
and ships, calling it extraordinarily effective, whether judged good or bad.
The supplied source does not independently test those other sectors.

First, build the whole chain at home.
Chinese state banks allegedly lend to mines and plants at "dirt cheap" rates, far below
those of a normal Western bank.
Land is easier to obtain because, in Maxinomics' formulation, the party owns it and there
is no private land.
Unlike a company in Texas or Australia, the project begins with a state backer that need
not demand profit in the next quarter.
Cheap capital and allocated land support sales to China, America, Europe, or anyone else.
Volume expands mines, plants, workforces, operating experience, and manufacturing scale.

Second, shut the export door once domestic capacity is large enough.
China can impose a hard weight cap or a tax high enough to make exporting uneconomic.
Maxinomics says this happened in late 2010.
Almost overnight, foreign buyers could access only a thin slice of former supply.
Prices outside China roughly quadrupled, while domestic prices stayed low.

Third, wait while the price wedge relocates demand and knowledge.
Foreign motor, phone, car, and magnet makers pay more for inputs than Chinese rivals.
Domestic factories can undercut them, win orders, and demand better magnets, unusual
shapes, and tighter specifications from Chinese processors.
Repeated production builds skill.

Foreign companies that cannot export semifinished metal then commission Chinese plants.
They provide drawings, quantities, exact recipes, and hard-won Detroit or Tokyo know-how.
Maxinomics says the foreign firms effectively pay to transfer their own secrets.
He compares an iPhone with roughly $9 in raw materials to a finished sale price about
120 times as high: value lies in the product rather than the rock.
In his account, export restrictions force a climb from rock to metal to magnet to product.

Fourth, reopen the door after trade litigation has done its strategic work.
Maxinomics claims such lawsuits and trade fights always arrive, take three to five years,
and are usually lost by China.
China need not win: in those three to five years, foreign firms bleed from high inputs
and low-priced competition while Chinese plants absorb skills, knowledge, and data.

High prices also induce mines and refiners in Canada, Australia, and the US.
Reopening then floods the world with supply priced below what entrants spend to mine rock,
even though China is selling finished material.
Maxinomics says company after company fails.
China also consolidates dozens or hundreds of domestic firms into two state champions:
China Northern Rare Earth Group and China Rare Earth Group.

This mechanism links scale, supply-chain depth, exports, and industrial policy.
It is Maxinomics' causal account, not a causal finding established by evidence supplied
with the video; the transcript gives examples but no comparative cost or firm-level data.

## Military technology and the narrowest chokepoint

Maxinomics says the strategically alarming monopoly is not every rare earth.
It is a small subset associated with southern China's sticky red clay, unlike northern ore.
The description names dysprosium and samarium among the key military materials.
The detailed missile segment focuses on samarium.

A cruise missile can fly low for hundreds or perhaps thousands of miles.
It reads terrain and adjusts fins one degree at a time to enter a window or bunker.
At each fin, permanent magnets sit inside a copper coil.
Current rotates a connected rod and then continuously holds it in position.
Because holding converts energy into heat without mechanical movement, magnets weaken.
Enough heat can permanently destroy a magnetic pole.

The video invokes the first law of thermodynamics: energy is transferred or converted,
not created or destroyed.
A ceramic magnet contains no rare earths, but Maxinomics says matching a neodymium
magnet's power requires three times the weight and three times the size.
If a magnet loses about 50% of its power after one hour of flight, doubling it would be
hard to fit into a Tomahawk and would demand testing and certification for a worse missile.

The video says 100 Tomahawks were fired in the first 24 hours of the Gulf War.
They struck Iraqi command centers, air defenses, and power stations before ground troops,
reaching heavily defended targets without risking a pilot.
Although most Tomahawk information is classified, Maxinomics says each needs at least
12 magnets to steer.

Samarium is presented as the heat-resistant ingredient used by the US military.
Maxinomics says it reduces the temperature problem by 95%.
He says China controls 99% of samarium processing, versus 94% for magnet rare earths.
Samarium allegedly needs about one-third more processing steps than neodymium, costs
12 times as much to refine, and occurs in fewer places.
These technical and market figures are asserted, not substantiated in the supplied text.

The video says China fully banned samarium exports in response to tariffs.
The automatic caption says "terrorists," an evident ambiguity that cannot be safely
resolved from the transcript alone; the surrounding discussion and description identify
a 2025 magnet cutoff and tariffs, respectively.
China's quoted rationale said the measure reflected its consistent stance on
"upholding world peace and regional stability."
Maxinomics interprets the reference to peace as an acknowledgment of military use.

## From commercial concentration to geopolitical leverage

For at least two decades, Maxinomics says Pentagon and congressional-subcommittee voices
warned that China could someday cut off critical minerals.
The risk remained a suspicion because it was unclear whether China would act on it.
The apparent bargain gave China pollution and manufacturing while the West got cleaner
industry, lost manufacturing, and accepted dependence.
Each side seemed to gain and lose something.

Maxinomics compares tolerated market power with De Beers, "Luxodica" in the captions,
ASML, and Bloomberg Terminal.
The caption says "Luxodica" makes 95% of all regular glasses and sunglasses.
The description provides no correction, so the uncertain name is retained as captioned.
ASML is described as making the machines that make semiconductors.
The video also says Ray-Bans that cost $7 to make sell for $200.

The distinction, for Maxinomics, is using control not merely to earn money but to compel.
He says China crossed that line on April 4, 2025.
The video's quoted news material says 2025 export controls disrupted supply chains and
caused factory shutdowns in the United States.
Maxinomics argues this transformed customers' sense of dependence into that of hostages
and validated claims of a deliberate strategy for international control.

He nevertheless states a major caveat: US semiconductor restrictions provoked China.
The US began restricting China's access to leading semiconductors around 2016,
tightened controls substantially in 2019, expanded them in 2020, and imposed large
tariffs in 2025.
Maxinomics says there is "no question" that these actions were provocative.

He contrasts this with what he calls the organic development of the earlier US chip
industry: hundreds of competing firms, no government barrier to foreign winners, and no
state backstop.
Intel was "crushed" over the prior two decades, he says, and Japan and Taiwan hollowed
out the broader US chip industry in the captioned "90s and 2000s."
The US government did not stop that decline or intentionally build dominance to withdraw it.
That contrast is Maxinomics' interpretation, not independently demonstrated here.

Maxinomics calls rare-earth controls the most extreme US-China trade move of the past
decade because revealing a monopoly has no long-term upside.
He says such leverage is normally saved as a last resort when war is beginning.
Using it earlier motivates customers to eliminate the chokepoint.
That is both the geopolitical power of concentrated supply and the risk of deploying it.

## The response: state-backed diversification

Japan, Australia, and especially the US are said to be taking steps to break the monopoly.
The Pentagon has agreed to guarantee companies $110 per kilogram of rare earths,
above the market price, so Chinese oversupply cannot destroy US producers.
This resembles US support for aluminum aircraft capacity around World War II,
when the airplane era began and adequate production did not yet exist.

The rare-earth arrangement goes further by guaranteeing both price and purchase volume.
The source calls it "take or pay."
If the market price exceeds $110, the US government receives 30% of the difference
between $110 and the higher price on other sales to other buyers.
The upside-sharing and guarantees run for 10 years.

The deal principally extends the Mountain Pass story.
The mine is operating again, now both mining and processing and refining rare earths.
It is also building an advanced processing facility in Texas.
Maxinomics says these are the steps needed to restart the industry but asks whether they
will work and answers only, "We'll see."
The arrangement is uncommon in the US, though not unprecedented.

## Energy security, domestic constraints, and strategic risk

In the source's framework, energy security is really electrification-component security.
Permanent magnets enable compact motors in EVs and many other electric machines.
Control of separation, refining, magnet production, and manufacturing know-how can make
foreign energy and defense systems dependent even when mineral deposits exist elsewhere.
That is the closest the evidence comes to something "better than oil": a chokepoint in
high-value electric and military equipment, not a replacement fuel.

China's advantages also create domestic liabilities.
Maxinomics identifies thorium-bearing waste, toxic pools, weak legal recourse, risky
protest, cheap state credit, politically allocated land, and projects insulated from
short-term profit discipline.
He does not quantify health damage, cleanup liabilities, subsidy costs, overcapacity,
local debt, power demand, labor conditions, or public opposition.

Foreign dependence creates exposure to export caps, taxes, bans, price discrimination,
factory shutdowns, military shortages, and coerced policy choices.
China's exposure runs in the opposite direction: restrictions encourage substitution,
new capacity, guaranteed-price competitors, and coordinated action by Japan, Australia,
and the US.
Alternatives can be larger, heavier, less efficient, more expensive, and slow to certify,
but the source does not establish that they are impossible.

## What the source establishes—and what it does not

The source offers a coherent thematic argument connecting mineral processing,
manufacturing scale, supply chains, industrial policy, exports, and strategic leverage.
Its concrete narrative spans GM, Hitachi Metals, Magnequench, Mountain Pass, Baotou,
China Northern Rare Earth Group, China Rare Earth Group, Intel, ASML, De Beers,
Japan, Taiwan, Australia, Canada, the Congo, China, and the United States.
It also connects motors, AirPods, iPhones, laptops, hard drives, refrigerators, EVs,
radar, aircraft, semiconductors, ceramic magnets, and Tomahawk missiles.

What it does not establish is equally important.
It does not show that rare earths have replaced oil, or compare their revenues, volumes,
energy density, trade, or macroeconomic influence.
It does not substantively explain wind, batteries, or their material dependencies.
It mentions solar only as an asserted parallel and EVs mainly as magnet applications.
It provides no full clean-energy balance, no lifecycle analysis, and no emissions result.
It supplies no citations or underlying datasets for its market shares, prices, or costs.
It does not prove every acquisition, export measure, or industrial outcome was one unified
plan, nor rule out market learning, domestic demand, ordinary scale effects, or other causes.
It does not establish that US diversification will succeed, only that it has begun.

Accordingly, the defensible conclusion is not the headline's literal oil replacement.
It is Maxinomics' narrower claim that command over difficult midstream processing and
high-value manufacturing can convert widespread minerals into concentrated economic and
geopolitical power—and that exercising that power can accelerate efforts to escape it.
