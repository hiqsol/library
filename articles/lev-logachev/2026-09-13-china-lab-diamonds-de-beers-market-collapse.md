# How China and Lab-Grown Diamonds Broke the Scarcity Model

## A note on the source

This article explains the argument made by Lev Logachev in his Russian-language video of
September 13, 2026. It is based on Russian automatic captions, which may contain errors.
Except where a passage is explicitly identified as interpretation or a transcript caveat,
the historical, market, technical, regulatory, and causal claims below are Logachev's and
have not been independently verified. Terms such as “diamond” also shift in the captions
between rough crystals and polished gems; this article distinguishes them where context
allows.

Logachev's central thesis is that the diamond business did not simply discover scarcity. It
organized scarcity, protected it through distribution, and taught consumers what that
scarcity was supposed to mean. China then built an industrial system able to reproduce the
same material at falling cost. Once a visually similar product could be manufactured at
scale, both the controlled supply and the cultural story supporting natural-diamond prices
became harder to defend.

## Scarcity was a system, not just a geological fact

In Logachev's account, De Beers built the old model by controlling how many rough diamonds
reached buyers. The captions truncate the company's founding year to “188…”. The likely
intended date is 1888, but the missing digit is not recoverable from the transcript itself.
Logachev attributes the early strategy to Cecil Rhodes: buy South African mines, consolidate
production, and translate control of supply into control of price.

At one point, Logachev says, De Beers controlled almost 90% of the world's rough-diamond
supply. It allegedly kept millions of carats of already-mined stones in vaults rather than
letting excess inventory depress prices. When other producers appeared in Russia,
Australia, or Canada, De Beers would try to purchase their output or incorporate it into its
distribution system. Logachev further claims that an independent seller could be disciplined
by a temporary release of De Beers inventory, lowering prices until the rival's business
became uneconomic.

The sales channel reinforced that leverage. Selected buyers, known as sightholders, were
invited several times a year to closed “sights.” According to Logachev, each buyer saw a box
of stones at a price fixed in advance. The choice was to take the whole box or leave it;
frequent refusal could mean losing a future invitation. De Beers therefore influenced the
quantity offered, who could buy it, and the terms of sale.

This was more than mine ownership. It was an inventory, access, and expectations regime.
The system could absorb surpluses instead of allowing them to reveal a lower clearing price.
Its vulnerability was that supply discipline could not by itself create demand.

## Advertising manufactured the engagement rule

Logachev argues that the diamond engagement ring was not an ancient or universal custom.
In the 1930s, amid the Great Depression, buyers in many countries might give gold rings,
rubies, sapphires, or other stones. A proposal did not automatically require a diamond.

In 1938, according to Logachev, De Beers hired an American advertising agency whose name is
garbled in the captions as “NWI,” probably N. W. Ayer & Son. The campaign placed diamonds
on Hollywood stars, turned celebrity engagements and large rings into magazine stories, and
presented the stone as the supreme symbol of luxury. In 1947 came “A Diamond Is Forever,”
which Logachev calls one of the twentieth century's best-known advertising slogans.

The message worked on both demand and recirculated supply. By linking a diamond to eternal
love, advertising made a serious stone appear necessary for a serious proposal. It also made
resale feel emotionally improper. Logachev argues that millions of previously sold stones
therefore remained in family jewelry boxes instead of competing with new inventory.

Later advice told buyers to spend several months' salaries on an engagement ring, although
the captions give neither an exact number nor a date. Logachev says the same model was
exported: diamond engagement rings were rare in Japan in the 1960s but normal a few decades
later, while campaigns also targeted China, India, and other growing markets. The formula
was mutually reinforcing: associate diamonds with love, make purchase socially obligatory,
and restrict new supply so prices remain high.

## The substitute is not an imitation

The disruption matters because a laboratory-grown diamond is not glass or cubic zirconia.
Logachev says it has the same carbon crystal structure and hardness, with practically the
same physical and optical properties as a mined stone. An ordinary buyer cannot identify
its origin by appearance, and even an experienced gemologist cannot reliably do so with a
loupe alone; specialized instruments are needed. Growth-related signatures may permit
instrumental identification, but that is different from visible imitation.

Logachev describes two main production routes:

- **HPHT — high pressure, high temperature.** A small seed crystal, approximately the size
  of a grain of rice, sits with a carbon source, usually graphite, and a metal catalyst.
  Six pistons compress the capsule from all sides. At tens of thousands of atmospheres and
  high temperature, carbon migrates to the seed and forms a diamond lattice layer by layer.
- **CVD — chemical vapor deposition.** A seed is placed in a vacuum chamber containing
  hydrogen and methane. Microwaves turn the gas into plasma; methane breaks apart, and
  carbon settles on the seed “like invisible snow.” Logachev says growth takes several
  weeks and does not require the immense pressures of HPHT.

The exact HPHT temperature must not be inferred from these captions: the displayed value is
corrupted as “1лу°.” The captions also briefly say “HPH,” but immediately expand the term as
“high pressure, high temperature,” making HPHT the evident intended acronym.

Logachev locates the first machine-made diamonds at General Electric on December 16, 1954.
He says physicist Tracy Hall used a massive press, tens of thousands of atmospheres, and
several thousand degrees to turn graphite into small crystals, compressing a process framed
as taking nature millions or billions of years. According to Logachev, Hall received only a
$10 savings bond. The early output served drilling tools, saws, grinding wheels, and cutters,
not rings. Yet the key barrier had fallen: industry could make diamond without a mine.

## Henan turned synthesis into an industrial cluster

Logachev says China made its first synthetic diamond in 1963 under “Project 121,” becoming
the fifth country to do so after the United States, Sweden, South Africa, and the Soviet
Union. He frames the program as industrial self-reliance: China needed superhard material
for heavy industry and did not want to depend on Western supplies.

Henan Province became the center of the six-sided-press ecosystem. Within Henan, Zhecheng
County is Logachev's emblem of cluster economics. In the early 1980s it was an agricultural
area known for chili peppers. One of its first small diamond-press factories opened in 1983.
Cheap land and labor, nearby equipment and material suppliers, and supportive local
authorities allegedly drew more factories. Industrial land and infrastructure accelerated
the process; by the early 2000s, Logachev says, dozens of enterprises operated there.

For years these plants mainly produced diamond powder and small yellow or brown industrial
crystals. The shift to jewelry quality came through cumulative engineering rather than one
breakthrough: more precise presses, tighter control of pressure and temperature, better
seed crystals, and improved catalysts. The stones became larger, cleaner, more transparent,
and suitable for cutting as gems.

Logachev says China produced about 22 million carats of gem-quality laboratory-grown diamond
in 2023, nearly half of it in Zhecheng alone. He also claims that the county produces more
synthetic diamond than South Africa, Botswana, and Russia combined, but the captions do not
say whether this comparison concerns mass, carats, units, industrial material, gem-quality
output, or like-for-like categories. In 2025, he says, Henan companies displayed a crystal
weighing more than 156 carats, grown in an industrial press at a facility operating hundreds
of similar machines.

His causal interpretation is important: China did not build this base to destroy De Beers.
It spent decades developing superhard materials; jewelry disruption was “collateral damage”
after the factories learned to make large, clear stones. That account of Chinese intent is
Logachev's interpretation, not a verified policy statement.

## China grows; Surat cuts; rich markets buy

Manufacturing a rough crystal does not finish a jewel. Logachev describes a cross-border
value chain in which Henan grows the stones and Surat, India, cuts and polishes them. Surat
has, he says, been the world capital of cutting for decades, employing hundreds of thousands
of people. Material that once came mainly from African and Russian mines increasingly
arrives from Henan, then leaves as polished stones for New York, London, Dubai, Hong Kong,
and other affluent markets.

The caption says the stone travels “1,000 km” from China to Surat, but this looks corrupted;
the spoken phrase may mean “thousands of kilometers.” It should not be treated as a reliable
exact distance.

Surat's role reveals why the substitution can move quickly. The same cutting ecosystem can
process either origin. The decisive distinction lies upstream: natural supply emerges from
an industry designed to restrict volume, while laboratory supply emerges from manufacturing,
where every added press expands capacity, process improvement lowers cost, and new entrants
increase competition.

## Prices collapsed because reproducibility replaced rarity

Logachev gives several dramatic, but not fully reconciled, price series:

- A wholesale 1-carat laboratory-grown stone cost about $1,800 in 2018 and about $80 per
  carat by 2025, which he describes as a 96% fall.
- Elsewhere, a wholesale 1-carat laboratory-grown polished diamond is said to have cost as
  much as $4,000 in 2015 and $300–$500 “today,” a decline of more than 90% in ten years.
- Later, uncut laboratory-grown stones are quoted at roughly $300 per carat on the wholesale
  market.
- De Beers launched Lightbox around $800 per carat in 2018; Logachev says De Beers later
  acknowledged that wholesale lab-grown prices had fallen almost 90% since launch.

These figures may describe different qualities, dates, sellers, stages of processing, or
datasets. The transcript does not reconcile them, and the $80 figure cannot simply be merged
with the $300–$500 range or the $300 rough-stone figure.

The market-share claims are similarly specific but unverified here. Logachev says lab-grown
stones rose from 1% of global polished-diamond sales in 2015 to 20% in 2024. In the United
States, he puts their share at 12% in 2019 and about half of diamonds sold in 2024. He later
says they appear in approximately half of new U.S. engagement rings; “all diamonds sold” and
“new engagement rings” are not necessarily the same denominator.

Resale exposes the new economics most clearly. A lab-grown stone purchased for $3,000 can
later compete with a new stone of the same size and quality costing several times less. A
jeweler has little reason to repurchase the old stone at a high price when a cheaper one is
available directly from a producer. Logachev says some jewelers refuse such buybacks
entirely.

Natural stones are not immune. Logachev first says a comparable 1-carat natural polished
diamond fell from about $6,000 in 2021 to “2…” today, with the remainder lost from the
automatic caption; approximately $2,000 is plausible from his “more than half” statement,
but is not recoverable as an exact quote. Later he gives $4,200 as the subsequent price for
a stone that cost $6,000 in 2021. He also cites an unidentified major jeweler's estimate that
natural diamonds lost almost half their value over several years. These natural-diamond
figures conflict or use undisclosed quality and dataset assumptions, so they should not be
presented as one consistent index.

The broader logic, in Logachev's telling, is clearer than the datasets. A manufactured stone
behaves like an industrial commodity: efficiency and capacity push the marginal price down.
That also weakens the investment story for ordinary natural stones. The customer seeking
appearance can pay several times less for lab-grown; the customer seeking a store of value
may find gold easier to understand.

## Lightbox helped expose the category problem

Logachev links a regulatory and strategic turning point to 2018. He says the U.S. Federal
Trade Commission removed “natural” from the definition of diamond. Laboratory-grown stones
still had to be disclosed as lab-grown or lab-created, but were no longer framed merely as
imitations. This regulatory account has not been independently checked here.

That same year, De Beers launched Lightbox. According to Logachev, the goal was to preserve a
category boundary: natural diamonds would remain serious, permanent luxury for engagements
and weddings, while lab-grown stones would be inexpensive fashion jewelry. The fixed price
of about $800 per carat was supposed to advertise the difference in status.

Instead, Logachev argues, the campaign advertised substitutability. Consumers saw two
outwardly similar stones and asked why one should cost several times more. Lab-grown stones
entered engagement rings—the category De Beers wanted to reserve for natural gems. In 2025,
Logachev says, De Beers announced Lightbox's closure after laboratory wholesale prices had
fallen almost 90% since its launch. The attempt to label the product a cheap novelty helped
show that physical diamond itself could be cheap.

## China also weakened the demand side

China appears twice in Logachev's argument: as a vast new source of supply and as a formerly
important buyer reducing demand for traditional stones. He attributes weaker Chinese buying
to fewer marriages, the real-estate crisis's effects on household wealth and confidence,
and younger consumers' caution about expensive luxury.

The captions garble a Chinese phrase that Logachev glosses as “IQ tax,” apparently referring
to a purchase thought overpriced because advertising persuaded the buyer. In that climate,
a natural diamond sits awkwardly between a much cheaper, visually similar lab-grown stone
and gold as a more legible store of value. The claimed causes and cultural interpretation
are Logachev's, and the garbled phrase should not be treated as a secure transcription.

This two-sided pressure matters. More Chinese presses expand supply while softer Chinese
purchases reduce demand. The old scarcity model is squeezed at both ends.

## Electronics may matter more than jewelry

The same industrial base has uses unrelated to romance. Logachev says diamond conducts heat
about five times better than copper and thirteen times better than silicon. He argues that
as processors become more powerful, removing heat becomes a limiting engineering problem.
Synthetic-diamond heat spreaders or heat sinks can move heat away from high-performance
chips, with such solutions already used or being developed for power electronics.

Those technical ratios and application claims are Logachev's and have not been independently
verified here. His strategic inference is conditional: if diamond components become common
in next-generation electronics, China already possesses the presses, suppliers, skills, and
production cluster required to scale them. Jewelry may therefore be only one market served
by an industrial capability built around a functional material.

## The shock reaches national budgets

For Botswana, Logachev says diamonds provide roughly one-third of government revenue and
about three-quarters of foreign-exchange earnings. He connects its pressure to weaker
natural-diamond demand, reduced Chinese buying, and lab-grown substitution. In March 2026,
he says, S&P again lowered Botswana's sovereign credit rating; the captions render the name
as “SNP.” He also says the main diamond company had cut production and that the economy had
been under severe pressure for two consecutive years. These market, fiscal, rating, and
causal claims remain unverified in this article.

Russia is exposed through ALROSA and Yakutia. Logachev says ALROSA mined 30 million carats in
2025, about 30% of world production and 90% of Russian production by the company's own
estimate. Planned 2026 output was approximately 25 million carats. He lists weak global
demand, sanctions, restricted market access, a strong ruble, high interest rates, and
inflation alongside Chinese laboratory-grown competition. The synthetic challenge is
structural, he argues, because manufacturing costs can continue to fall.

The regional transmission mechanism is unusually direct. Over ten years, Logachev says,
ALROSA-group companies transferred 630 billion rubles to Yakutia and its district budgets,
averaging 35% of the republic's own-source revenue—roughly every third ruble. Yakutia and
eight diamond-mining uluses together own 33% of ALROSA. Falling diamond income can therefore
reach salaries, schools, hospitals, roads, and regional ownership returns. Logachev also
makes an unsupported allegation that some public money is misappropriated; that allegation
is his and is not substantiated by the video segment.

Logachev says Russia responded with sales rules adopted in summer 2026 and effective
September 1, 2026. Under his account, an artificial cut stone could no longer be called a
“brilliant”; use of the natural material's name required “synthetic”; traditional natural
quality and color classifications were barred; and weight had to be stated in grams rather
than carats. The wording around classification is garbled in the captions, and the entire
regulatory account requires independent confirmation. He says Deputy Finance Minister
Alexey Moiseev proposed promoting these rules internationally as best practice, while Russia
sought coordination with other mining states to protect industrial capacity and local
communities.

## What actually broke

Logachev's argument is not that natural diamond ceased to exist or that mining vanished. It
is that physical identity and commercial scarcity came apart. De Beers could manage mined
inventory and social expectations, but it could not permanently monopolize the production
of carbon crystals once HPHT and CVD became scalable manufacturing processes.

Henan's presses undermine controlled supply. Surat makes the substitute compatible with the
existing cutting and retail chain. Falling resale prices undermine the wealth-preservation
story. Lightbox demonstrated the price gap while failing to keep lab-grown stones out of
engagements. Softer Chinese demand weakens the old luxury market from the other side.
Electronics gives producers a reason to keep improving the technology even if jewelry prices
continue to fall.

The result, in Logachev's framing, is a collision between two economic models. Botswana,
ALROSA, and Yakutia depend on the value of natural scarcity. China benefits from learning to
reproduce the material more cheaply and at greater scale. Every additional press does more
than create another stone: it makes the old claim—that the material itself must be rare and
expensive—harder to sustain.
