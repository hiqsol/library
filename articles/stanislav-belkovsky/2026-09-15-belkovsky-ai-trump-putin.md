# AI, Power, Trump, and the Future of Putin’s System

## Source and interpretive frame

This article explains an interview with political commentator Stanislav Belkovsky.
It does not endorse or independently verify his factual claims, allegations, or predictions.
The Russian-language interview appeared on *I Gryanul Grem* on September 15, 2026.
It runs 54 minutes and 38 seconds and is available at
[YouTube](https://youtu.be/aoVWkujPcsQ?is=IPynSrYbK8-Ps9jh).
The source notes derive from auto-captions, so uncertain names and phrases remain flagged.

Belkovsky’s argument connects artificial intelligence, religion, political economy,
nuclear weapons, Donald Trump, and the durability of Vladimir Putin’s system.
His central claim is not that AI will independently conquer humanity. It is that people
who own AI systems can turn fear, data, and scarce physical resources into political power.
He places that thesis inside a larger story: secular society keeps creating substitutes for
God, elites privatize common inheritances, and rulers demand control without responsibility.

## AI apocalypse as an old religious story

The interviewer begins from a thought attributed to Arthur Schopenhauer: people mistake the
limits of their own vision for the limits of the world. Superintelligence could therefore
look like an apocalypse because human comprehension ends where a greater intelligence begins.

Belkovsky rejects the idea that this fear is historically new. He says expectations of an
imminent end have accompanied humanity for centuries. In his psychology, people fear life
as much as death because life exposes them to innumerable risks that they cannot control.
Those who consider themselves exceptional, especially technology billionaires, may feel that
ordinary mortality and ordinary probability should not apply to them.

He interprets elite survival plans through that lens. He says Peter Thiel helped popularize
New Zealand refuges about 15 years earlier and that attention later shifted toward Argentina.
He speculates about the Falkland Islands, the Drake Passage, Antarctica, and Elon Musk’s Mars
ambitions as versions of escape. These geopolitical connections are Belkovsky’s conjectures,
not established facts. His moral point is that powerful people plan to save themselves rather
than use their influence to prevent catastrophe for everyone.

For Belkovsky, this survivalism reflects a religious vacuum. He asserts that humans possess
an unconscious knowledge of God, while Enlightenment culture teaches them to deny it.
The vacated position is then filled by the self, dictators, infallible doctrines, aliens, or
technology. AI is only the latest substitute deity. Because humans made it, he says, it lacks
the transcendent origin that would make it divine.

The cultural prototypes are the Golem, Mary Shelley’s unnamed creature in *Frankenstein*,
and Karel Čapek’s robots. Each tells a story in which humanity creates a force it can no
longer govern. Belkovsky also invokes the Soviet film *Teens in the Universe*, although the
auto-captions leave some of its terminology uncertain. He treats these monsters as products
of a society that has lost God and now fears its own creations.

## Fear as the technomagnates’ political instrument

Belkovsky names Sam Altman, Elon Musk, and the Amodeis, likely Dario and Daniela, as members
of a very small class of AI owners. He says the Amodeis formulate risks more sharply and
links the elaborate character of Anthropic and Claude to what he calls their humanities or
philological background. That educational description should not be treated as verified.
His joke that Musk “never attended school” is comic denigration, not biography.

In Belkovsky’s account, the industry’s message follows three steps. First, developers present
AI as a terrifying force that may escape control. Second, they claim that only its creators
can restrain it. Third, they request exceptional authority and access to resources in return.
He describes the Amodeis’ version as a psychotherapeutic maneuver: since the public fears
them, it should give them more power so that it can fear them less.

His harsher analogy is an organized-crime protection racket. A small gang threatens a victim;
a criminal authority then offers protection for 30 percent. Likewise, he says, AI owners
warn that their creation may devour society and offer rescue in exchange for control.

Belkovsky denies that AI is an autonomous political subject. Nuclear weapons do not know
that they exist, and he regards claims that AI will become self-aware as a science-fiction
plot rather than demonstrated reality. Both nuclear arms and AI are dangerous as instruments
in human hands. If a technology is truly intolerable, he says, society should abolish it.
If its owners resist, the relevant question is what interest their resistance serves.

## The physical economy beneath the digital vision

The argument then moves from myth to material limits. AI may appear virtual, but data centers
need land, water, energy, environmental capacity, and hyperscale infrastructure. Biological
human beings also cannot simply be digitized out of physical existence. Belkovsky says AI
firms want control over common natural resources as well as common intellectual resources.

He cites protests against data centers and resistance in both Democratic New York and
Republican Texas under Governor Greg Abbott. In his interpretation, the bipartisan reaction
comes from a basic intuition of fairness: why should a tiny private group receive shared
resources at little or no cost? The examples are his account and require outside checking.

Belkovsky also claims that Donald Trump failed to secure abundant cheap energy, that prices
rose amid what he calls Trump’s war with Iran, and that efforts to overturn environmental
constraints failed. These are interview-era political assertions, not verified background.
They support his contention that AI development has slowed because its resource base and
business model are inadequate, not because executives voluntarily chose moral restraint.

His quantitative forecast is stark. He says AI companies would need revenue to grow by tens
of times within five years to avoid a bubble like the dot-com crash. He sees too little
paying demand to make that possible. If firms paid market prices for all the knowledge and
physical inputs they consume, he argues, AI would never become self-financing.

Calls to slow AI are therefore, in his interpretation, efforts to disguise scarcity and
protect valuations. A burst bubble would destroy the quasi-sacred aura around AI, making it
hard to recover. Political authority offers an alternative: firms could redistribute global
resources, subsidize their costs, and preserve both solvency and power.

Belkovsky broadens this into a theory of empire. AI does not favor many small competitors;
it favors a handful of giants able to concentrate capital and infrastructure. He compares
them with nineteenth-century empires and predicts that such AI empires may eventually clash
because each will regard the world’s division as unjust. He imagines peripheral zones such
as Putin’s Russia supplying resources where ordinary rules can be suspended.

A brief exchange about bears and orcas is partly opaque because the visual context is missing.
Belkovsky jokes that Russia is a “bear corner,” fitting the ruling party’s symbol, and casts
orcas as hostile creatures that eat polar bears. The joke should not bear interpretive weight.
The durable point is that digital empires still depend on mines, energy, water, and compliant
territorial rulers.

## General Intellect and the return of the left

To explain ownership, Belkovsky revives Karl Marx’s term “General Intellect.” He uses it for
knowledge accumulated over thousands of years, from the Sumerians through Plato and
Aristotle to modern contributors. In his account, AI companies acquired that inheritance
without compensating humanity and now sell it back as both a service and an instrument of
rule. He calls this potentially the greatest theft in history.

He argues that big data, rather than code alone, gives AI its value. Drawing on his past as a
systems programmer, he minimizes programming’s mystical status: code can be written by
organized teams, but the collective inheritance cannot be recreated on demand. AI ownership
therefore privatizes both the intellectual commons and the natural commons required to run it.

That contradiction, he predicts, will revive left-wing and Marxian politics. He names Bernie
Sanders and, almost certainly, Zohran Mamdani; the latter name is distorted in the captions.
He also mentions a Sanders proposal to prohibit artificial superintelligence, which he calls
poorly prepared but conceptually timely. The precise proposal needs independent confirmation.

By a “new communist revolution,” Belkovsky does not mean a Soviet dictatorship or a seizure
of one national government. He means a renewed demand to decide who owns collective knowledge
and why a tiny group can use it to seek political domination. Republicans and Democrats may
reach that question from different directions because, he says, the instinct for justice
survives even in people who deny that justice has an objective existence.

He illustrates that intuition with an anecdote attributed to Niels Bohr. Asked why he kept a
lucky horseshoe, the physicist supposedly replied that it worked even for people who did not
believe in it. Belkovsky says justice operates in the same way: disbelief does not extinguish
the impulse to demand it.

## Immortality, human uniqueness, and the purpose of technology

Belkovsky’s political economy remains tied to theology. He says humans possess numinous
experience and a channel to God that AI cannot have. He acknowledges that the argument fails
if God does not exist; under that premise, machine intelligence can appear superior. He also
allows that animals and plants may have analogous capacities that science could explore in
the coming decades.

Technological immortality is another proposed substitute for religion. Belkovsky describes
a promise in which biotechnology and stem cells extend elite life to 120 years, after which
minds are uploaded into robots. If a person is merely transferable information and no soul
exists, he concedes that such a scenario is theoretically imaginable. Yet he questions who
would control it, whether it would be available to everyone, what would happen to the soul,
and whether persistence in a non-biological body would still count as life.

He returns to an old joke about a person who gives up drinking, smoking, sex, and other
pleasures in order to live to 120. The doctor asks why such a life should be prolonged.
Belkovsky directs the same question at Thiel and other longevity seekers. Power and money,
he argues, can make their owners hostages to the resources they accumulate rather than happy.

The larger test is purpose. He asks whether nuclear weapons made humanity happier and rejects
the idea that every technical achievement justifies itself. He invokes the Einstein letter
to Franklin D. Roosevelt but says the U.S. bomb was not ready when Hitler was defeated.
In his religious reading, providence worked through errors in the German nuclear program.
The captioned date “1643” is almost certainly 1943, and the historical claims need checking.

Nuclear weapons also illustrate asymmetric power. Belkovsky says established democracies fear
mass death, whereas authoritarian or criminal regimes may be willing to sacrifice millions.
That makes the same weapon, in his view, a stronger instrument of blackmail for Putin,
Pakistan, or a potentially nuclear Iran. He cites North Korea as a case where nuclear arms
protected rulers from external overthrow. These are his political interpretations.

Progress is therefore a double-edged sword. Belkovsky dismisses destroying Earth so that
“apple trees” might someday grow on Mars. He portrays post-disaster Chernobyl as an earthly
Eden where forests and animals flourished after people withdrew. His ecological conclusion
is that balance may require non-intervention rather than ever greater extraction.

## Putin’s system did not simply escape its creators

The interviewer tests the runaway-creation analogy through Boris Berezovsky and Vladimir
Putin. If Berezovsky helped create Putin, did the dictator escape his maker as AI might?
Belkovsky rejects the premise. He says Berezovsky played a role but that Boris Yeltsin’s
family, especially Valentin Yumashev and Tatyana Dyachenko/Yumasheva, was decisive.

Belkovsky goes further and alleges that members of that circle possessed the means to arrange
the Chechen war that enabled Putin’s rise. This is a grave, unverified allegation and should
not be read as established history. His analytical conclusion is that Putin did not broadly
betray his actual sponsors. Their interests remained protected, while Berezovsky overestimated
his importance and failed to see that the Yeltsin family wanted him removed.

Only three months before Putin became Russia’s most popular politician, Belkovsky says, the
project still looked implausible. He calls the resulting leader a reliable Golem rather than
a rebel. Putin preserved the main settlement of the 1990s, particularly the outcomes of
privatization, even though later war and stagnation imposed indirect costs on insiders.

Belkovsky says Berezovsky sold assets at market prices in 2000, regretted missing their later
rise, and then pursued a hopeless case against Roman Abramovich. He notes that the British
judge distrusted Berezovsky’s testimony. The likely case and judge should be verified before
adding details beyond the interview.

The broader elite, in his telling, survived. He names Vladimir Potanin, Alexei Mordashov,
Mikhail Fridman, Roman Abramovich, and Alisher Usmanov. A captioned “Oleg Perov” may mean
Oleg Deripaska, but the identification is uncertain. Belkovsky also makes vague allegations
about hostages, Azerbaijan, and sanctions relief; the captions do not support a safe factual
reconstruction, so those claims cannot be repeated as fact.

Punishment was selective. Belkovsky says Vladimir Gusinsky opposed Putin’s faction,
Berezovsky lost political influence, and Mikhail Khodorkovsky acted as an independent
political force. Those who violated the system’s rules could be crushed, while major insiders
who accepted them retained wealth and status. In this account, the old oligarchic order did
not disappear; it became the social foundation of Putinism.

Drawing on Hegel’s “cunning of reason,” Belkovsky gives Putin an unintended historical role:
to reveal the moral and intellectual bankruptcy of the Russian elite formed since the 1990s.
Systemic liberals praised Putin for shielding privatization from popular revision. The later
war against Ukraine is, in Belkovsky’s view, meaningful to Putin personally but irrational
from the system’s own interest. Putin remains human, not a robot, and therefore cannot be
reduced to the AI analogy.

## The Russian 1990s as a warning for global AI

Belkovsky finally makes the Putin argument part of his AI argument. In the 1990s, Russians
asked how assets created by collective labor became the property of a narrow group. He recalls
economist Yevgeny Yasin replying, approximately, that nothing could have been taken from
citizens because they owned nothing.

Belkovsky now calls that answer a manipulation. Soviet citizens, he argues, held an implicit
claim on common assets. In that sense, he and Abramovich began with equal moral claims to what
many generations had built. He admits that he once accepted concentrated ownership as unfair
but tolerable, provided everyone kept opportunities for advancement.

He later concluded that those opportunities were illusory and that concentrated ownership
led structurally toward a Putin-type dictatorship. Putin was not personally inevitable;
someone else could have filled the role. The concentration of property and power created the
political destination. He sees AI repeating that pattern globally through the privatization
of knowledge, infrastructure, and authority.

## Trump as symptom, transition, and exposure

The interview’s Trump section begins with an unclear allegation about Kremlin-linked access
to Trump’s family through a wedding payment of hundreds of thousands of dollars. Belkovsky
says Trump’s circle is unusually receptive to freebies regardless of their size. He retells
an anecdote in which Steve Witkoff supposedly entered Trump’s orbit by buying him something
like McDonald’s and Coca-Cola because Trump carried no cash. The story is unverified.

Belkovsky compares this personality type with Osip in Nikolai Gogol’s *The Government
Inspector*, who advises taking even a spare piece of string. He also vaguely recalls a proposal
to sell advance access to Trump’s social posts, but explicitly says he does not remember
whether it happened. Captions render another figure as Umar Kremlev; Belkovsky alleges that
this person gained influence over the Trump family. The identity and allegation are uncertain.

Yet Belkovsky gives Trump an objective historical role distinct from Trump’s intentions.
He says Trump is closing the American-centered world order and exposing the exhaustion of the
Enlightenment nation-state. Trump may also, intentionally or not, rehabilitate traditional
religion. Belkovsky invokes Goethe’s *Faust*: a force intending evil may still produce good.

Trump’s vanity, pettiness, and refusal of accountability also discredit his own model.
Belkovsky predicts that its obsolescence will become visible in the nearest elections, though
he does not specify which ones. If Trump were twice as technically competent and less eager
to proclaim his genius, Belkovsky says, the project would be more dangerous because its
mechanisms would be harder to see.

Elite irresponsibility connects Trump to Benjamin Netanyahu, Putin, and the technomagnates.
Belkovsky claims Trump blames Volodymyr Zelensky for fuel prices that he attributes mainly to
Trump’s Iran war. He also accuses Netanyahu of cultivating Hamas, receiving advance warning
of the October 7, 2023 attack, failing to act, and then evading responsibility. These are
serious allegations made by Belkovsky and are not verified by the interview itself.

His general thesis is that today’s ruling class demands resources while rejecting liability.
He argues that such conduct revives communism more effectively than left-wing rhetoric does.
He criticizes an older left for replacing broad social and class interests with a narrow focus
on transgender rights, while also saying transgender people deserve protection. His point is
that minority rights should not substitute for the social rights of the majority.

Trump thus becomes both symptom and unwitting corrective. He accelerates the decline of an
old order, exposes a new concentration of power, and revives Sanders- and Mamdani-style
questions about ownership. In Belkovsky’s telling, his obvious flaws give humanity a chance
to recognize the mechanism before a more competent leader makes it durable.

## What future does the interview imagine?

Belkovsky’s future is not one in which machines independently rule. It is one in which human
owners use machines to rule other humans unless the public contests ownership and purpose.
The nation-state is fragmenting into hostile “subnations,” while transnational AI empires seek
resources from peripheral territories. A struggle over the General Intellect could produce a
new left, and concentrated ownership could produce new dictatorships or conflicts.

His preferred resolution is religious as well as political. Humanity will stop deifying AI,
he says, when it remembers God as the true superintelligence and recognizes human beings as
participants in a transcendent order. Whether or not one accepts that theology, it structures
his whole analysis: the fear of AI, the fantasy of uploading, the worship of progress, and the
desire for elite immortality are all misplaced attempts to answer religious questions.

The interview closes without resolving those tensions. Asked about a “novel with no ending,”
Belkovsky says everything continues and directs viewers to his restored Telegram channel.
He says the channel had been “kidnapped,” had lost subscribers, and would add new sections,
including a novel or rubric called *Synopsis*; the exact format remains caption-ambiguous.

The most coherent secular core of his argument is a warning about accountability. AI systems
are built from shared knowledge and shared resources but owned by a small number of actors.
Putin’s Russia shows, in his interpretation, how concentrated property can harden into
political hierarchy. Trump shows how a ruler can expose that hierarchy through vanity and
carelessness. The future therefore turns less on whether AI awakens than on whether societies
allow its owners to convert collective inheritance into unanswerable private power.
