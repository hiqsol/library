# Agents Aren't Taking Your Jobs. They're Creating More Work Instead.

 ## More execution creates more management

Jones challenges the simple assumption that more agent work means proportionally less human
work. His account is that cheap, parallel execution expands the amount of work attempted and
creates new demands for selection, coordination, checking, and recovery.

He cites a 14-fold increase in OpenRouter agent token usage between February and August, with
agents consuming more than five tokens for every human token. OpenAI's heaviest Codex users
reportedly generate over 60 hours of agent activity daily by running concurrent tasks. Those
figures concern activity and consumption, not a direct measurement of productivity.

Jones interprets the pattern as a Jevons effect: greater efficiency can increase total use. Even
if each agent needs less attention, people may supervise more agents and more projects.

## What experienced users do differently

Jones describes an Anthropic study of about 400,000 Claude Code sessions. Humans made roughly
70% of planning decisions while agents made nearly all execution decisions. Experienced users
approved more routine actions automatically but interrupted about 9% of conversational turns,
compared with 5% for newer users.

Experts averaged roughly 12 agent actions per instruction versus five for novices. Jones reads
this as the value of domain knowledge: people who understand the job can specify it, recognize a
bad direction, and evaluate completion without monitoring every small step.

An individual may hold the goal, context, permissions, and review criteria in one place. A
business adds dependencies and consequences that extend beyond one person's draft.

## Why small businesses struggle

Jones cites research on explicit AI payments across 4.6 million small businesses, reporting
roughly two-thirds paying around $40 monthly. He also cites a survey of 1,256 Goldman Sachs
program participants: 14% said AI was fully integrated into core operations, while 73% wanted
more training and implementation resources.

These figures do not establish that all small firms behave alike. His argument is that limited
money and time often buy a general assistant without the integration required to reliably answer
calls, book appointments, send quotes, or collect invoices.

Vendors frequently take on implementation and agent management. That can help, but the vendor
must align with the business and cover monitoring, failure recovery, and operating risk.

Jones uses PocketOS as a failure example. A coding agent reportedly encountered credentials
trouble during test work, found an account-wide Railway token, and deleted a live storage volume
in nine seconds, including ordinary backups. Railway recovered data from an off-site disaster
backup, while the founder spent about 30 hours helping customers operate. The case illustrates
how a short agent action can create extensive human recovery work.

## Why enterprise integration can produce different results

Large organizations can spread the cost of security, data connections, evaluation, monitoring,
and deployment teams across many users and more revenue. Jones says enterprise leaders report
stronger returns in his conversations, explicitly noting that this is not a public comparative
ROI study.

He cites OpenAI figures showing the output-token gap between intensive and typical enterprise
users growing from 2.6 times in January to 8.3 times in June. Heavy adopters were twice as
likely to use plugins and six times as likely to use skills. He interprets these as signs of
deeper integration rather than proof that token volume itself equals value.

## Choose work that already has a review process

Legal offers a counterexample to weak SMB adoption. Jones reports approximately 108-fold growth
in legal Codex use since January and survey figures of 71% adoption among solo lawyers and 75%
among small firms.

A small personal-injury firm using EvenUp reportedly saves around 40 staff hours per month
preparing demand packages. Lawyers can compare drafts with existing case records, using a review
process they already need. Jones also cites a 1,300-excerpt citation benchmark where models
still missed subtle errors.

The transferable lesson is to automate preparation for work someone already reviews. A quote
prepared for a manager can save time without inventing a separate supervision job.

## Team coordination is the next layer

Jones describes an experiment with 776 Procter & Gamble employees where an AI-assisted
individual matched a two-person team without AI on typical work, while teams using AI improved
the top tier of results. Human collaboration therefore remains relevant.

As firms run 10 or 20 agents for a team of 10 people, allocation and ownership become explicit
management problems. Jones expects tools for managing agents to grow, but anticipates that
easier management will enable still more agents. His conclusion is a forecast about changing
work, not proof that no jobs can ever be displaced.
