## The Real Problem Isn't Meetings — It's Team Size

Average employees spend 12 hours a week in meetings. People managers: 16 hours.
Executives: 23 hours. Meetings have tripled since 2020. AI note-taking apps are
"barnacles" — they don't fix the root cause. The real problem is team size. Teams
are 3–10x too big, and every AI tool adopted makes it worse by amplifying output
through a coordination structure that is fundamentally broken.

Team size determines Slack channels monitored, approvals waited on, people to
align before shipping. It shapes costs, speed, and decision quality.

## The Number Five

The number of communication pathways between people in a group is defined
mathematically: n(n-1)/2. Five people = 10 pathways (manageable). Ten people = 45
pathways. Twenty people = 190 pathways — a joke.

Robin Dunbar's 1992 research on primate neocortex size established layered limits:
5 for core group, 15 for deep trust, 50 for meaningful working relationships, 150
for stable social connections. Army mathematicians confirmed the pattern
empirically — effectiveness peaks at 5, 15, 50, and 150.

A US infantry fire team is four people plus a leader. Jeff Bezos landed on the
same number (two-pizza team). Fred Brooks got there in 1975 through software
engineering — adding people to a project made it slower, not faster. Three
disciplines (evolutionary psychology, military preparedness, software engineering)
all converged on the same answer: the human brain sustains deep coordination with
about five people.

## What AI Actually Changed

AI didn't rewire our brains — it changed the consequences of getting team size
wrong.

Before AI, a five-person team produced X output. Adding a sixth person gave more
capacity with diminishing returns because coordination overhead grew faster than
output. Toby Lutke of Shopify calls this a 10x loss of productivity with each
addition beyond five.

After AI, the same five-person team produces 5–10x more. Evidence: revenue per
employee at AI-native companies. Lovable hits hundreds of millions of ARR with
tiny headcount. Midjourney, ElevenLabs, Anthropic, OpenAI — same story. The SaaS
benchmark was historically below $500K per employee. AI-native companies run
5–10x that.

If each person on a five-person team generates $2–3M per year in value, the
coordination cost of a sixth person is no longer a minor tax — it's a
catastrophe. That sixth person must justify their coordination cost against a
baseline where every existing member generates output that previously required an
entire department. When each person produced $250K/year, the coordination cost of
person six was manageable. At $2M per person, it's measured in millions of lost
productivity.

## Volume vs Correctness

Every conversation about AI and teams obsesses over volume — more code, more
content, faster. This leads to disastrously incorrect organizational decisions.
Volume is no longer a scarce resource. AI made volume cheap. What's scarce is
correctness: whether the thing shipped is architecturally sound, strategically
coherent, right for the customer, free of subtle errors that look fine in a demo
and compound into real failures in production.

A Harvard Business School field experiment (published 2025) studied 776
professionals at Procter & Gamble on real innovation challenges. Teams using AI
were 3x more likely to produce ideas in the top 10% of quality — not 3x more
output, 3x more likely to be right at the highest level. AI also broke functional
silos: both R&D and marketing produced more balanced, integrated ideas, extending
each person's competence into adjacent domains.

This is the mechanism: five excellent people using AI can each operate across a
broader domain. They don't need 10 specialists in 10 narrow lanes. They need five
generalist architects who use AI to extend their reach and each other as
verification against AI's errors.

## Two Archetypes: Scouts and Strike Teams

### Scouts

Scouts operate alone — one person, full AI toolkit, defined mission. The work is
exploration: is this technology viable, is this market real, can we build a
prototype? Zero coordination overhead. Constraint: one person's judgment with no
peer review or error correction.

Peter Steinberger demonstrated the Scout model at its extreme: in ~60 days running
4–10 coding agents simultaneously, he built OpenClaw in a language he'd never
used. One person, 20 years of judgment, a swarm of agents — output that the
world's most valuable companies were desperate to acquire. But OpenClaw shipped
with lots of holes. The solo model doesn't work when correctness requires multiple
perspectives, when the cost of being subtly wrong is high, or when sustained
production is the goal.

### Strike Teams

Five people with AI, executing where correctness matters. Every person's AI output
passes through at least one other brain sharing enough context to catch meaningful
errors. A team of five covers product, engineering, design, data, and domain
expertise — the real minimum surface area for a complete decision. Below five:
blind spots. Above five: silos. In a team of five, there is nowhere to hide.

Scouts explore, strike teams execute. Scouts map territory, strike teams build the
road. Most organizations currently have neither — they have oversized teams too
slow for exploration and too diluted for precision execution.

## The Ambition Expansion

Everyone frames AI and team size as a cost story: same work, fewer people. This is
a staggering failure of imagination for companies with strong talent.

If you have 500 people and each just got 5–10x more capable, the correct response
is not "I can run my company with 50 people." It's "I have the capacity of
2,500–5,000 people. What was I previously unable to do?"

Lovable didn't start with 45 people and build a tiny product — they built a global
platform serving millions of users touching every industry, becoming a unicorn.
Midjourney didn't focus on a niche with 100 employees — they went after the
entirety of visual creation. These companies didn't use AI to shrink. They used
small teams to think really big.

A SaaS company with 400 engineers maintaining one product restructured into 80
strike teams — can they build a platform with 10 products? A regional insurer with
200 people serving 3 states reorganized into 40 strike teams — could they serve 30
states? Both plausible.

The question: given that every five-person team now has the capacity of a 50-person
department, how many teams do we need to pursue the mission we actually want — not
the mission we settled for when headcount was expensive?

## Composing Strike Teams at Scale

The layers scale in Dunbar ratios. Five people make a strike team. 3–4 strike
teams share a domain, coordinated by a single person focused on inter-team
coherence. 3–4 domains share a strategic objective. At each level, the leader
maintains the quality of relationship required for the coordination being asked.

Management layer: dramatically thinned. No project managers when AI tracks
projects. No coordination roles when there are fewer humans to coordinate. But the
taste layer gets more significant — people obsessed with maintaining correctness
standards. What Toby Lutke at Shopify calls "the constitution": the specific
principles where a reasonable competitor would choose the opposite. In an
organization of federated strike teams producing at 10x, the people who define
and enforce taste are the most important people in the building.

## The Talent Bar

In a five-person team, every person occupies one of your 10 communication
pathways. Every person's judgment gets multiplied by AI. A mediocre contributor
doesn't just underperform — they consume a coordination slot without providing
judgment that justifies their cost. When mediocre judgment gets amplified by AI,
it generates verification burdens on everybody else. Nate calls this "the AI slop
tax" — making the team actively worse by consuming the team's most precious
resource: shared attention required to maintain correctness.

This is why Lovable hires former founders. Not because they can prompt AI, but
because they can architect systems at a level where agents can execute without
them reading every line.

## How to Test Who's Ready

Give someone a real scout mission — a problem your company has been ignoring. Full
AI tooling, one week, clear objective, no committee, zero check-ins. Test for:

- Can they define the problem without being handed a spec?
- Do they know what right looks like at the architectural level, not syntax level?
- Can they hold the whole system in their head?
- Do they default to action or to getting permission?

Results probably won't match current performance reviews. Highest-rated people
(great at navigating large org structures, running good meetings, writing clear
status updates) may struggle — those are coordination skills, overhead in a strike
team. Most frustrating people (ignore meetings, build without asking, occasionally
ship something brilliant nobody requested) may be exactly who you need.

## The Shopify Mandate Model

Toby Lutke required every Shopify team to prototype with AI before beginning a
real build. AI fluency became part of performance reviews. Teams must demonstrate
why AI could not do a task before requesting headcount.

Deeper reading: Toby built a systematic evaluation pipeline. Every AI prototype
generates a data point on what AI can and can't do in that domain. When the next
model drops, they have a pre-built test harness revealing what's possible. Every
forced AI prototype is a training route for strike team skills. The person who
prototypes 10 times and fails 7 has built more specification skill than the person
who attended 10 meetings on AI strategy. Executive mandate removes the permission
barrier — everybody gets the reps.

## Key Takeaway

AI raised output per person by an order of magnitude, which raised the
coordination cost of each additional person by the same order of magnitude. Five
optimizes for correctness. AI made volume free — correctness is the scarce
resource. The right response is not to shrink to five but to restructure into
teams of five that radically expand ambition. You didn't get a cost reduction —
you got a force multiplier. The companies defining the next decade are keeping
their people, reorganizing into strike teams, and going after missions that were
impossible when each person produced $250K/year. The leaders who ask "what becomes
possible?" will build the defining companies of this era.
