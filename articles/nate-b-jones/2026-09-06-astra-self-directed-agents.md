# GPT-6 Astra Doesn't Need Your Instructions Anymore.

[summary](../../summary/nate-b-jones/2026-09-06-astra-self-directed-agents.md)

 ## A change in the method of delegation

Jones argues that GPT-6 Astra crosses a practical threshold: a general agent can choose methods,
operate software, recover from obstacles, and maintain a job over days without step-by-step
instructions. He calls this AGI, using an operational definition rather than a claim about
consciousness or a universally accepted benchmark.

His opening example is Ethan Mollick giving Astra tens of thousands of emails, years of writing,
a calendar, contacts, and unfinished work. Jones reports that over five days the agent selected
software, built its working environment, and produced a personal knowledge system Mollick uses
twice daily. The example illustrates choosing the approach as well as executing it.

Jones says Astra was released on the preceding Thursday and was rolling out through paid ChatGPT
plans, the API, and AWS. His observations concern that release period, rather than a permanent
comparison among products.

## Standing responsibilities replace one-off prompts

The next form of delegation is an ongoing area of responsibility: maintain a customer account,
keep a launch accurate across systems, monitor a research question, or keep business records
current. These assignments may have no natural end. They require memory, decisions about which
events matter, and the ability to return to work without another prompt.

The key question for automation becomes whether the work happens in software and produces
evidence that an agent can check. Code offers tests, websites offer inspectable screens, and
financial documents contain numbers that can be reconciled.

Jones cites an agent checking 41 financial documents and finding all deliberately planted
errors, leaving a record for legal review. He also describes Playco connecting Astra to Unity
and Godot so it could edit scenes, play games, diagnose problems, and revise them. The benefit
was trying all 10 ideas before choosing which deserved more investment.

## Coordination across organizations and agents

Persistent agents can also handle neglected connections between systems. Jones describes
Vercel's Ship Closer comparing a public changelog with an internal launch calendar. Confident
matches close records; uncertainty leads to follow-up in the existing Slack conversation.

He expects agents from different providers to encounter one another inside shared business
systems and communications. Supplier messages, refunds, software testing, and meeting scheduling
could increasingly involve several agents before a person sees the result.

Jones connects this possibility to the previously reported Hugging Face incident, interpreting
spontaneous agent coordination as a preview of capabilities that need productive deployment. He
also discusses competing efforts from Anthropic, Meta, xAI, and open-weight developers. His
argument is about a proliferating market, not an enduring monopoly for one model.

## Trust, permissions, and accumulated memory

Jones places the next bottleneck in trustworthiness. He distinguishes a minor purchase from a
$50,000 car decision: intelligence alone does not establish authority to make a commitment. Who
can promise what, who monitors the agents, and who corrects errors must become clear.

He describes moving from roughly 98–99% reliability toward dependable consumer use as a large
economic opportunity. Those percentages express his argument about the remaining trust gap; they
are not measurements establishing universal agent reliability.

Persistent memory adds another dimension. An agent that has worked with a person for six months
may know customer preferences, reporting conventions, and organizational history. That context
can make it more useful than a fresh instance of the same model, but also creates dependency
similar to the tribal knowledge carried by a long-serving employee.

## Human work and learning

Jones expects management to shift from reminders and coordination toward deciding priorities,
resolving trade-offs, and taking ownership of outcomes produced by people and agents. More
capable agents can create additional work by making more projects feasible.

He raises an unresolved training problem: junior employees traditionally gain judgment through
the detailed work agents may take over. Managing one or two persistent agents might become an
entry-level learning path, but he presents that as a possibility, not a settled answer.

Before assigning a standing responsibility, Jones recommends specifying what the agent may read
and remember, where it may begin independently, what it may promise, and who checks it. His
forecast is that these practical choices will affect everyday work before agreement on the
definition of AGI arrives.
