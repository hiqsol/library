# 48 Days. That's How Long Before the Helium Runs Out for AI Chips

Hyperscalers plan to spend over a trillion dollars on AI in the next
24 months. Google co-founder Sergey Brin said on record he would
rather go bankrupt than lose the AI race. Companies are issuing bonds
and going negative on free cash flow to build data centers. But data
centers depend on chips, and chips depend on helium from a plant in
Qatar that has been hit by missiles.

## The Ras Laffan Crisis

Qatar's Ras Laffan complex produces roughly 33% of the world's
helium — about 2.4 billion standard cubic feet per year out of 7
billion globally. The facility has been damaged by missile strikes
and is offline. Qatar Energy has admitted that 14% of its helium
capacity is permanently damaged, with reconstruction timelines of up
to five years.

The planned Helium-4 plant — which would have been the largest ever
built at 1.5 billion standard cubic feet per year — has unknown
status. No one is shipping in or out of the area. Its 2027 target is
now delayed.

## Why Helium Is Irreplaceable

Helium is required at every critical step of advanced semiconductor
manufacturing. During plasma etching, fabs blow helium over the back
of the wafer to pull heat out and maintain temperature uniformity.
Georgetown researcher Jacob Fieldboy explains: "You have to maintain
a constant temperature over the wafer to maintain uniformity. Helium
is the only thermal conductor that makes it possible at that scale."

Helium is also critical for leak-testing vacuum chambers. Because
helium is the smallest element, leaks are detected sooner than with
any other gas. There is no substitute.

Per-chip helium consumption increases with each generation. A 300mm
EUV fab consumes 5,000–20,000 cubic meters per month, placing helium
among the largest specialty gas line items in the fab budget. Purity
requirements are extreme: 6N (99.9999%) pure. Only a tiny number of
sites can produce it.

## The 48-Day Clock

Specialized ISO containers hold liquid helium for 35 to 48 days
before it starts to vaporize. Once it evaporates, it cannot be
recovered. Containers currently stranded and bound for South Korea
and Taiwan are on this clock.

## Three Channels of Impact

**1. Helium supply to chips.** SK Hynix imported two-thirds of its
helium from Qatar. SK Hynix and Samsung — the world's two largest
memory chip manufacturers — now face helium rationing. They produce
the HBM that goes into every Nvidia GPU, AMD AI accelerator, and
Google TPU.

**2. LNG prices and energy costs.** Helium is a byproduct of LNG
cryogenic distillation — the two cannot be separated. The LNG
disruption raises energy costs for East Asian fabs. TSMC holds only
11 days of LNG reserves and imports 97% of Taiwan's energy. European
data center costs are also vulnerable over a multi-year horizon.

**3. Geopolitical restructuring.** The crisis favors Chinese compute
economics. China is developing domestic helium production — a
Guangdong plant has achieved 6N certification and is an ASML-approved
supplier. The Power of Siberia 2 pipeline would deliver cheap Russian
LNG to China. If China controls its energy and helium supply, it can
drive down chip fab costs and deploy cheap inference chips in the
late 2020s.

## Market Pricing

Helium spot prices have doubled. Contract surcharges are up 30%. The
US Federal Helium Reserve ended sales in 2023, removing a global
buffer. Russia cannot get its helium supply online due to the Ukraine
war. Qualification of new suppliers at 6N purity takes months to
years, not days.

## Memory Cost Outlook

HBM was already sold out before the crisis. DRAM prices were already
up 70%. Memory costs are expected to stay elevated through at least
mid-2027. The fabs producing HBM are the fabs most exposed to helium
rationing. Capacity relief that was planned has been cut off.

## What This Means

The fabs will not shut down completely — inventory managers will find
ways to keep operating. The question is whether they operate at lower
capacity, run slower, ship chips late, and lose the ability to scale
production against exploding AI inference demand. Only about 0.1% of
the world are heavy AI users running agentic workflows, and available
capacity is already maxed out.

US data centers are insulated from a physical plant perspective but
extremely exposed on chips — they get their chips from Taiwan and
South Korea. The Arizona fab is a tiny fraction of total need and
does not cover high-value inference chips.

The advice: buy compute now. IT procurement should move immediately.
Memory, laptops, phones — prices are going up and timelines are
stretching. The physical substrate of AI is far more fragile than
the trillion-dollar spending plans assumed.
