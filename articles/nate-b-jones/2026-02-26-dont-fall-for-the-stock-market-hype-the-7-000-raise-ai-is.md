# Don't Fall for the Stock Market Hype: The $7,000 Raise AI Is Giving You

[summary](../../summary/nate-b-jones/2026-02-26-dont-fall-for-the-stock-market-hype-the-7-000-raise-ai-is.md)

## A Fictional Recession Crashed the Stock Market

A Substack post written as speculative fiction from 2028 wiped out over $100
billion in market cap. IBM cratered 13% — its worst day in 25 years — because
Anthropic published a blog post about COBOL. But the Substack post was from
investment research firm Catrini, which wrote a highly regarded piece about
how bad things could get if AI-driven labor replacement takes hold.

The doom narrative driving seven major sell-offs has not been about the
technology — it has been about the economics. Both the doomer case and the
boomer case deserve honest examination, along with the thing neither side
is talking about.

## The Doom Case (Steel-Manned)

Catrini Research wrote "The 2028 Global Intelligence Crisis," a fictional
macro memo from 2028. The scenario: AI capabilities compound, companies
rationally cut white-collar headcount, displaced workers spend less,
consumption crashes cascade through mortgages and credit, the S&P drops
38%, unemployment hits 10.2%.

The mechanism is consistent and well-constructed:

- White-collar workers make up ~50% of US employment and drive 75% of
  discretionary consumer spending
- The top 20% of earners account for ~65% of consumer spending
- A 2% decline in white-collar employment could translate into a 4% hit
  on discretionary spending
- Catrini describes an "intelligence displacement spiral": AI gets better,
  companies cut payroll, savings fund more AI, AI gets better

The haunting line: "In 2008, the loans were bad on day one. In 2028, the
loans were good on day one. The world just changed after the loans were
written."

Private credit grew from $1 trillion (2015) to over $2.5 trillion (2026),
rolling up SaaS companies at valuations assuming perpetual revenue growth.
Those assumptions are dying in real time.

## The Bull Case

### Economic Modeling (Alex Emis, University of Chicago Booth)

Alex Emis modeled the actual conditions Catrini describes. When you stack
every assumption — no consumption rebound, no policy response, wealthy
capital owners don't spend more, interest rates at the floor — you get
Catrini's result. But the idea of no policy response is untenable. When
things get bad enough, governments respond — for entirely selfish electoral
reasons. This happened even in 2008 with a divided government.

### The Services Deflation Argument (Michael Bloke)

Most consumer spending is in services: mortgage processing, tax preparation,
insurance brokerage, travel booking. These are fundamentally complexity
tasks that AI agents plausibly make 40-70% cheaper. Michael Bloke's
analysis suggests $4,000-$7,000 in annual gain per median US household —
tax-free, no legislation required.

That money does not evaporate. If you pay less in mortgage commission,
you spend on furniture, renovations, moving costs. It goes back into the
economy. This is Jevons' paradox applied to services.

### Business Formation

The Census Bureau reported 532,000 new business applications in January
2026 alone, up 7% from December, continuing an accelerating trend since
2021. One-person businesses have more leverage than ever: lower overhead,
more reach, more tools, all thanks to AI.

## The Thing Nobody Is Talking About: The Capability-Dissipation Gap

Both doomer and boomer narratives assume AI capabilities translate incredibly
rapidly into economic impact. They don't.

Capabilities are not deployment. Deployment is not adoption. Adoption is not
deep integration. Deep integration is not economic impact.

### Four Forces of Social Inertia

**Regulatory inertia.** Financial services firms need approval from
regulators who haven't finished writing the rules. Healthcare navigates
HIPAA, FDA, IRBs. Government procurement cycles run in years. The COBOL
systems Anthropic discussed run 95% of US ATM transactions — hundreds of
billions of lines in production. Nobody is migrating them because of a
blog post. IBM's CEO said their mainframe AI assistant understands existing
COBOL, it doesn't replace it.

**Organizational inertia.** Catrini assumes companies cut headcount
rationally and rapidly. Companies are not rational actors. Headcount
decisions filter through HR policies, employment law, unions, severance,
institutional knowledge preservation, management politics, and the fact
that most executives have never managed an AI transition. The gap between
"Claude can technically do parts of this job" and "we've reorganized
workflows, retrained staff, built QA processes, and confidently reduced
headcount" is enormous. Multiple pilot programs were abandoned because AI
moved past them — RAG is a prime example.

**Cultural inertia.** Most people still don't use AI daily. Shopify CEO
Toby Lutke — one of the most technically fluent CEOs, running a tech
company — had to issue a company-wide mandate making AI usage a performance
review criterion. He maintains a personal folder of prompts he runs against
every model release. If that level of personal investment is required at
Shopify, consider what it takes at a mid-market manufacturing firm.
Multiply by a million.

**Trust inertia.** Enterprises do not and should not trust AI output by
default. Building formal verification systems requires capital most
organizations lack and cultural transformation most can't stomach.

## Two Curves on the Same Chart

**Curve 1: AI Capability.** Goes up very fast. Gemini doubled its reasoning
in three months.

**Curve 2: Societal Dissipation.** The rate at which capabilities actually
permeate the economy. Way flatter. Governed by inertia forces. Does compound
over time, but starts from a low base.

The gap between these curves is where we all live today. It explains:

- Why AI capabilities are stunning and economic disruption is still modest
- Why the stock market cannot make up its mind
- Why both doom and boom narratives sound compelling
- Why a blog post can crash a stock

More importantly, the gap reveals a specific, large economic opportunity.
The tools are powerful but unevenly distributed, understood by very few,
integrated by even fewer. That creates asymmetric returns for anyone who
invests seriously in AI capability.

## Who Benefits from the Gap

**Large firms** have capital, data, distribution, and compliance budgets —
but carry the full weight of organizational inertia. Every new AI workflow
must survive procurement, legal, security. It can take 18 months from "this
will save $10 million" to actually saving it. Exception: highly involved
founders like Lutke.

**Small firms and individuals** lack capital, data, and distribution but
have speed. The gap creates an asymmetric advantage for anyone who can
collapse the integration timeline. A solo consultant integrating AI today
operates at the capability frontier while competitors hold quarterly
meetings.

The practical heuristic: AI-native people think in hours or "by end of day,"
not weeks or quarters.

## Toby Lutke's Playbook

- Mandate: demonstrate why AI can't do this before asking a human to
- Personal discipline: structured evals on every model release
- Require AI exploration in prototype phase of every project — not because
  AI will succeed, but because even failure creates an eval for the next
  model
- Building organizational muscle memory so each model release immediately
  reveals what's newly possible
- Insight from the Acquired podcast: the best chess game every year is
  machine vs. machine and nobody watches. We care about humans playing
  chess. Tools are instruments to be played, not replacements for the player

## Three Takeaways

1. **Recontextualize stock market activity.** The AI scare trade creates
   mispriced assets and organizational chaos. Companies getting hammered
   won't face disruption for years, not the weeks the market prices. The
   buy side (what companies do with 40% software cost savings, $42B
   redirected from real estate commissions) isn't being priced at all.

2. **Recontextualize doom narratives.** The bear case is built on real
   economic forces but requires extreme conditions. It's useful as a policy
   warning, not as a career planning framework. It is 10-50x more viral
   than counter-evidence. Calibrate accordingly.

3. **Map the capability-dissipation gap for yourself.** Are you at the
   capability frontier — testing models, integrating into real workflows,
   building evaluation frameworks? Or at the dissipation rate — aware AI
   exists, maybe using it occasionally, fundamentally working the same way
   as 2 years ago? The gap is where economic value concentrates. Because
   social inertia is strong, the advantage persists and compounds far longer
   than most models predict.

The career move: become the person who walks into a room of panicking
executives and says with genuine authority: "I've tested this. Here's what
AI can actually do in our workflow. Here's what it cannot. Here's the
implementation plan, the budget, and the timeline." That person does not
exist in most organizations.
