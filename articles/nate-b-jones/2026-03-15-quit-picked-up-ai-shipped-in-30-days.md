## Introduction

Your most extraordinary people are operating at 25% of their actual capacity.
The biggest barrier to AI productivity is not tooling — it is the human
coordination overhead that consumes our days: syncs, scheduling, meetings,
emails. Solo founders offer lessons that apply far beyond startups. Their
success reveals soft skills — unnamed until now — that matter more than any
tool skill in the AI era.

## Solo Founders: The Numbers

Several solo founders demonstrate what becomes possible when overhead
disappears:

- **Ben Sira** — zero employees, $2.5M ARR with Pulsia (an AI company
  builder). Went from $1M to $2.5M in four days due to surging demand for
  the solo-founding model.
- **Mauromo** — bootstrapped Base44 to 300K users and ~$3.5M ARR with no
  VC funding.
- **Peter Levels** — got his flight simulator app to $1M. Later learned
  TypeScript via AI, treating his PHP expertise as a tool rather than an
  identity.
- **Sarah Gilliam** — profiled by The Economist as a potential one-person
  unicorn. She had years of domain expertise working with bereaved people
  but was blocked by a technical barrier. AI removed that single blocker.

The objection that solo founders operate in greenfield conditions — free
from enterprise complexity, integrations, and legacy systems — is a fair
instinct but the wrong conclusion.

## The Enterprise Evidence

A **Harvard Business School field experiment** studied 776 professionals at
Procter & Gamble working on real product innovation challenges. Key
findings:

- Individuals with AI were **3x more likely** to produce ideas rated in
  the top 10% of quality by independent experts.
- AI broke down functional silos: R&D people produced more commercially
  viable ideas; marketing people produced more technically grounded ones.
- A single person with AI matched the performance of a two-person team
  without it.

The coordination cost of combining multiple perspectives was absorbed by
the tool instead of the organization. AI acts as a coordination value-add,
proxying in expertise from adjacent functions.

**Shopify** provides a corporate example: Toby Lutke made prototyping a
requirement. Beyond enforcing AI fluency, this strips out coordination
overhead. Instead of consulting six teams, you build the prototype. AI
proxies in marketing, product, and engineering perspectives so you can
assess value rapidly.

## Taste vs. Conviction: The Missing Feedback Loop

The popular formula — "80% AI, 20% taste" — does not tell the whole story.

**Taste** is the ability to evaluate whether something you are building is
good. **Conviction** is the willingness to act on that evaluation before
anyone else confirms you are right.

When Ben designed Pulsia's interface as a minimalist clicker game with an
auto-playing Daft Punk track, rejecting the dark-mode sci-fi gradient
every other AI product uses, no design committee approved it. He had
conviction.

Taste and conviction are not separate traits — they form a feedback loop:

1. Taste tells you what is good.
2. Conviction drives you to ship against that judgment.
3. Real-world feedback refines your taste.
4. Refined taste produces more reliable conviction.

This loop is learnable. People who develop it create a virtuous flywheel:
shipping fast, gathering feedback, sharpening judgment, shipping again.
Without conviction, even excellent taste remains theoretical. Without
taste, conviction ships wrong.

For leaders: if you are told to "develop taste" on your team and wonder
what that means practically, combine it with cultivating conviction — the
confidence to place bets and ship.

## Speed of Control > Span of Control

We talk about AI expanding our **span of control** — managing 5, 10, 15
agents. But the real differentiator among extraordinary AI talent is
**speed of control**: how fast you can make high-quality decisions.

Ben directs dozens of AI agents across multiple businesses. He does not
manage them like a VP manages a department through layers of context
setting and alignment documents. Each morning, his AI CEO agent emails a
compressed status update of what happened overnight across all work
streams. He reads it, makes a series of judgment calls — yes, no, ship
that, change direction — and the agents execute until the next check-in.

The constraint on his output is not how many things he can hold in his head
at once. It is how fast he can make high-quality decisions once given
information. This is an executive-level skill — triaging, allocating
attention disproportionately to trouble spots, like an editor who develops
a sense for where problems hide rather than reading every word with equal
intensity.

The "Attention Is All You Need" paper (2017) was written about transformer
architecture, but it applies to humans too. The dozen-agent workflow is the
output of an inner skill: sequencing attention toward what really matters
and making decisive calls.

## Overhead as the Talent Blocker

A core thesis emerges:

> Extraordinary people were never blocked by ability — they were blocked
> by overhead.

Consider a PM who has shipped dozens of features, whose instincts are
honed by a decade of customer contact. She is extraordinary but trapped by
coordination burdens, unable to ship more than one or two features at a
time. Lift the overhead and she builds at a completely different scale.

This is precisely why solo founders exist. When they develop taste,
conviction, and high-quality decision-making, the best place to apply
those skills is often their own business — because the overhead is gone.

**Carta data** confirms the trend quantitatively: solo-founded businesses
are rising to **one-third of all new US ventures**, up from one-quarter
previously. When AI removes the technical barrier that once required
co-founders, people take the biggest bet of their lifetimes.

For leaders, this is a retention problem. Removing overhead is now a
talent retention strategy. If your best people cannot ship boldly inside
your organization, they will leave to solo-found — and they will probably
be right to do so.

## AI Grows Talent, Not Just Unleashes It

AI does not only unblock people who are already extraordinary. It
accelerates skill development itself.

The taste-conviction feedback loop compresses learning cycles. People who
ship fast, update their understanding, and iterate are building pattern
recognition at unprecedented speed. The uncomfortable implication:

> Two years of AI-native building may produce more relevant pattern
> recognition than eight years of traditional execution.

Department heads and VPs report privately that a hungry team member with
18 months of AI-native experience is developing domain expertise faster
than veterans with a decade of traditional execution who are not engaging
with AI.

Peter Levels exemplifies this growth mindset. He did not protect his PHP
expertise as core identity. He used AI to learn TypeScript, keeping his
judgment and decision-making skills intact while adding new capabilities.

## The Averaging Cost

Separate from overhead, organizations suffer from **averaging cost**: the
more people involved in a decision, the more average the output becomes,
unless extraordinary measures ensure decisiveness and clarity of vision.

Ben explicitly rejected this dynamic. Solo founders often cite it as a
primary motivation. For organizations, the antidote is Amazon's
**"disagree and commit"** principle — empowering people to make decisive
choices with or without full agreement, as long as they deliver results.

If you want people to act internally with the same freedom they would have
as solo founders, you must genuinely allow them to not compromise on their
vision. Accountability remains, but autonomy must be real.

## Three Skills to Identify and Cultivate

To find and develop extraordinary AI talent, look for three specific
qualities — not years of experience, not vague "taste":

1. **Judgment density** — How much relevant pattern recognition does this
   person carry? Are they calibrated to current conditions? Can they
   reliably distinguish better from worse in changing circumstances? This
   is the foundation: good judgment is the precursor to good taste, which
   is the precursor to conviction.

2. **Conviction velocity** — How quickly can they move from recognizing a
   pattern to acting on it? Not reckless speed, but the instinct to act
   because they believe they are right. This is the raw material that
   feeds into the taste-conviction feedback loop.

3. **Execution bandwidth** — How much can they manage and ship? This is
   cultivable. It underpins the higher-order skill of allocating attention
   intentionally across multiple AI agents and work streams.

A critical note on generative AI: much of what makes extraordinary
products extraordinary is what the builder said **no** to. Ben rejected
many options before choosing his bold direction. The ability to say no —
to meetings, to good-but-misaligned ideas, to consensus-driven
compromise — is essential for unleashing AI talent.

## The Organizational Imperative

The solo-founding wave is partly a story of people fulfilling ambitions.
But it is also a symptom of organizational failure — environments that do
not coach, lead, or manage people in ways that let them be their best
selves at work.

What AI changes: if people cannot get that environment at work, they will
increasingly go get it for themselves. The data shows this is already
happening.

Leaders must foster environments where:

- Individual ambition is welcomed, supported, and given space to explore.
- People can say no — to meetings, to options that do not fit their
  vision — without career penalty.
- The taste-conviction feedback loop is actively cultivated, not
  accidentally suppressed.
- Overhead is aggressively eliminated as a talent retention strategy.
- Bold, autonomous execution is genuinely permitted, not just
  performatively encouraged.

Solo founding should be a free choice people make because they want the
adventure — not because they feel it is the only path to realizing their
AI-era potential. The lessons from solo founders belong inside
organizations too, and the companies that learn them will keep their most
extraordinary people.
