# Why $650 Billion in AI Spending Isn't Enough — The 4 Skills That Survive

## The Capex Shock

Google announced $175–185 billion in 2026 AI capital expenditure — roughly
double the $91 billion spent in 2025 (itself a 74% increase over 2024).
Analysts had expected ~$120 billion; Google blew past that by 50%. The
stock dropped 7% after hours. Not because the number was too high — because
Wall Street started realizing it might not be high enough.

Alphabet Q4 earnings context:

- Revenue exceeded $400 billion for the first time.
- Earnings per share beat expectations.
- Cloud revenue accelerated.
- CFO Anat Ashkenazi broke down allocation: ~60% servers, ~40% data
  centers and networking.
- Sundar Pichai described maintaining a "brutal pace" to compete on AI.

## From Bubble to Underbuilt — In Weeks

Six months prior, the consensus was that AI infrastructure spending had
decoupled from reality:

- Goldman Sachs published a widely cited note asking whether big tech was
  spending too much on AI with too little to show for it.
- Sequoia's David Cahn wrote his "$600 billion question" analysis — total
  AI company revenue could not justify the infrastructure being built.
- Jim Covello at Goldman called generative AI overhyped.

Then agents happened. Not the concept — the actual deployment into
production workflows, consuming massive inference tokens and delivering
undeniable value:

- **Anthropic's Claude Co-work:** plugins for contract triage, compliance
  review, audit summaries. A single legal plugin — 200 lines of structured
  markdown — wiped 16% off Thomson Reuters.
- **OpenAI's Frontier:** enterprise agent platform, launched with HP,
  Intuit, Oracle, State Farm, Uber as production customers.
- **Coding agents (Cursor, Codex, Claude Code):** crossed from
  autocomplete to autonomously generating thousands of production commits
  per year.

The SaaS Apocalypse — $285 billion erased from enterprise software market
cap — was proof of demand. Not projected, not theoretical — revealed
demand priced by the market in real time.

Derek Thompson: "The odds that AI is a bubble declined significantly and
the odds that we're quite underbuilt went up."

You cannot simultaneously believe AI agents are powerful enough to crash
enterprise software and that the infrastructure spending to support them
is excessive. Pick one.

## The Scale of the Bet

The five largest tech companies will spend ~$700 billion on AI
infrastructure in 2026. Goldman projects that rising to well over
$1 trillion between 2025 and 2027.

| Company   | 2026 CapEx (approx.)      | Notes                     |
|-----------|---------------------------|---------------------------|
| Google    | $175–185B                 | Double 2025's $91B        |
| Microsoft | ~$145B annualized         | Capital intensity at 45%  |
| Meta      | $115–135B                 | Supercomputer labs        |
| Amazon    | CapEx exceeds free cash   | Tapping debt markets      |
| Oracle    | Tens of billions          | Barely in cloud 2 yrs ago |

Google's single-year spend exceeds the entire GDP of Ukraine.
Microsoft's capital intensity — 45% of revenue — is historically
unthinkable for a software company.

OpenAI's ARR hit $20 billion in 2025 — impressive, but that represents
~3% of the infrastructure investment being made on its behalf. The math
doesn't close on revenue. It closes on what agents are about to consume.

## Why This Isn't a Dumb Pipe

Every previous infrastructure boom followed the pattern: massive
overbuilding → investor panic → discovery of what it's actually for.

- **Railroads (1865–1873):** mileage doubled in 8 years, 121 railroads
  went bankrupt, 18,000 businesses collapsed. Then Philip Armour invented
  refrigerated rail cars — fresh meat from Chicago to New York.
- **Fiber optics (1996–2001):** $500 billion in bonds, 90 million miles
  of cable, 95% went dark after the bust. Then YouTube launched on
  near-free bandwidth. Netflix pivoted to streaming.

But AI infrastructure has a structural difference: it is not a dumb pipe.

Railroads sold bandwidth. Fiber sold bandwidth. AI companies sell
intelligence. Every inference call is a purchase of cognitive capability.
The infrastructure and the intelligence are vertically integrated — model
makers capture value from the applications built on top, not just hosting
fees but an actual share of the cognitive work performed.

This doesn't guarantee any company wins, but the analogy to telecom
bankruptcies is misleading. The model makers are not laying dumb cable.

## Training vs. Inference: The Phase Shift

The first wave (2023 to mid-2025) was about training — massive GPU
clusters, bursty and front-loaded. This is what bears were analyzing.

The current wave is about inference — running trained models continuously
for millions of users and millions of agents, 24/7. Inference is cheaper
per unit but never stops.

Agents change the inference math:

- A human using ChatGPT generates a modest inference workload.
- An agent generates ~1,000x a human workload — reviewing contracts,
  writing code, running continuously.
- Multiply 1,000x by every workflow the SaaS Apocalypse showed is about
  to be automated: contract review, financial auditing, data analysis,
  CRM management, customer service.

Google's 60/40 split (servers vs. data centers) signals they understand
this. They are building inference capacity for a world where AI agents
are the primary consumers of compute.

Fidji Simo, OpenAI's CEO of Applications: "We spent months integrating
and we didn't even get what we wanted." Enterprise AI integration is
harder than expected — not because models aren't good enough, but because
the plumbing (connectors, security layers) isn't mature. Demand is
outrunning infrastructure.

## The Infrastructure Window

Every infrastructure inversion has a 3–7 year window. Companies that
build during the window become platforms; companies that wait become
tenants.

- Amazon built AWS between 2003 and 2006 — dominant cloud platform before
  most enterprises knew they needed one. Those who waited paid Amazon's
  margins for 20 years.

The current cycle is compressed to ~18 months because demand signals
arrive fast — agents develop that fast. Google's $185 billion makes
sense when you understand the compression. They'd rather be wrong and
have spent too much than be right and have spent too little.

The companies that look like they're burning cash in 2026 will look like
they were laying foundations when viewed from 2028.

## Code as the Breakthrough Domain

Code proved to be the breakthrough application for agents because output
is immediately and objectively verifiable — it works or it doesn't. That
tight feedback loop is what agents excel at.

Today coding agents work in bursts — an hour here, a few hours there.
But context windows are expanding, working memory is multiplying:

- Opus 4.6 delivered 5x working memory versus 4.5 in a couple of months.
- If that pace holds (zero evidence of deceleration), by year-end agents
  can sustain months of continuous work.
- An agent coding autonomously for a month consumes inference compute at
  volumes no analyst model has accounted for.

Domains where output quality can be systematically evaluated are next:
legal analysis, financial auditing, medical diagnostics, engineering
design. The infrastructure that looks like overbuild today will look
undersized in a year or two.

## The Four Skills That Survive

The pattern is fractal — it plays out at company scale and at individual
career scale. What human skills survive when agents can code for months,
review contracts, and generate production-quality work at machine speed?

### 1. Taste

The ability to look at agent output and know — not by checklist but by
hard-won instinct — whether it's right, whether it solves the real
problem or a poorly framed one. Agents generate enormous volumes of
competent output. They cannot reliably tell the difference between
competent and extraordinary, or between technically correct and
strategically right. Taste becomes a filter. People who have refined
their judgment through years of doing the work become exponentially
more valuable when generating options costs zero.

### 2. Exquisite Domain Judgment

Not general intelligence — agents will have that in abundance. The
specific, contextual, hard-to-articulate understanding of how a
particular domain actually works:

- The lawyer who knows which clauses matter in negotiation, not just
  which clauses must exist.
- The engineer who knows which architectural decisions create pain in
  18 months.
- The executive who knows which market signals are noise and which
  are structural.

This knowledge is accumulated over years and encoded in intuition that
agents approximate but cannot yet replicate — it depends on experience
not in the training set.

### 3. Phenomenal Ramp

The ability to learn fast when everything is evolving fast. Not "I took
a course on AI" — using the tools daily, mental model updating weekly,
comfortable operating at the frontier even when the frontier moved since
last Tuesday.

Opus 4.6 arrives, Codex follows 20 minutes later, something else drops
next week. The ability to absorb change at speed is a meta-skill that
makes all other skills usable. The humans who can keep up with AI have
an edge that compounds.

### 4. Relentless Honesty About Where Value Is Moving

The hardest one. Looking at your own work and asking which parts are
genuinely valuable and which an agent could handle better, cheaper,
faster. Most people don't want to do this inventory — it can mean
admitting that skills you spent years building are depreciating fast.

But those who do the inventory can reallocate their time toward what
still matters before the market forces them to.

## Conclusion

If you're waiting for AI to settle down before investing serious time
in skills — don't. You're making the same bet as companies that waited
for cloud to prove itself in 2008. Stability is not coming. The pace is
accelerating.

The gap between "I use AI tools" and "I have rebuilt how I work around
what AI makes possible" is the individual version of the gap between
"we added AI features" and "we built our architecture to be agent-first."
The first feels productive. The second changes outcomes.

Google's $185 billion is not reckless. It's probably not enough. The
market will look back on 2026 the way we look back at early AWS data
centers, the first transcontinental railroad, the fiber optic cables
lying dark under the Atlantic. The foundation of everything that comes
next is being built this year — the year agents proved they were real.
