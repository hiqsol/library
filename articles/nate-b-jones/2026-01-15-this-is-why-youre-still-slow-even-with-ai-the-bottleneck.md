## The Bottleneck Has Moved

Two scenes from the last month:

**Scene 1:** Anthropic shipped Co-work — a full product feature built in
10 days with four people, written entirely in Claude Code. They ship
60–100 releases daily.

**Scene 2:** At most companies, a leader is asking for a 30-day
implementation roadmap with phases, milestones, resource allocation, and
a plan to protect capacity.

Execution capacity is not scarce anymore. But every organization and
all of our individual work habits are built around the implicit answer
to one question: **What is expensive here?** For most of our careers,
the answer was execution — scarce hours from scarce people with scarce
skills.

We evolved elaborate rituals to protect that capacity: planning
processes, approval gates, specs, PRDs, meetings to align before anyone
built. When the meeting takes less time than building, hold the meeting.
When gathering requirements costs less than writing code, gather first.
When rework is expensive, plan meticulously.

**AI has inverted the entire cost ratio.** Cursor went from $1M to $500M
in annual revenue faster than any SaaS company in history. Coinbase
engineers report single people refactoring or building new codebases in
days. A PRD can now take longer than the prototype.

## Where the Bottleneck Went

Manufacturing principle: when you eliminate a bottleneck, it does not
disappear — it moves downstream. The bottleneck has shifted to:

### Clarity

Do you actually know what is worth building? You can now build faster
than you can think. PRDs were always a substitute for clarity — a hedge
against expensive rework. Now writing a PRD can cost more than shipping
the whole thing.

### Ambition

Are you swinging hard enough? When you get 50 swings a year instead of
3–4, the risk is timidity. The danger is not building the wrong thing —
it is not building enough things toward a larger transformative vision.

### Distribution

When everybody can build, product is not the moat. Getting it into
people's hands is. Cognition (makers of Devon) partnered with Infosys
and deployed across their 300,000+ person team and global client base
rather than building their own distribution. Infosys has decades of
enterprise relationships.

### Relationships

You cannot vibe-code a relationship. This is fractal — true for
individuals and for companies. When capabilities compound quickly and
platforms keep shifting, relationships are the durable advantage.

## Eight Habits to Break

### 1. The Permission Loop

Old logic: doing is expensive, so check before you do. New reality:
asking takes longer than doing. The email thread to get approval can
take more time than building the prototype. The Slack conversation to
confirm direction takes longer than trying both directions.

Organizations need people who default to doing, building the rough
version, showing it, and asking forgiveness when needed.

### 2. Polish as Procrastination

Old logic: you get one shot, make it count. New reality: people spend
80% of their time on the last 20% of quality when the marginal value
of that polish is dropping. A directionally correct, ambitious idea is
enough to get going.

The rough version that exists beats the polished version that does not.
Notebook LM is a good example: they shipped, saw reaction, and have
been polishing the UI ever since.

### 3. Meetings as Default

Old logic: get alignment before action. New reality: an hour of six
people's time is 6 hours of work — often enough to just build the
thing. Meetings about what to build often do not even resolve what to
build. Replace the meeting with a product demo.

### 4. Structured Waiting

Old logic: wait for feedback, respect the process. New reality: most of
what you are waiting for does not need to be waited for. Waiting an hour
in the 2010s was waiting an hour. Waiting an hour now is waiting a
prototype. If blocked on a decision, make a provisional decision, let
people know, and keep moving.

### 5. Planning Before Doing

Old logic: measure twice, cut once. New reality: the plan almost always
does not survive contact with reality. Try cutting planning by 90%.
Replace it with learning through prototyping. If you have not built
something in the last couple of weeks, you are probably overplanning.

### 6. The Deck Instead of the Demo

Old logic: build consensus with a presentation. New reality: build a
working prototype and show that instead. All of the rituals we prepared
as a hedge against execution are in question.

### 7. Consensus Before Action

Old logic: get everybody aligned. New reality: the cost of consensus
has 10x-ed or 100x-ed. Consensus often was not real anyway — people
agreed in meetings then undermined decisions later.

Let results create alignment. "I tried X and here's what happened" is
much more persuasive than "let's agree to try X."

### 8. Hoarding Until Ready

Old logic: don't show half-finished work. New reality: you are getting
feedback late after investing in a direction that might be wrong.
Finding out you are wrong a week from now is better than a month from
now.

This requires some ego death. But half-finished work with real thought
behind it is different from AI slop with no thought.

## The Through Line

All eight habits are risk-management rituals that made sense when doing
was expensive. Those unit economics have flipped. The risk is no longer
wasting execution time — it is wasting time on anything that is not
building.

**The chaos you feel is not random. It is the gap between where the
bottleneck has moved and the habits you still have.** When you close
that gap — aligning work habits to how AI changes scarcity — the chaos
starts making sense.

The truly precious resources are clarity, ambition, distribution, and
relationships. Those are the bottlenecks growing more scarce as
AI-driven execution becomes pervasive.
