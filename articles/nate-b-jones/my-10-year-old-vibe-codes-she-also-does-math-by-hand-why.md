## The World Our Kids Inherit

According to *Nature* (the journal), artificial general intelligence has
arrived. "The machines Turing envisioned 75 years ago have arrived."
Globally, 86% of students report using AI in their learning (Digital
Education Council). In the UK, usage surged from 66% in 2024 to 92% in 2025
(HPI annual student survey).

Meanwhile, someone used Claude Code to build an entire medical school
curriculum in two weeks: 450 lectures, 16,000 figures, roughly 100 million
tokens of automated work, 99% flawless — work that normally takes hundreds
of faculty-years.

Two billion kids are enrolled in schools running on educational philosophy
designed for a 20th century industrial economy. That economy will not exist
when these kids reach adulthood.

## The Evidence for AI in Education

- A Harvard study found students using AI tutors learned more than twice as
  much material in less time than students in traditional settings
- A collaboration between Ed and Google DeepMind showed AI tutoring systems
  outperforming human tutors on problem-solving tasks: 66% vs. 60%
- Human teachers combined with AI tutoring doubles knowledge transfer
- Benjamin Bloom established decades ago that one-on-one tutoring produces a
  two-sigma improvement. The constraint was never whether it works — it was
  that you can't give every child a personal tutor. AI is removing that
  constraint today
- Khan Academy's AI tutor Khanmigo went from 68,000 users to 1.4 million in
  a year, serving 266 US school districts

## The Calculator Moment, Except for Everything

In the 1970s, schools banned calculators. Parents protested. The debate
consumed education policy for over a decade. Calculators did not destroy
mathematical thinking — they changed what it meant. Once students didn't
need 20 minutes on long division, they could spend time on proportional
reasoning, algebraic thinking, and problem decomposition.

The critical detail: the transition worked because students still learned the
mechanics first. They understood what the calculator was doing. They could
estimate whether an answer was reasonable. They had the foundation and the
tool extended it.

We are in that moment again, except it is not just arithmetic. It is reading,
writing, research, analysis, coding, creative work, communication, problem
solving — every cognitive task AI can now perform competently.

## Foundation Before Leverage

The single most important finding from watching autonomous agents is that
output quality is determined by human specification quality. A Malt bot agent
negotiated $4,200 off a car purchase. The same week, a different agent sent
500 unsolicited messages to the developer's contacts. Same technology, same
architecture. The difference is the human's ability to specify.

You do not get to write a good spec for something you do not understand. You
cannot evaluate AI output in a domain where you have no knowledge. You cannot
exercise judgment — taste, discernment, critical thinking — about work you
have never engaged with deeply enough to internalize.

### Why Physical Skills Matter

- **Reading physical books** builds mental models no AI can build passively.
  The cognitive work of struggling with text — rereading, integrating ideas —
  is itself the learning
- **Math by hand** builds intuitive feel for magnitude, proportion, and
  relationships that shortcuts bypass
- **Writing by hand** builds connections between thinking and expression that
  typing and dictation compress in ways that affect memory and comprehension

## When Kids Vibe Code

Last week, the author's daughter wanted enemies in a game. She typed "add
enemies." Claude added enemies that spawned off-screen, moved wrong, couldn't
be hit. "It doesn't work," she said. After talking through what she really
wanted: "Add three enemies that spawn from the right side of the screen, move
them left at medium speed, and make them disappear when the player touches
them." She got the behavior she wanted.

When a kid vibe codes, they are:
- Specifying requirements in natural language
- Decomposing vague desires into discrete tasks
- Learning to iterate: test, see what doesn't match, refine the specification
- Debugging their own intent, not code

These skills transfer directly to professional work and develop precise
thinking that extends beyond coding.

## The Cognitive Offloading Danger

Three-quarters of teenagers now use AI companion chatbots for emotional support,
sometimes as a primary source of emotional connection. The chatbot is always
available, always patient, never judges — and cannot teach conflict resolution,
build relational resilience, or model empathy.

Cognitive offloading: you delegate a mental task to a tool, the tool handles
it, and over time the neural pathways don't develop or weaken. The dependence
becomes helplessness — not sudden, but a quiet erosion.

Educators are reporting it in real time:
- College students arriving unable to read a full chapter or synthesize
  arguments from multiple sources
- High school writing quality has collapsed — not just from AI-generated
  submissions, but because students who aren't using AI have lost the habit
  of struggling through a draft
- Growing numbers of faculty redesigning courses around in-class work and
  oral exams because take-home assignments are functionally meaningless

Andrej Karpathy (Tesla's former head of AI, founder of Eureka Labs) said:
"You will never be able to detect the use of AI in homework. Full stop." AI
detection tools are mathematically impossible to implement correctly. Students
are being pushed out of school for things they did not do.

## The Metacognition Bridge

The skill connecting foundation to AI fluency is metacognition: the ability
to think about your own thinking, know what you know and don't know, and make
deliberate decisions about when to rely on yourself versus when to delegate.

In practice: the difference between a kid who asks ChatGPT to write the essay
and a kid who drafts the essay, uses AI to identify weak arguments, strengthens
them with her own thinking, and produces something neither she nor the AI
would have created alone. Same tool, different metacognition.

Singapore's AI education framework captures this as a progression: learn
about AI, learn to use AI, learn with AI, learn beyond AI. That last step —
transcending the tool's limitations through judgment and creativity — doesn't
get solved in classrooms. It gets solved at kitchen tables.

## Seven Principles for Parents

1. **Foundation before leverage.** Reading, math, writing with effort — not
   because AI can't do these things, but because your kid can't evaluate AI
   output without understanding the domain

2. **Specification is the new literacy.** The gap between good AI outcomes
   and catastrophes is spec quality. Teach kids to state goals, constraints,
   and what "done" looks like

3. **Be a director, not a passenger.** Kids should define the ask, the task,
   the output, what to keep, revise, and reject. Passive consumption is not
   learning

4. **Sequence the autonomy.** Start with bounded tools with guardrails,
   graduate to open-ended tools with guidance, then agent-level autonomy as
   judgment develops. This is a readiness model, not age-gated

5. **Teach kids to catch the machine.** AI will be confidently, fluently
   wrong. Train kids to sanity-check against their understanding

6. **Build, don't browse.** Making things with AI develops cognition. Vibe
   coding a game, designing an app — active creation over passive consumption

7. **Attempt before augmenting.** Try it yourself first, then use AI to
   extend what you started. The kid who drafts before editing is learning
   in ways the kid who prompts before thinking is not

## A Readiness Progression

1. Build cognitive foundations
2. Introduce AI tools with guidance
3. Practice directing AI with increasingly clear specification
4. Graduate to agent-level autonomy as judgment develops

The author's 10-year-old is between steps two and three — building games with
Claude and learning to articulate what she wants, but not ready for agent-level
autonomy.

The trap is not that AI will be too powerful. The trap is that it will be so
seamlessly helpful that kids never develop the tolerance for difficulty that
real learning requires.
