# OpenAI, NVIDIA And Anthropic Just Split. Here's How I'd Spend $20, $60 Or $200.

 ## Three overlapping strategies

Jones interprets recent chip, distribution, and infrastructure announcements as three loose
strategic camps. OpenAI wants more control over its full system, NVIDIA wants to supply all
sides, and Anthropic wants multiple sources of compute and distribution. These are his
analytical groupings, not announced alliances.

He connects OpenAI's custom inference chip to its dispute with Cursor and NVIDIA's response. The
strategic question for an individual is how to buy useful AI without letting one provider hold
the only copy of their working context.

## OpenAI's integration loop

Jones says OpenAI's chip reached tape-out in nine months. Selected AI-generated code ran 1.5–1.8
times faster than expert-written versions, and three additional model families were adapted to
the chip in two months. He emphasizes that these are selected results, not evidence of
superiority across every workload.

The automatic transcript varies between Habanero, Jalapeno, and other renderings of the chip's
name. The stable argument is about a custom inference platform rather than its transcription.

Inference hardware does not replace NVIDIA's role in training and general workloads. Jones cites
NVIDIA's statement that OpenAI has roughly 12 gigawatts of its systems planned or committed
through 2030.

OpenAI's proposed advantage is a feedback loop: applications reveal expensive recurring jobs,
model and serving teams change how they run, coding agents improve chip software, and those
results inform the next hardware design. Controlling enough repetitive inference can change unit
economics without owning every chip.

Jones reports that OpenAI will stop supplying future models to Cursor on November 12 following
SpaceX's acquisition, citing trust and contractual issues. He treats this as an example of how a
user's access can change when companies become rivals, rather than just a product dispute.

## NVIDIA and Anthropic

NVIDIA's position extends beyond an individual processor to training systems, networking,
software, cloud distribution, and the ability to accommodate unfamiliar workloads. A customer
can move some inference to custom silicon while increasing its total NVIDIA purchases as usage
grows. Jones argues that NVIDIA can therefore benefit even when it loses particular workloads.

Anthropic, in his account, uses Amazon Trainium, Google TPUs, and NVIDIA capacity through
Microsoft and SpaceX. He cites more than 220,000 NVIDIA GPUs at Colossus 1. Diversification
creates options when price or capacity changes, at the cost of some coordination available to a
more integrated company.

These relationships overlap. Google sells its own chips and NVIDIA systems; Microsoft supports
OpenAI while providing Anthropic capacity; Cursor distributes models that compete with its
owner's models. Jones compares this to Netflix running on AWS while competing with Prime Video.

## A practical budget

At approximately $20 a month, Jones would choose one main paid provider based on weekly work and
maintain familiarity with a serious rival through a free account. He would avoid spending time
comparing every ordinary prompt across many models.

At $60, his default is direct $20 plans from OpenAI and Claude plus Cursor Pro for someone who
codes daily. A Google plan can replace a component when most work is in Workspace. The point is
to fit the budget and workload rather than accumulate subscriptions automatically.

Jones personally pays for multiple $200 frontier plans, including Anthropic, Codex, and Grok.
That is more than a $200 total budget. He expects each plan to justify itself independently
through useful work, saved time, reduced costs, or additional capacity. A plan that does not
earn its place should be reduced or removed.

## Keep the work portable

Jones's consumer analogy is Anthropic's supplier diversification. He uses OpenBrain for memory
he controls, keeps documents as ordinary files, stores code in his repositories, and maintains
portable process instructions and skills. A router can provide model choice without becoming the
sole home of the user's computing environment.

Switching still has costs because models behave differently. The objective is to resume work
without rebuilding its context from nothing. His closing test is whether the disappearance of a
main model would merely be inconvenient or would strand the work itself.
