# Whale Communication, AI, and the Distance Between Structure and Meaning

[summary](../../summary/curious-explorer/2026-07-30-ai-whale-language-claims.md)

## The headline outruns the evidence

The video promises that Grok decoded whale language and uncovered a shocking first message.
Neither its transcript nor the credible sources reviewed for this article identify any role for
Grok, xAI, or a Grok model in the research.
That omission is not a minor naming error. The work discussed belongs to several separate
research programs, species, datasets, and experimental traditions. None reports a validated
whale-to-human translation.
The strongest defensible conclusion is more interesting than the headline, but less cinematic:
computational tools are revealing rich organization in cetacean sounds while their meanings
remain largely unknown.
The source transcript is based on automatic English captions. It visibly mangles names and
terms, including Project CETI, codas, rubato, and several researchers' names. Exact quotations
and technical labels should therefore be taken from the linked papers, not from the captions.
The video also moves repeatedly among humpback whales, sperm whales, and orcas. Evidence from
one species cannot simply be transferred to another. The Twain playback, CETI coda analysis,
vowel-like findings, WhAM, DolphinGemma, and Earth Species Project are distinct lines of work.

## Four questions that the video collapses into one

Research on animal communication often asks at least four different questions:

1. Can researchers detect and classify recurring sound patterns?
2. Can they relate those patterns to speakers, behavior, and social context?
3. Can a model generate a plausible sound or predict what sound may come next?
4. Can experiments show what a signal means to the animal receiving it?
The first three can advance without answering the fourth. A model may sort calls accurately,
recover regular timing, or synthesize realistic audio while knowing nothing about reference,
intent, or meaning.
Human speech recognition offers a useful analogy. Software can cluster unfamiliar syllables or
imitate their sound without knowing whether they refer to food, danger, identity, or nothing at
all. Acoustic similarity is not semantic equivalence.
A translation claim therefore needs evidence beyond pattern discovery. It needs a reproducible
mapping between a signal and a communicative function from the animals' perspective.
Ideally, controlled playback would manipulate the proposed signal and elicit the predicted
response. Results would replicate across individuals and contexts, survive tests against rival
explanations, and generalize beyond the recordings used to build the hypothesis.
A 2026 review warns that acoustic similarity need not match a receiver's perceptual space. That
is one reason careful researchers use behavior and playback, not model confidence alone, to test
meaning ([Current Biology](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0960982226008109)).

## Twain: a real exchange, not a translated conversation

The video's opening episode has a strong factual core. On August 19, 2021, researchers near
Alaska played a recorded humpback contact-call exemplar to an adult female known as Twain.
During a 20-minute trial, the team broadcast 38 exemplars and recorded 36 calls from Twain. The
2023 paper describes coordinated acoustic and behavioral turn-taking, including changes in
response timing across phases of engagement
([PeerJ paper](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC10693240/fullTextXML)).
This was an interactive playback experiment with a wild humpback, not an AI translation. The
researchers had evidence that Twain responded contingently and matched interval variation. They
did not establish an English meaning for her calls or prove that she understood a proposition
composed by humans.
The paper calls the result preliminary. It involved one whale, one opportunistic encounter, and
one playback exemplar, followed by post-hoc analysis. Those limits do not make the event trivial;
they define what can responsibly be inferred from it.
The SETI Institute described the episode as a potential model for communicating with nonhuman
intelligence and quoted the team's interpretation of it as a communicative exchange.
Calling it a "conversation" is acceptable only in this narrow call-and-response sense. It was
not a decoded dialogue, and no verified message passed between English and humpback semantics.
The video uses Twain to lead into sperm-whale research. That transition is rhetorical, not an
evidentiary bridge: humpback contact calls and sperm-whale codas come from different species,
methods, and datasets.

## Project CETI: mapping the structure of sperm-whale codas

Sperm whales produce social click sequences called codas. Project CETI combines long-term field
observation, acoustic recording, behavioral sensing, robotics, and machine learning to study how
those codas are organized and used.
Its central 2024 study analyzed 8,719 annotated codas from the Eastern Caribbean EC-1 clan,
recorded from 2005 through 2018 and estimated to involve at least 60 whales
([Nature Communications](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC11076547/fullTextXML)).
The video rounds this to "9,000 recordings," but codas are the analyzed units, not 9,000 separate
recording sessions. It also says AI found 156 distinct codas. The paper instead discusses about
150 types reported globally and a much richer organization within its Caribbean sample.
Earlier work used a 21-type repertoire for that dataset. The newer analysis reported 18 rhythm
types, five tempo modes, and at least 143 frequently realized rhythm-and-tempo combinations.
These figures should not be compressed into a claim that 156 translated words were found.
The paper identifies four interacting structural dimensions:

- **Rhythm:** the relative timing pattern among clicks.
- **Tempo:** the overall speed at which a coda is produced.
- **Rubato:** gradual timing variation related to neighboring codas.
- **Ornamentation:** additional clicks attached to some patterns.

These features vary with context and can be combined. Whales also overlap, imitate aspects of
one another's codas, and participate in turn-taking exchanges. This is strong evidence that the
signals are more structured than a small menu of rigid, fixed forms.
The authors use "phonetic alphabet" as an analogy for a compact set of reusable acoustic
elements. It is not an alphabet with letters that spell known whale words, still less a bilingual
dictionary.
The paper is explicit about its boundary: it investigates structural elements, not semantics.
It contains no playback experiments that test what particular codas mean to sperm whales.
The findings make duality of patterning a research possibility rather than a demonstrated fact.
In human language, meaningless sound units combine into meaningful forms, which then combine
again. Without established whale meanings, the full linguistic claim cannot yet be tested.
The same limit applies to "grammar." Context-sensitive ordering and combinatorial form are
relevant to grammar, but they do not by themselves prove syntax, compositional semantics, or an
unbounded capacity to create propositions.
The video assigns imagined functions to the dimensions: urgency to tempo, emotion to rubato,
and meaning reversal to ornamentation. The paper does not validate those glosses. They are
narrative illustrations presented as if they were measured semantics.
Nor does the study establish theory of mind. Turn-taking, imitation, and contextual modulation
can arise through several mechanisms. Inferring a representation of another whale's mental
state would require targeted behavioral evidence, not acoustic structure alone.

## Vowel-like patterns: an analogy with important limits

A separate line of research examined spectral patterns within sperm-whale codas. A 2025 paper
reported two recurring categories and diphthong-like trajectories, calling them *a-coda* and
*i-coda vowels* by analogy
([Open Mind](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12594577/fullTextXML)).
The terminology is intentionally comparative. Human vowels are phonemic categories that can
distinguish meaning. No such referential relationship has been established for sperm-whale
codas, as the paper itself states.
A 2026 follow-up analyzed 3,948 speaker-identified codas before applying further quality filters.
It reported parallels involving duration, category interaction, individual baselines, and
coarticulation-like effects
([Proceedings B](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2025.2994)).
These results matter because similar organizational principles may appear in very different
sound-production systems. They do not show that sperm whales produce human vowels, use the same
categories, or attach the same functions to them.
The video calls the patterns acoustically and geometrically identical to human vowels and treats
that resemblance as convergent evolution toward language. Both steps go beyond the studies.
Structural analogy can motivate experiments; it cannot substitute for them.
AI helped researchers locate patterns and formulate hypotheses, while explicit acoustic
analysis characterized them. Neither paper presents semantic translation.

## The “I am here” message was not verified

The video's centerpiece is the 1+1+3 coda, a recognizable timing pattern with two more isolated
clicks followed by three quicker ones. It is common in the Eastern Caribbean material.
The video first says researchers only "may" understand it, then converts a tentative gloss into
a dramatic declaration: the first decoded message was "I am here."
No reviewed CETI paper assigns that proposition to 1+1+3 or to any other coda. The 2025 and 2026
papers explicitly say that the meanings of particular codas or sequences have not been revealed.
“I am here” is therefore an unsupported narrative gloss, not a validated semantic translation.
It should not be repeated as a scientific finding, even with the softer phrase "scientists
believe," unless a primary source and a tested meaning are supplied.
Claims that this message has been sent for 30 million years compound the problem. The age of a
lineage cannot establish the age, stability, or meaning of a particular vocal pattern.
Nothing credible reviewed here shows that whales have been discussing humans for centuries or
that researchers decoded what they supposedly said about us.

## What WhAM does—and does not do

WhAM, presented at NeurIPS 2025, is a transformer trained on roughly 10,000 codas. It can produce
synthetic sperm-whale codas from audio prompts and learn representations useful for classifying
rhythm, social unit, and vowel-like categories
in its reported evaluations.
Its title describes a step "towards" a translative model. That does not make it a translator in
the ordinary semantic sense. Its reported evaluations concern acoustic generation and feature
classification, not conversion between whale meanings and human language.
Generation can still be scientifically useful. A controlled system can create stimuli that
vary one feature while holding others steady, enabling playback experiments that natural
recordings alone may not permit.
But plausible output is not comprehension. A model can generate a whale-like coda without
knowing what a whale would perceive, expect, or do after hearing it.

## DolphinGemma is a different project on a different species

Google's DolphinGemma is an approximately 400-million-parameter audio-in/audio-out model for
pattern discovery, next-sound prediction, and dolphin-like sound generation
([Google announcement](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/products/dolphingemma)).
It concerns dolphins, not sperm whales. Google also distinguishes it from CHAT, an underwater
system intended to establish a small, synthetic shared vocabulary linked to objects.
CHAT is not designed to decipher dolphins' natural communication. A learned artificial
vocabulary would demonstrate an interface animals can use, not a translation of their existing
calls.
As of the fact-check cutoff, Google DeepMind still described DolphinGemma as in development and
presented two-way communication as a possible future outcome
([DeepMind model page](https://deepmind.google/models/gemma/dolphingemma)).
DolphinGemma should therefore not be cited as evidence that whale or dolphin language has
already been decoded.

## Earth Species Project and the wider bioacoustics field

Earth Species Project builds datasets and general bioacoustic models for detection,
classification, representation learning, and pattern discovery. Its work includes cetacean
research, but its public materials do not report a validated whale-to-human translator
([ESP](https://earthspecies.org)).
Models such as NatureLM-audio aim to work across species and tasks. That breadth is useful for
finding recurring structures in large unlabeled archives
([ESP publications](https://earthspecies.org/what-we-do/publications)).
The distinction remains the same: detecting a call, identifying a caller or clan, clustering
similar signals, predicting a sequence, and generating plausible audio are all valuable. None
alone identifies what an animal means.
NOAA likewise describes sperm-whale codas as organized and culturally transmitted clan markers
while emphasizing recording, identification, and comparison rather than English translation
([NOAA Fisheries](https://www.fisheries.noaa.gov/science-blog/diving-deep-sperm-whale-clicks)).

## Historical stories are not decoded testimony

The video uses historical whaling to suggest sperm whales transmitted ocean-wide warnings about
humans. It cites a reported 58% decline in whalers' strike success within roughly 2.5 years of
entering new Pacific grounds.
Even if that statistic is correctly represented, it supports rapid behavioral adaptation more
directly than it supports a particular communication channel or message. Local social learning,
movement, selection, changing tactics, and record bias are alternative explanations.
A decline in whaling success cannot reveal the content of codas or prove that information moved
thousands of miles through an ocean-wide warning network.
The transcript also imagines grief, warnings, or historical records in clicks heard after whales
were harpooned. It admits those sounds were never decoded. The proposed meanings are therefore
speculation, not recovered testimony.
The 1820 sinking of the *Essex* is similarly turned into a language story. The video suggests
repair hammering may have sounded like a coda and prompted a deliberate response.
That hypothesis is not established by the acoustic studies discussed above. Historical reports
of ramming do not reveal the whale's perception, motive, or intent, and they do not demonstrate
revenge or coordinated counterattack.

The same caution applies to Iberian orcas interacting with sailboats. Social transmission of a
behavior is compatible with culture, but it does not prove that vocal language taught the
behavior or that the animals are retaliating against humans.

The video presents a trauma-origin story as fact and later concedes that scientists do not know
the motive. Responsible reporting should preserve that uncertainty rather than choosing the
most emotionally satisfying explanation.

## Surveillance, injury, and other unsupported escalations

The transcript tells of 25 sperm whales allegedly detecting underwater microphones, discussing
surveillance, and coordinating silence. It provides no reliable primary source for this account.

Passive hydrophones listen rather than transmit. Whales could react to boats, handling noise,
active equipment, or unrelated circumstances, but silence alone cannot establish that they
detected surveillance or discussed it.

The video also compares a reported 230 dB underwater sperm-whale source level directly with a
jet engine and airborne human-injury thresholds. Those numbers are not directly comparable
because air and water acoustics use different reference pressures and propagation conditions.

Risk also depends on distance, frequency, duration, directionality, and exposure. The claim that
a whale click can vibrate or dissolve a human body from within is unsupported sensationalism.

An anecdote about temporary arm numbness is not controlled evidence of causation. The shared
anatomy of echolocation and social sound production does not justify calling every powerful
click a weapon or a "killing click."

## Conservation ethics do not require a fictional translation

Cetacean communication research has clear ethical significance even before semantics are known.
Whales live in social groups, learn culturally variable behaviors, depend heavily on sound, and
face human-generated noise and other pressures.

Noise can mask biologically important signals and alter behavior. Effects depend on source,
level, duration, habitat, and species; broad claims about universal internal injury or inevitable
death need specific evidence.

The video turns acoustic masking into the destruction of a language and treats legal advocacy
as if it followed from a completed translation. That argument skips the uncertain middle.

Legal declarations and rights proposals should also be described according to their actual
status and jurisdiction, not automatically called binding personhood law. Scientific evidence,
ethical reasoning, and legal force are separate questions.

Conservation does not have to wait for a whale dictionary. Social complexity, cultural variation,
long lives, and acoustic dependence already support caution toward disruptive noise, ship
strikes, entanglement, and habitat degradation.

## What would count as progress toward translation?

A credible program would begin with synchronized data: who vocalized, which animals were near,
what each was doing, and what changed before and after the signal.

Researchers would propose a narrow function, such as coordinating a dive, identifying a caller,
or requesting reunion. The hypothesis would specify observable predictions before testing.

Playback stimuli would then preserve relevant acoustic details while varying the proposed cue.
Responses would need objective scoring, suitable controls, and safeguards against observer and
model bias.

The same relationship should recur across encounters, individuals, and social settings. Tests
should distinguish reference from arousal, identity, familiarity, sequence expectation, and
simple acoustic novelty.

Independent groups should reproduce the result. If a model generates stimuli, researchers must
show that whales perceive them as intended rather than merely reacting to an unusual sound.

A translation system would ultimately need bidirectional validation: it should predict whale
responses to controlled messages and correctly infer functions from new signals outside the
training corpus.

Even then, a functional gloss such as "contact call" would not automatically justify a quoted
English sentence. Translation should reflect the resolution of the evidence, not the drama of
the headline.

## A more accurate conclusion

The real achievement is not that Grok—or any AI—has solved whale language. No Grok involvement
is identified, no validated semantic translation exists, and no verified "I am here" message
has been received.

What researchers have shown is that humpbacks can engage in contingent playback exchanges and
that sperm-whale codas contain rich, context-sensitive, combinatorial acoustic structure.
Separate work has found vowel-like spectral organization and built models that classify or
generate plausible sounds.

Those results narrow the search space for meaning. They can guide better field observation,
more controlled playback, and more precise hypotheses. They are tools for asking whales better
questions, not evidence that scientists can already read the answers.

The most responsible response is neither dismissal nor credulity. It is disciplined wonder:
protect the animals, improve the experiments, and keep structure, behavior, generation, and
semantic translation clearly separated.
