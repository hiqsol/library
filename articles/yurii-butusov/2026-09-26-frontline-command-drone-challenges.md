# Ukraine's Frontline Adaptation, Drone Shortfalls, and the Next Threat

## A case study in active defense

Yurii Butusov presents the fighting near Lyman as a successful active-defense operation,
not as the opening phase of a campaign to liberate all of Donetsk region. In his account,
Russian troops had crossed to the right bank of the Chornyi Zherebets River, formed a
large bridgehead, threatened Lyman, and entered the city more than once. He also says
that they took Shandryholove and most of Drobysheve.

Butusov says Ukrainian forces first stopped the advance and then counterattacked. He
credits the Third Army Corps with effectively eliminating the bridgehead. He stresses
that this was achieved without sending in extra assault regiments or an Air Assault
Forces brigade. He also notes support from some National Guard and State Border Guard
units, while describing the operation as principally a Ground Forces success.

For him, the result matters because it interrupted a familiar pattern: Russian
infiltration, an improvised Ukrainian defense, and the eventual loss of a settlement.
The Lyman example instead shows how local forces can move from defense to a sharp
counterstroke through preparation, organization, and command quality.

The interview links this counterattack to Operation Vivaldi. Interviewer Yanina Sokolova
cites a reported second-stage maneuver that used unmanned ground systems, a north-to-
south approach through ravines, and air-defense and electronic-warfare measures to hide
the plan and isolate the battlefield. Those operational details are introduced by the
interviewer, not independently established by Butusov in this exchange. Butusov does
say that the head of unmanned systems in the Third Army Corps, known by the call sign
Fil, made a major contribution to Vivaldi and to the corps' drone operations.

The example supports a limited proposition: effective planning, surprise, integration,
and delegated execution can produce gains without a numerical advantage or a large new
reserve. It does not establish that the wider front has turned, that all reported terrain
was permanently secured, or that deoccupation of Donetsk region has begun. Nor does the
interview independently verify the losses, territorial claims, or strategic effects
attached to the operation.

## Drapatyi's reforms and the Third Army Corps model

Butusov does not describe Mykhailo Drapatyi as a simple replacement for Oleksandr
Syrskyi. He says Syrskyi controlled a unified command vertical and could influence
appointments and decisions down to operational and tactical levels. Drapatyi, in his
view, does not possess the same concentration of authority. Separate senior officials
have their own responsibilities and views, so a change of commander has not produced a
complete change of system.

Even so, Butusov identifies several reforms that he attributes to Drapatyi. The most
important is the creation of larger force groupings based on functioning army corps. He
says two such groupings each contain three corps and argues that the whole front should
eventually be divided among no more than four or five army-level groupings. In his view,
this concentrates authority and responsibility while improving coordination.

The Third Army Corps is his practical example. He hopes that placing its commander,
Andrii Biletskyi, over the Center force grouping and three corps will allow successful
methods to spread. He also welcomes Fil's appointment to lead unmanned systems across
the Armed Forces, seeing it as an effort to scale a field-tested corps capability.

Butusov highlights two further changes. First, corps are to establish training
battalions for infantry rather than depend entirely on distant training centers that
prepare personnel and then distribute them across unrelated formations. Second, assault
regiments are no longer treated chiefly as a commander-in-chief's mobile reserve. They
are being subordinated to particular army corps.

These changes represent a move toward durable command relationships, not proof that all
command problems have been solved. Butusov explicitly says there has been no wholesale
replacement of one model by another. His praise of Drapatyi concerns specific reforms;
it should not be converted into the headline claim that Drapatyi definitively
"corrected Syrskyi's mistake."

## From plugging gaps to supporting a corps

Butusov's sharpest criticism of the earlier assault-unit model is organizational. He
says assault regiments were repeatedly sent to plug collapsing sectors, then expanded
as new gaps appeared. Tasks could arrive overnight without adequate reconnaissance,
coordination, prepared positions, or fire planning. He associates that pattern with
heavy casualties and missing personnel.

He rejects the idea that these outcomes necessarily resulted from malicious commanders
or poor soldiers. His explanation is failed planning and employment. Under corps-level
subordination, assault units can work within a known sector, support formations already
holding the defense, and prepare jointly rather than arrive after the defense has
broken. He calls this a change in the philosophy of Ground Forces employment.

This argument supports the case for continuity, local knowledge, and integrated
planning. It does not show that reassignment alone will reduce losses. Outcomes will
still depend on intelligence, logistics, training, fire support, command competence,
and the condition of the units involved.

## Drones: a battlefield shortage and a planning failure

Butusov says drone provision has recently deteriorated sharply. On his sector, he
estimates fulfillment at only 10–15 percent of need. He also says he sees similar
shortages of drones and money across many units in the area. This is a frontline
estimate from one officer, not a verified national procurement statistic.

He cannot identify a single cause, but points to weak planning for finance, acquisition,
and distribution. The contrast is central to his argument: the Third Army Corps offers
an example of scaling drone employment inside a formation, while the wider supply system
still fails to provide enough systems for routine combat work.

The 10–15 percent figure supports the conclusion that units in the area he observes face
a severe shortfall. It does not prove that every formation receives the same share, that
all drone categories are equally scarce, or that national deliveries have fallen to
that level.

## Personnel, foreign volunteers, and the TCC system

Butusov connects equipment shortages to an equally serious lack of people. He argues
that Ukraine has failed to build a funded system for recruiting foreign volunteers even
though, he says, demand is far greater than the available intake. He estimates that
about 3,000 foreigners would be brought in during 2026, compared with about 2,500 in the
previous year, and calls that result a failure. He says queues run into the tens of
thousands and that the Khartiia Corps alone has around 5,000 prospective recruits whom
it cannot process for lack of stable funding.

According to Butusov, much of the work has depended on businesses, limited support from
territorial recruitment centers, and a network organized by the commander of Khartiia's
23rd Assault Regiment. He says promised state payments and targeted funding did not
reach the corps. He also identifies transport, legal status, and pay as unresolved
barriers. A newly authorized National Guard recruitment center gives him some hope, but
he expects its practical results only after funding, activation, and processing delays.

His broader criticism concerns Ukraine's Territorial Recruitment and Social Support
Centers, or TCCs. He contrasts spending on coercive street mobilization and large
training centers with the failure to fund willing foreign recruits. In his view, this
removes taxpayers and workers from the economy, damages trust, and still does not solve
infantry shortages. He alleges that many trainees leave training centers, but the
interview supplies no independent data for that claim.

These figures and criticisms illuminate bottlenecks experienced by Butusov's formation.
They do not establish the total foreign-recruit pipeline, the national desertion rate,
or the relative combat effectiveness of foreign and Ukrainian recruits. They also do
not answer the legal, security-vetting, language, integration, and long-term personnel
questions that a national program would need to resolve.

## Trump, sanctions, and energy prices

Butusov interprets Donald Trump's Ukraine messaging primarily through US electoral and
political incentives. He says Trump wants to display another peace initiative to voters
and to preserve some relationship with Russia. He also argues that Trump is trying to
shift attention from energy-price increases that Butusov associates with the US war
with Iran, while blaming Ukrainian attacks on Russian oil infrastructure.

In Butusov's reading, appeals to Vladimir Putin are not a strategy for defeating Russian
aggression. He sees them as short-lived political messaging that weakens US influence.
The interview also discusses legislation allowing Trump to impose tariffs of up to 100
percent on countries buying Russian energy. Sokolova stresses that this is discretionary
power, not automatic sanctions; Butusov offers no reason to expect decisive use of it.

This section is political interpretation, not evidence of Trump's private motives or a
causal analysis of world energy prices. It supports the narrower observation that
sanctions authority matters only when implemented and that US domestic politics can
shape how policy is presented. It does not prove why Trump acts, what future measures he
will take, or how markets would respond.

## Hybrid pressure on NATO and the Baltic risk

Sokolova describes drone incidents, sabotage, and other suspected Russian activity in
Europe, then asks whether Russia could test NATO with limited force. Butusov calls
military provocations or operations against European states, especially the Baltic
countries, a real scenario. His reasoning is that Russia benefits from destabilization,
Europe is divided, US support is not consistently organized, and NATO is not acting
proactively.

He adds a major condition: in his view, such a scenario becomes plausible only after
Russia ends the war in Ukraine in some form. He does not predict an imminent invasion.
He also says Russia currently lacks the resources for a new major thrust from the north
against Ukraine because its forces are committed and suffering heavy losses.

Butusov further argues that Ukrainian intelligence has good visibility into Russian
operational and strategic preparations, so a new strike grouping should be detected in
advance. That is his assessment, not a guarantee. Hybrid actions can remain ambiguous,
and accurate warning does not itself ensure political agreement or an effective NATO
response.

His comments support Baltic and Polish efforts to prepare for coercion and limited
provocation. They do not prove that Russia has selected a Baltic target, that NATO is
legally or militarily incapable of responding, or that Article 5 would fail.

## Jet-powered drones and the next procurement race

Butusov describes jet-powered long-range drones as a growing threat. They are faster and
less vulnerable to existing air defenses than propeller-driven systems, while Russia is
scaling production and improving precision and range. The interviewer adds a warning,
attributed to the specialist known as Flash, that cheap missile-like weapons could
follow within roughly six months. That timing is not Butusov's verified forecast.

The deeper issue, Butusov argues, is not surprise. Russia's advantages in ballistic and
cruise missiles have been visible throughout the war, and the development of faster
drones has been publicly discussed. Ukraine's problem is delayed adaptation: the state
waits for a weapon to inflict damage, watches volunteers and engineers improvise a
response, and only then funds what has already been demonstrated.

He cites heavy investment in propeller-driven FPV interceptors after that requirement
had become urgent, while orders for jet-capable interceptors lagged behind the new
threat. He expects the next step may require radar-linked air-defense systems using
small, inexpensive missiles. His figure of roughly one billion euros invested in the
older interceptor response is an estimate stated in the interview, not an audited total.

For Butusov, technological initiative requires advance funding for research and
development, testing, production, and the units that will employ new weapons. It also
requires administrators and engineers who can turn money into a scalable capability.
Changing a commander or defense minister cannot substitute for that system.

His examples support a warning that procurement cycles can trail battlefield change.
They do not prove that propeller-driven interceptors are now useless, that one predicted
weapon will arrive on schedule, or that a single interceptor design can answer every
threat. A layered response would still have to balance sensors, electronic warfare,
guns, interceptor drones, missiles, dispersal, and protection of critical sites.

## Winter preparation without fatalism

Butusov rejects the idea that one winter is a single existential deadline. Every season
of this war has carried severe risk, he says. The more useful question is whether
Ukraine can scale effective command decisions, plan ahead, and act before visible damage
forces a response.

At unit level, his winter program is practical: improve positions, provide resilient
power supplies, and prepare winter camouflage. He describes military life as executing
today's mission with the people and equipment available while still planning ahead.
That is not a national winter-energy plan, but it offers a usable readiness framework.

For units and households, the principle translates into inspecting shelter and heating,
securing backup power and charging, testing communications, preparing cold-weather
clothing and camouflage where relevant, dispersing critical supplies, and rehearsing
what to do during outages or attacks. Exact measures must follow official safety advice
and local conditions.

The interview's recurring lesson is narrower than a promise of victory and more useful
than fatalism. Lyman is evidence that better organization can change a local outcome.
The shortages and delayed procurement show why one success cannot be generalized. The
case for adaptation rests on repeating proven practices, measuring supply honestly, and
funding the next response before the next threat becomes routine.
