# When Programming Stops Feeling Like a Craft: Vlad Ten on AI, Work, and Agency

In this interview, Vlad Ten and the hosts of *Мы обречены* ask a question narrower
and more personal than whether AI will eliminate software jobs: what if the job remains,
but the activity that made someone want to become a programmer disappears?

Their answer is deliberately pessimistic. Agentic coding may leave people employed while
moving the satisfying work to a machine and retaining waiting, review, testing, coordination,
and accountability for humans. Yet the conversation also supplies its own counterarguments:
agents can remove drudgery, help people learn, make services easier to use, and let small teams
attempt work that once required much more labor.

The episode is best read as testimony about changing craft, identity, and agency, not as a
measured forecast of employment. Vlad repeatedly speaks from his own experience and values.
Several market, salary, revenue, and product claims are anecdotes or jokes. Two promotional
segments also contain sponsor claims rather than independent evaluations.

## From programmer to supervisor

The central thesis appears in the cold open and returns throughout the interview. Vlad says it
feels as though programming itself is leaving programmers, rather than programmers simply
choosing to leave the field [00:07:56–00:08:15].

For him, “programmer” was not merely a job title. It was an identity expressed through tools,
languages, handles, avatars, keyboards, architectural arguments, and the expectation of learning
new technologies for the rest of one's career [00:08:15–00:10:34].

He does not predict that every developer will suddenly become unemployed. His darker scenario is
that many will keep their jobs while losing the part they liked: directly shaping software. The
remaining work is specification, supervision, verification, integration, and explanation
[00:10:44–00:11:50].

The hosts compare the change to turning every mechanically rich game into interactive cinema.
The title still exists, and the player still makes decisions, but movement, combat, and mastery
have been replaced by choosing among options [00:12:34–00:14:05].

That distinction matters. Faster production is not automatically a gift of free time. Vlad says
AI does not merely allow a developer to do more; organizations can require the developer to do
more [00:09:49–00:10:34].

## The queue metaphor

The episode's clearest description of agentic work arrives near the midpoint. Waiting for an
agent can feel like standing in a queue for two hours, or for an entire workday
[00:55:34–00:57:15].

The developer watches output accumulate while suspecting that the agent chose the wrong path. If
that suspicion proves correct, the human must inspect a large body of unfamiliar code, identify
the mistake, and restart. The agent generated the work, but responsibility remains with the
person delivering it [00:56:36–00:57:38].

Manual typing once paced implementation close to the speed of thought. By writing the code, the
developer built a mental model of it. Generated code can arrive faster than a person can absorb,
so speed at the keyboard may become cognitive overload at the review stage
[00:57:38–00:58:09].

The speakers frame this as a shift in an old conflict. Businesses want more features; engineers
want systems that remain understandable and maintainable. Instant output strengthens the case
for “more” and weakens an engineer's ability to argue for “better” [00:58:09–00:59:20].

This is an experience claim, not a universal productivity result. Different tasks, models,
repositories, controls, and teams can produce different outcomes. The interview offers no study
of time saved, defect rates, incidents, rework, or long-term maintenance.

## Why adoption can win before quality is settled

Vlad argues that corporate belief may matter more than whether AI code is objectively excellent.
A technology can become standard because organizations adopt it, just as an imperfect language
can dominate through ecosystem and momentum [00:28:20–00:30:14].

The group says ordinary human code was often mediocre too, so generated code does not have to be
perfect to be commercially acceptable. Recent models, in their view, are often “not worse than
us,” which is a lower and more plausible bar than flawless engineering [00:29:34–00:30:08].

They also acknowledge a boundary. Models are weaker in proprietary or unusual domains with little
public data. Junior humans also struggle there, but domain experts may retain an advantage when
the model cannot reconstruct the relevant context [00:30:14–00:31:11].

Later, they explicitly limit much of the critique to generic application and service development.
Operating systems, specialized hardware, and scarce technical domains still demand deep knowledge
that public-code training may not provide [01:07:43–01:08:34].

## What remains valuable

System design may become more important, not less. Someone must decide what agents should build,
how responsibilities should be divided, and how the components should fit together
[00:31:11–00:32:01].

That answer does not comfort everyone. A person who enjoyed small technical problems may not want
to become a solution architect or agent manager. Preserving employability and preserving the same
kind of satisfaction are separate goals.

The discussion of machine-learning work makes a similar task-level distinction. An unnamed Meta
researcher reportedly still develops a core computer-vision algorithm while AI handles dataset
plumbing, visualization, and surrounding code [00:32:01–00:33:46].

This is a second-hand anecdote, not evidence about the whole ML profession. Its useful point is
that automation acts on tasks, not job titles. Research, product judgment, data selection, and
routine implementation may change at different rates.

An image-search anecdote reinforces the role of product judgment. Improving relevance can still
hurt retention if repeated users expect the same familiar results. Technical improvement must fit
behavioral and business constraints [00:34:17–00:35:14].

## Learning when answers arrive immediately

The speakers mourn the decline of searching as a programming practice. Stack Overflow rarely gave
someone the right answer without detours. Reading adjacent questions and failed approaches created
incidental knowledge [01:04:50–01:06:10].

An LLM can still teach. One host learned about anti-bot systems by asking why synthetic browsers
failed during a scraping project. But understanding was optional: he could also have said “fix it”
and accepted the output [01:06:10–01:07:43].

The literal claim that Stack Overflow “closed” is inaccurate or rhetorical. The public Q&A site did
not simply disappear. The defensible observation is cultural: some developers now ask assistants
instead of searching the site, reducing the role it once played in everyday work.

The hosts fear a future in which technical knowledge becomes optional and the remaining skill is
choosing models, posing requests, and arranging critique loops. That is their concern, not a proven
endpoint of software education [01:07:10–01:07:43].

That creates an uncomfortable possibility for Vlad. Perhaps agentic work is liberating for people

## “Open source is dead” is a provocation, not a finding

Vlad's strongest slogan is that open source is dead, at least in its previous form
[01:14:22–01:16:16]. Two mechanisms sit behind the phrase.

First, cheap generation can make submitting a pull request nearly effortless while review remains
costly. Maintainers may face low-context or low-quality AI-generated contributions and respond by
restricting access to trusted contributors [01:14:22–01:15:02].

Reports of AI-generated pull-request burden make this concern plausible. They remain project-level
experiences, not evidence that all open-source development has collapsed or must become closed.
Code, communities, governance, reputation, maintenance, and user trust still matter.

Second, the hosts argue that source code loses scarcity when models can recreate familiar patterns.
That may reduce the teaching value of reading a particular implementation, but it does not erase
licenses, provenance, security review, reproducibility, maintenance history, or collaboration.

The categorical slogan therefore outruns the argument. A more supportable conclusion is that cheap
code generation raises review costs and changes incentives for maintainers.

## Books, training data, and a partly remembered legal story

The speakers then recall a report about Anthropic buying physical books, cutting off their
bindings, scanning the pages, and destroying the paper [01:16:16–01:18:40]. They openly admit that
they do not remember every detail, and the discussion appears to mix reporting and litigation.

The U.S. Copyright Office's summary of *Bartz v. Anthropic* confirms the central physical-book
process. Anthropic bought books and destructively scanned them. It also used pirated copies from
online shadow libraries.

The ruling was mixed. The court treated model training and the digital library made from purchased
and scanned books as fair use. It did not treat the retained library of pirated copies as fair use.
That distinction is essential; “the court approved everything” and “all training was unlawful”
would both misstate the summarized ruling.

The interview's unexplained claim that the paper had to be destroyed for a special legal reason is
not established by the material checked here. It should remain an admitted gap, not be completed
with speculation.

## Work, hobbies, and the value of doing the thing

Warhammer miniature painting gives the conversation a positive counterexample. An image model can
repaint a photograph of a miniature, but that output does not replace the hobby. The value lies in
mixing paints, improving technique, making mistakes, and handling the object [01:27:49–01:31:09].

The group calls the practice almost ritualistic. The miniature need not be objectively superior;
it matters that the person painted it. Outsourcing the process defeats the purpose.

This clarifies the programming complaint. The hosts are not only defending a market for manually
written code. They are defending activities whose difficulty creates attention, competence,
memory, and attachment.

It also explains their ambivalence. Automation is welcome when it removes pointless transcription.
Two speakers remember copying old records by hand, only to discover in one case that the records
were already digitized [01:18:40–01:19:19].

Their complaint is that AI appears to automate creative construction while leaving people to
supervise, test, and manage. Vlad's “seagull manager” sends a Jira task to an LLM, checks it,
and returns to demand revisions [01:19:19–01:20:05].

## Vlad's personal account of ambition

The later interview becomes less about technology and more about Vlad's pattern of reinvention. He
has moved through stand-up, IT, YouTube, education, and boxing. Rather than describing this as
success, he says he repeatedly stopped before an outcome could become final
[01:40:16–01:43:25].

His formulation is unusually candid: staying “promising” can be more comfortable than trying fully
and discovering that one has failed. This is personal interpretation, not an objective biography.

Boxing gives him perspective. He has met people who trained for years and encountered hard limits,
including failure to reach a national team or an unpromising professional record
[01:43:25–01:45:17]. These are anecdotes, and the career implications of a specific record are not
universal.

The hosts debate whether an ordinary, repetitive life makes someone an “NPC.” Phil resists the
term because strangers look generic only when their stories are unknown. Other speakers define
agency as turning out of an assigned current and making something, even privately
[01:45:17–01:52:02].

The debate never fully resolves. Creation can be a humane source of agency, but making output into
a measure of human worth reproduces the productivity pressure they criticize.

## Progress can help the world and hurt the person

Near the end, the speakers distrust Silicon Valley's language of “improving the world,” yet concede
that mixed motives can coexist. Leaders may believe a mission while also benefiting from it, and a
technology can produce help and harm at the same time [01:52:02–01:54:19].

They name accessible knowledge, tutoring, troubleshooting, and preliminary health guidance as ways
AI might help [01:54:18–01:54:42]. These are possible use cases, not evidence that an AI provides a
safe medical diagnosis. Health outputs require professional and regulatory safeguards.

The episode's mature position is not that progress must stop. Vlad says AI may be good for the
world while making his own work and life less joyful. A displaced craftsperson is not obliged to
celebrate the displacement [01:19:48–01:20:31].

## The perfect arrow

Phil closes with a childhood story. He and his brother carved arrows from wood. One arrow came out
especially well, flew better than the others, and became valuable because Phil had struggled to
make it [01:57:46–01:58:19].

His brother then found perfectly round construction blanks that could be cut into uniformly good
arrows. The result was objectively better and easier. Phil nevertheless lost interest and soon
stopped playing with the bow [01:58:19–01:58:54].

The story captures the interview better than its sensational title. The fear is not simply that a
machine will make worse code or take every job. It is that abundant, standardized competence can
erase the difficulty through which a person formed skill, distinction, and attachment.

That fear is real as experience even when the economic forecast is uncertain. It also points to a
practical question the episode does not settle: which parts of work should be optimized for output,
and which should remain human because doing them is itself the source of value?
