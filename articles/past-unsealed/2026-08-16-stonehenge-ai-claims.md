# Stonehenge AI Claims: A Fabricated Study Built from Real Archaeology

[summary](../../summary/past-unsealed/2026-08-16-stonehenge-ai-claims.md)

Past Unsealed says a peer-verified 2026 AI study analyzed every Stonehenge scan, measurement, and
archaeological record. According to the video, the system discovered a lunar computer, an
infrasound machine, a pentatonic stone instrument, a landscape-scale master plan, and evidence of
knowledge inherited from a lost civilization.

No identifiable study matching that description could be found. The video gives no paper title,
authors, journal, DOI, institution, model name, dataset, methods, code, or repository. Searches of
Crossref, OpenAlex, arXiv, and official Stonehenge research found no matching work. The three
quoted validators—Jennifer Walsh, Marcus Chen, and Sarah Bradford—could not be connected to
Stonehenge research in the claimed specialties.

This does not prove that nobody with those common names exists. It does establish that the video's
assertion of a published and independently verified analysis is unsupported. “Peer-verified” is
also not a recognized publication status. The narrative appears to combine real archaeological
facts and older disputed theories with invented measurements, quotations, and expert reactions.

## What is real

Stonehenge is an extraordinary Neolithic and Bronze Age monument built and altered over many
centuries. Its major sarsens average about 25 tonnes, and the largest surviving upright is about
30 tonnes. Most sarsens were traced geochemically to West Woods, about 25 kilometres north of the
site: https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.abc0133

The smaller “bluestones” are several different rock types. Many dolerites and rhyolites came from
the Preseli region of west Wales, roughly 225–250 kilometres away. The Altar Stone is a separate
case: a 2024 study traced its geological fingerprint to northeast Scotland, at least 700 kilometres
away: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07652-1

The builders used sophisticated stone dressing and woodworking-style joints. The lintels have
mortise-and-tenon and tongue-and-groove connections. That craftsmanship is real, but it is not
lost technology. Archaeological debris, tool marks, experiments, and reconstruction support the
use of stone mauls, ropes, sledges, prepared tracks, pits, ramps, levers, timber frames, and large
coordinated workforces.

English Heritage summarizes the evidence and uncertainty around construction here:
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/building-stonehenge/

Experimental transport demonstrates feasibility rather than the exact historical method. A UCL
team moved a one-tonne block on a birch sledge and timber track using about ten people. That result
cannot be scaled mechanically to every 25-tonne sarsen, but it contradicts the suggestion that
Neolithic transport was inherently impossible.

## The alleged 2026 AI study does not exist in the scholarly record

The video says every finding comes from a 2026 analysis checked by independent specialists in
physics, engineering, astronomy, and archaeology. It supplies none of the identifiers needed to
locate or reproduce that work.

An OpenAlex search returned 97 Stonehenge-titled works from 2025–2026. None described the alleged
system or included the three named experts. Crossref searches found genuine contemporary
Stonehenge research but no matching article. An arXiv title search returned only unrelated older
records.

Negative database searches cannot exclude a private or unpublished experiment. They are decisive
against the video's stronger claim that this was a published, peer-verified study. A credible
account would name the paper, authors, institution, model, training data, measurement uncertainty,
statistical protocol, and independent replications.

The absence matters because the video presents precise results: millimetre measurements, exact
centimetre errors, near-zero chance probabilities, acoustic frequencies, and nine alignments under
0.3 degrees. Precision in narration is not evidence of measurement.

## The Aubrey Holes are not a new AI discovery

Stonehenge has 56 pits known as the Aubrey Holes. They belong to the monument's early phase around
3000 BCE. Their original use remains debated; they may have held timber posts or stones, and later
cremations were placed in and around them. Only 32 have been excavated.

They were not ignored for three centuries. John Aubrey recorded them in the seventeenth century,
William Hawley excavated many between 1919 and 1926, and their possible astronomical function was
at the centre of a famous dispute during the 1960s and 1970s.

The video claims AI measured all consecutive gaps to fractions of a millimetre and found a
sinusoidal wave encoding the Moon's 18.6-year nodal cycle. No scholarly source reports that result.
The pits are broad archaeological features reconstructed from depressions and partial excavations,
not preserved survey points capable of supporting original millimetre-scale setting-out claims.

The argument that 56 approximates three lunar nodal cycles is old. Three times 18.6 is 55.8. Gerald
Hawkins proposed moving markers around the holes as a lunar or eclipse computer in the 1960s.
English Heritage describes that interpretation as controversial. R. J. C. Atkinson's contemporary
critique identified selection and multiple-comparison problems:
https://doi.org/10.1017/S0003598X0003252X

A numerical resemblance does not establish function. Counting 56 holes is also different from
showing that their irregular spacing encodes lunar declination.

## Stonehenge and the sky

The monument has a deliberate solar axis. Its principal architecture aligns with midsummer sunrise
and midwinter sunset, and the Avenue follows the same general line. Archaeological evidence from
Durrington Walls supports large midwinter gatherings. Seasonal and ritual interpretations are
therefore grounded in more than geometry alone.

Possible lunar significance remains a legitimate but unresolved research question. The long sides
of the Station Stone rectangle point approximately toward a major lunar standstill extreme, and
early cremations cluster in the general sector. English Heritage uses cautious terms such as
“possible” and “may,” and a 2024–25 observation programme was designed to test intentionality.
English Heritage's Stonehenge research portal documents that work:
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/

The video's “nine alignments averaging under 0.3 degrees” has no published list of azimuths,
horizon profiles, dates, uncertainty ranges, or selection rules. Stonehenge contains many points
that can be connected after the fact. Without a predeclared protocol, multiple comparisons make
chance matches likely.

The claim that builders encoded precession-aware stellar targets is still less supported. Modern
software can back-calculate ancient star positions, but a match selected now does not demonstrate
prehistoric intent. English Heritage says there is no evidence for equinox or cross-quarter-day
marking and that beliefs about particular stars and planets cannot be recovered from present
evidence.

Observing solstices, lunar phases, or changing moonrise limits is not equivalent to Keplerian
orbital mechanics. No surviving writing, equation, instrument, or notation demonstrates knowledge
comparable to seventeenth-century celestial mechanics.

## Stonehenge had acoustics, but not the acoustics described

A real 2020 peer-reviewed study used a 1:12 physical scale model of a reconstructed Stonehenge. It
found an average mid-frequency reverberation time of 0.64 seconds and speech amplification of about
4.3 decibels inside the circle: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jas.2020.105218

Those effects could support speech and make music more reverberant while keeping it intelligible.
Sound was more contained inside the monument than projected outward. This is interesting ritual
and social evidence, not proof of a consciousness-altering machine.

The same study tested different prehistoric bluestone arrangements. The acoustic differences were
inaudible, and the authors concluded that sound was unlikely to have motivated those rearrangements.
An earlier simulation identified low-frequency modes around 47–48 Hz but explicitly lacked
archaeological evidence of intentional acoustic design.

The video's phrase “infrasound between 95 and 120 hertz” is technically wrong. Infrasound is below
about 20 Hz. Frequencies from 95 to 120 Hz are audible bass. The video does not explain whether it
has confused full-scale frequencies with a scale-model conversion.

No credible study maps acoustic standing-wave nodes to Stonehenge cremations. The chronology also
works against the story: many cremations belong to the early Aubrey Hole phase around 3000 BCE,
while the main sarsen architecture was erected around 2500 BCE. A standing-wave pattern would vary
with frequency, source, monument phase, and listener position rather than defining one permanent
set of “hot spots.”

Research on low-frequency noise does not establish predictable visions, trance, or hallucinations.
A randomized study exposing participants to genuine 6 Hz infrasound for 28 nights found no global
behavioral effects: https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-82203-6

Ritual, rhythm, darkness, expectation, crowd behaviour, and reverberation can affect experience.
That is different from demonstrating a targeted neurological mechanism built into the stones.

## Some bluestones ring; a pentatonic instrument is not established

Researchers have reported an unusually high incidence of sonorous rocks in parts of the Preseli
region. Some Stonehenge bluestones can make distinctive, muted tones when struck. This supports the
hypothesis that sound may have contributed to how particular rocks were perceived or selected:
https://doi.org/10.1080/1751696X.2013.860278

The research did not show that surviving bluestones collectively form a tuned pentatonic scale,
were selected by pitch, or occupy harmonic nodes. Many stones now sound dull because they are
weathered or embedded. The institutional account calls ritual selection a possibility and asks for
further archaeological testing, not a demonstrated musical design.

The video takes a real observation—some rocks ring—and adds an unsupported chain of conclusions:
quality-controlled pitch selection, a five-note scale, harmonic placement, sympathetic vibration,
and nervous-system targeting.

## The landscape is complex, not one proven geometric figure

LiDAR, geophysics, excavation, and aerial survey have revealed a dense ceremonial landscape around
Stonehenge. It includes earlier monuments, barrows, pits, enclosures, avenues, timber circles, and
settlement evidence. New survey anomalies can be important, but they require excavation and dating
before researchers can determine whether they are human-made or contemporary.

No credible project reports one landscape-scale geometric figure laid out to metre-level accuracy.
Monuments separated in space and time can be connected into patterns retrospectively. A meaningful
master-plan claim would require independent dating, explicit geometric rules, uncertainty bounds,
and a statistical test against alternative connections.

Published chronology instead describes episodic construction, rebuilding, and rearrangement over
roughly 1,500 years. The first bank, ditch, and Aubrey Holes date to around 3000 BCE; major sarsen
and bluestone settings were erected around 2500 BCE; bluestones were rearranged later; and Y and Z
holes belong broadly to 1800–1500 BCE.

Earlier monuments may have influenced the location. Chronological succession does not establish a
single blueprint transmitted unchanged across 20–25 generations.

## The precision-decline numbers are invented

The video says early stones averaged 3.2 centimetres from acoustically perfect positions, later
stones missed by 12.7 centimetres, and early contact surfaces varied by under two millimetres over
50 centimetres. No official or peer-reviewed source was found for any of those figures.

Historic England's laser scans do reveal dressing, tooling, carvings, and architectural sequence.
They do not report a fourfold decline in acoustic positioning or the stated metalworking tolerance.
The acoustic experiment found changes between bluestone arrangements inaudible, contradicting the
premise that centimetre-scale placement preserved or degraded a tuned instrument.

The story of inherited knowledge decaying with each generation is circular. The video first
assumes a master acoustic design, then treats deviations from that imagined optimum as evidence
that the builders had forgotten it.

## Stonehenge was not carefully “shut down” in 1600 BCE

The video says people deliberately laid fallen stones down, buried vulnerable features, and sealed
the monument like an instrument in a case. Published chronology contradicts that narrative.

Activity and construction continued into roughly 1520 BCE, including stone working, carvings,
occupation, and the Y and Z holes. Those holes appear to have remained open and accumulated
windblown sediment, not protective coverings. Documented stone falls occurred much later, including
1797, 1900, and 1963. English Heritage's history portal summarizes the site's chronology:
https://www.english-heritage.org.uk/visit/places/stonehenge/history-and-stories/history/

There is no archaeological evidence of an orderly closure ceremony designed to preserve a machine
for a future civilization. Reduced use, changing ritual practice, social reorganization, erosion,
stone removal, and natural collapse are more ordinary explanations.

## No evidence for an older lost civilization

Known archaeological communities are associated with Stonehenge's phases, including Grooved Ware,
Durrington Walls, Beaker, and Bronze Age groups. The monument demonstrates organization,
craftsmanship, long-distance connections, empirical observation, and major communal labour.

None of that requires a technologically superior predecessor. The video offers no settlement,
writing, tools, datable objects, genetic discontinuity, or material culture from its proposed lost
source. Its argument depends entirely on the unsupported acoustic, astronomical, precision, and
master-plan claims.

Calling Neolithic people “primitive” creates a false choice: either they lacked sophisticated
knowledge or inherited it from a forgotten civilization. Archaeology instead shows capable people
developing and transmitting specialized practices within changing societies.

## Verdict

The alleged 2026 AI study and quoted experts are not verifiable and are probably fabricated or
misattributed. The video's strongest-sounding numbers have no traceable source. Several claims are
directly wrong: 95–120 Hz is not infrasound, the acoustics study did not find a tuned trance
machine, and Stonehenge was not carefully shut down in 1600 BCE.

Real Stonehenge research is already remarkable. Stones travelled from west Wales, and the Altar
Stone came from Scotland. Builders shaped enormous sarsens with stone technology, created durable
joints, aligned architecture to the solstices, and repeatedly transformed a ceremonial landscape.
Some stones ring, the reconstructed circle has measurable acoustics, and lunar intent remains a
serious but disputed question.

The video turns those findings into false certainty. Its AI framing supplies an authority figure
without a paper, method, or data. The result is not a surprising new interpretation of Stonehenge
but a fabricated research narrative assembled from genuine archaeology, old speculation, and
invented precision.
