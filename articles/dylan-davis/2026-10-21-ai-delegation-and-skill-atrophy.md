# AI Is Removing the Practice That Builds Professional Judgment

Generative AI can complete many of the routine tasks once assigned to junior knowledge workers.
That creates an immediate productivity gain, but it may also remove the practice through which
people learned to recognize good work.

AI consultant Dylan Davis describes this as a problem with the bottom rungs of the career ladder.
New employees historically performed repetitive work, received feedback, noticed recurring
patterns, and gradually acquired judgment. If a model now produces the first draft, the worker may
submit a polished result without developing the experience needed to evaluate it.

The concern is credible, but the video's evidence needs qualification. Recent labor-market research
does show declining demand for entry-level workers and a relative decline in junior employment at
firms associated with generative-AI adoption. It does not prove that AI caused every decline, that
firms are uniformly eliminating junior roles, or that judgment-only training can replace practical
experience in every profession.

## Routine work was also training

Davis presents the old employment bargain as an informal apprenticeship. A new employee accepted
mundane work in exchange for exposure, feedback, and eventual access to more strategic work.

Searching documents taught relevance. Drafting reports and presentations exposed the employee to
standards they could not yet articulate. Repeated numerical work made common errors recognizable.
A senior reviewer's corrections gradually converted repetition into professional judgment.

This makes automation a more complicated trade-off than a task-level productivity calculation. A
task can be easy to automate while still being valuable as deliberate practice. Removing it may
save time today while weakening the organization's future supply of experienced reviewers.

The problem becomes especially visible when workers pass along AI output they have not read. They
have neither produced the work nor performed the review that might turn the interaction into a
learning experience.

## What the labor evidence actually says

Davis cites three statistics as evidence that the apprenticeship ladder is already shrinking. Each
has a real basis, but each is presented more strongly than its source supports.

### Entry-level postings are down, but AI is not the only cause

Davis says apprenticeship-style entry-level jobs fell 35% in three years. The identifiable source
is an analysis by Revelio Labs. It found that U.S. postings for graduate entry-level roles were more
than 35% below their January 2023 level, equivalent to over 100,000 fewer new postings per month.

That result covers graduate entry-level demand across the labor market. It is not a dedicated
measurement of law, consulting, and banking, and it counts postings rather than existing jobs or
completed hires.

Revelio also did not attribute the entire decline to AI. Its observational analysis found that a
10-percentage-point increase in occupational AI exposure was associated with 11% lower entry-level
demand and 7% higher non-entry-level demand after controls. Association is meaningful evidence,
but it is not proof that AI caused the full 35% decline.

### Some bank analyst classes may have fallen by as much as two-thirds

Davis says the largest banks cut incoming analyst classes by two-thirds. The underlying report,
published by Fortune and Bloomberg, used the more limited phrase "by as much as two-thirds." The
estimate came from McKinsey senior partner Debasish Patnaik and described the upper end at some
institutions, not a uniform cut across all major banks.

The same report noted that Bank of America remained committed to 2,000 interns and 2,000 full-time
recruits. No bank-by-bank dataset was published with which to reproduce the headline figure.

### The Harvard paper finds slower junior hiring, not mainly layoffs

The video's "Harvard analysis" is the working paper *Generative AI as Seniority-Biased
Technological Change* by Seyed Mahdi Hosseini Maasoum and Guy Lichtinger. Its June 2026 revision
uses résumé data covering 65 million workers at more than 280,000 firms, rather than the 62 million
mentioned in the video.

The researchers identify an adopting firm when its job postings indicate demand for workers who
integrate generative AI. Junior employment then declines at adopting firms relative to
non-adopters, particularly in AI-exposed occupations, while senior employment trends remain mostly
unchanged.

The distinction Davis misses is the mechanism. The paper says the decline is driven primarily by
slower hiring rather than increased separations. Describing the result as firms "cutting" roughly
10% of junior roles can therefore sound like layoffs when the reported effect is mainly fewer new
hires. The accessible abstract supports the direction of the result, but not the video's precise
10% figure at 18 months.

The paper is also a working paper based on an observational adoption proxy, not a randomized test.
It supports concern about a seniority gap without establishing a universal causal law.

## AI changes the review problem

Davis argues that the risk is not limited to junior staff. Experienced managers know many familiar
signs of weak human work: missing sections, rushed reasoning, careless formatting, or obvious gaps.
AI can wrap a mistake in fluent, confident prose, so visual polish is a poor proxy for correctness.

This is a real failure mode, but the claim that AI errors are generally harder to detect in every
task is too broad. Detectability depends on the model, domain, interface, type of error, available
evidence, and reviewer expertise. A legal citation, spreadsheet formula, construction assumption,
and software defect each require different checks.

Research does support a shift in cognitive effort. A peer-reviewed CHI 2025 study surveyed 319
knowledge workers about 936 examples of generative-AI use. Higher confidence in AI was associated
with less reported critical thinking, while higher self-confidence was associated with more.
Participants described their effort moving toward verification, integration, and task stewardship.

The study is cross-sectional and based on self-reports. It does not prove that AI causes expertise
to disappear. It does, however, reinforce the need to design work so that verification is an active
skill rather than a ceremonial approval step.

## Two proposed models for training judgment

Davis identifies two ways to replace learning that previously occurred inside routine production.

The first is judgment-first training. AI performs the task, and a junior employee must find errors,
explain why they are errors, and articulate the standard the output should meet. Senior experts must
make their tacit criteria explicit instead of merely asking for a rewrite.

Davis attributes this approach to Deloitte, but no matching Deloitte source was located. A closely
related documented example comes from PwC. Its AI assurance leader Jenn Kosar said junior auditors
need to learn how to review and supervise AI. PwC has shifted some training from task execution
toward audit fundamentals, critical thinking, negotiation, and professional skepticism.

That example supports the broader direction, but not the video's exact Deloitte attribution or the
claim that AI performs most associate work.

The second model preserves manual execution for selected skills. A worker completes a task without
AI, asks AI to perform the same task, and then compares both outputs. Reviewers identify weaknesses
in the human result and the AI result, turning the exercise into training for both the employee and
the workflow.

Wall Street Prep and Financial Edge announced this exact three-stage sequence for 2026 new-hire
programs: build the skill manually, apply AI to the same problem, then compare the outputs and
discuss value, risk, and the role of human judgment. The providers say they train analysts at nine
of the ten largest global investment banks.

This verifies that the method exists and is being implemented or marketed. It does not independently
establish how widely banks use it or whether it produces durable improvements in judgment.

## Not every manual skill must survive

Davis does not argue for preserving all existing work. He compares AI dependence with reliance on
GPS and calculators. Society accepts some loss of unaided navigation and mental arithmetic because
the tools provide greater convenience and capability.

The GPS analogy has some empirical support. A *Scientific Reports* study found that greater lifetime
GPS experience correlated with worse spatial memory during unaided navigation. A much smaller
longitudinal follow-up also associated greater GPS use with steeper decline in spatial memory. The
study does not establish the video's broader claim that navigation is extremely difficult for most
people.

The useful question is not whether every manual skill should be protected. It is whether losing a
specific skill creates unacceptable risk, removes the knowledge needed to supervise automation, or
leaves the organization unable to operate when a system fails.

A low-stakes internal email and a legal, financial, medical, construction, or security decision
should not share the same standard. Critical skills may need manual competence, independent testing,
or professional accountability even when AI usually performs the first pass.

## A practical operating framework

Davis's strongest contribution is a management framework rather than a research conclusion.
Organizations can make the apprenticeship trade-off explicit instead of allowing it to emerge by
accident.

### 1. Audit tasks and their learning value

List the work performed by junior employees. For each task, record both its production value and
what repeated performance historically taught: domain patterns, error recognition, source quality,
client context, or professional standards.

### 2. Separate skills into retain and delegate lists

Decide which skills humans must preserve and which can be transferred to AI. Retention should be
based on risk, accountability, resilience, and whether the skill underpins reliable review—not on
tradition alone.

### 3. Define what competent review means

"Use judgment" is not a training specification. Experts should create rubrics, error taxonomies,
worked examples, test cases, and explicit passing thresholds. Juniors should have to explain why an
output is correct or wrong and what evidence would change the conclusion.

### 4. Use judgment practice for routine, reversible work

For many tasks, employees can learn by evaluating varied AI outputs, tracing claims to evidence,
finding omissions, and correcting reasoning. This is faster than repeating all production manually,
but only if reviewers receive substantive feedback.

### 5. Preserve manual drills for foundational or high-risk skills

For selected tasks, require the worker to complete a meaningful version independently before seeing
AI's answer. Compare both results and record each side's failure modes. Manual practice is not
nostalgia when it is needed to develop a mental model or maintain operational resilience.

### 6. Test the system, not only the final prose

One AI reviewing another can reproduce shared assumptions and blind spots. Verification may require
independent data, deterministic checks, adversarial cases, tests, source inspection, or a reviewer
using a different method—not merely another fluent model response.

### 7. Transfer work only after measured performance

A model's ability to produce an answer is not evidence of reliable delegation. Use representative
test sets and measure accuracy, consistency, severe-error rates, review time, and recovery from
failure. Thresholds should reflect the consequences of error.

### 8. Revisit the boundary without assuming one direction

Davis recommends reviewing retained and delegated skills every couple of months as models improve.
That cadence may suit fast-moving, low-risk workflows, while regulated competencies may require
slower governance. Review should also allow work to move back to humans when failures emerge.

## The unresolved constraint

The framework assumes that workers can acquire judgment without doing much of the underlying work.
That may be true for some tasks, but the video does not establish where the substitution breaks.
Domain judgment often depends on causal models, edge cases, and embodied experience developed while
performing the task.

It also assumes that productivity gains will move the same workers into more strategic roles. The
labor evidence cited in the video shows fewer junior openings and slower junior hiring. It does not
guarantee that enough strategic work exists, that juniors will receive it, or that headcount will be
preserved.

The central organizational challenge is therefore larger than prompt training. Firms need a new
apprenticeship system that preserves the experience required for future accountability while still
capturing useful automation. Without that system, AI may increase today's output while weakening
tomorrow's pool of people capable of recognizing when the output is wrong.
